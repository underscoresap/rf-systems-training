Transmission lines: waveguide, coax, connectors and the rotary joint

Between every pair of components in a radar, an ESM receiver or a jammer there is a passive path: a cable, a waveguide run, a connector pair, a rotary joint. Nobody designs these as "the product", yet they decide a large part of the system's performance. Their loss sits in front of the LNA, where it becomes noise, and behind the PA, where it eats transmitted power; in a radar the same run is used twice, so its loss is counted twice. Their mismatches combine into ripple and false echoes, their phase drifts with temperature and flexing and becomes angle error, and their junctions can create intermodulation of their own. This page builds each of those effects from first principles, with worked numbers, and traces every datasheet line from your design choice to the system effect.

Step 0

Where the transmission lines sit, and why a "piece of cable" matters

You already know

From the LNA and receiver noise module: a passive loss of L dB at room temperature has a noise figure of L dB, and in front of the LNA it adds to the system noise figure almost dB for dB. From antenna Step 2: Γ, VSWR, return loss and mismatch loss. From the power amplifier module: every dB lost after the PA is a dB of EIRP that you paid for in DC power and heat.

Definitions

Transmission line: any structure that guides an RF wave from one place to another with a defined characteristic impedance Z0: coaxial cable (coax), waveguide, stripline, microstrip. On this page "line" means the runs between boxes: from a few centimetres to tens of metres.

Insertion loss (IL): how much weaker the signal is at the output of the line than at its input, in dB. For a uniform line it grows linearly with length, so datasheets give it as attenuation in dB per metre (dB/m) at stated frequencies. Example: 0.945 dB/m × 20 m = 18.9 dB.

Connector pair (mated pair): one plug and one jack screwed together. Each mated pair adds a little loss, a small reflection and a small, not quite repeatable, phase shift.

Rotary joint: a component that passes RF from a rotating antenna to the fixed equipment below it, while it turns.

Loss counted twice: a monostatic radar transmits and receives through the same antenna, so the run between the antenna and the duplexer is crossed by the transmitted pulse going up and by the echo coming down. 3 dB in that run costs 3 dB of transmitted power and another 3 dB (or more) of received signal-to-noise ratio.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive design treats the run as "just a cable". Take an X-band (10 GHz) radar whose antenna is on a 20 m mast, with the transmitter and receiver in a shelter at its foot, and connect them with 20 m of the ordinary braided cable in the store: a 7.24 mm solid-polyethylene cable of the RG-213 class. At 10 GHz it loses 0.945 dB/m (Step 1 derives this), so the run alone loses 18.9 dB. Only 1.3 % of the transmitter's power reaches the antenna. On receive, the same 18.9 dB sits in front of the LNA: by Friis the system noise figure rises by about 18.9 dB. Counting attenuation alone on both trips, 37.8 dB, the detection range falls to 10−37.8/40 = 11 % of the lossless value. The radar has effectively been switched off by a cable.

First, loss per metre varies by more than ten times between "a cable" and "a waveguide" at the same frequency, so the choice of line is a system decision, not a purchasing one. Second, the bigger, lower-loss coax stops being usable as the frequency rises, because a large coax starts to carry a second, unwanted mode of propagation. The rest of the page explains both, then the other ways a line affects the system: ripple from mismatches (Step 3), power limits (Step 4), phase drift (Step 5), the rotary joint (Step 6), connectors (Step 7), and intermodulation and discharge inside the line (Step 8). How the choices are made, in one paragraph

(1) Frequency and bandwidth decide which kinds of line can carry the signal in one clean mode. (2) Length and the noise and power budgets decide how much loss per metre you can afford. (3) Peak and average power decide the line size and whether it must be pressurised. (4) What the system measures with phase (arrays, interferometers, monopulse) decides how phase-stable the runs must be. (5) Rotation and maintenance decide the rotary joint and the connector family. A common outcome: waveguide for the long, high-power run; phase-stable coax for short, flexible jumpers; an LNA moved to the top of the mast when the receive noise budget cannot afford the run.

Check yourself

Why is the loss of the run between antenna and duplexer "counted twice" in a radar but only once in an ESM receiver?

The radar's transmitted pulse goes up the run and its echo comes back down the same run, so both the transmitted power and the received signal-to-noise ratio pay. An ESM receiver only listens, so only the receive path pays (but there the loss also raises the noise temperature, Step 9).

20 m of cable at 0.945 dB/m: what fraction of the power arrives?

18.9 dB → 10−1.89 = 0.013, so 1.3 %.

Step 1

Coax loss per metre against frequency: conductor loss (√f) and dielectric loss (f)

You already know

Skin effect from your own layouts: at RF the current flows in a thin layer at the conductor surface, so a conductor's RF resistance is higher than its DC resistance and rises with frequency. Loss tangent tan δ from substrate datasheets: the fraction of the stored electric energy that a dielectric turns into heat each radian of the cycle.

Definitions

Coaxial line: an inner conductor of radius a inside an outer conductor of inner radius b, separated by a dielectric of relative permittivity εr. Its characteristic impedance is Z0 = (η0/2π√εr)·ln(b/a), with η0 = 376.7 Ω. For 50 Ω in solid polyethylene (εr 2.25) b/a = 3.49; in air b/a = 2.30.

Skin depth δs and surface resistance Rs: δs = 1/√(πfμ0σ) and Rs = 1/(σδs) = √(πfμ0/σ). For copper (σ = 5.8 × 107 S/m) at 1 GHz, δs = 2.1 µm and Rs = 8.25 mΩ; at 10 GHz, 0.66 µm and 26.1 mΩ. Rs grows as √f.

Conductor loss αc: the I²R heat in both conductors. Dielectric loss αd: the heat in the dielectric. The total attenuation is α = αc + αd.

Velocity factor vp: the wave speed as a fraction of the speed of light, 1/√εr: 0.67 in solid PE, 0.70 in solid PTFE, 0.88 in foam PE.

Higher-order mode (TE11): above a certain frequency a coax can also carry a wave that is not the clean TEM wave it was designed for. The first one, TE11, starts at fc ≈ c/(π(a+b)√εr). Above that frequency, every bend, connector or dent can convert power into the second mode, which travels at a different speed and returns as sharp dips and phase jumps. A coax is only usable below about 0.95 fc.

αc = 8.686 · K · Rs · (1/a + 1/b) / (4π Z0) dB/m    αd = 8.686 · π f √εr tan δ / c dB/mK is a roughness or construction factor on top of smooth copper: about 1.05 for a smooth solid outer conductor (semi-rigid), 1.2 for a corrugated one, 1.5 for a braid (the current has to follow the woven wires). αc grows as √f (through Rs) and falls as the line gets bigger (1/a + 1/b). αd grows as f and does not depend on size at all.

Worked example (the RG-213-class cable of Step 0: b = 3.62 mm, solid PE, braid, so a = 1.036 mm, K = 1.5, tan δ = 2 × 10−4). At 1 GHz: αc = 8.686 × 1.5 × 0.00825 × (965 + 276)/(4π × 50) = 0.212 dB/m; αd = 8.686 × π × 109 × 1.5 × 2 × 10−4/(3 × 108) = 0.027 dB/m; total 0.240 dB/m. At 10 GHz the conductor term grows by √10 = 3.16× to 0.672 dB/m and the dielectric term by 10× to 0.273 dB/m: total 0.945 dB/m. The conductor dominates at low frequency; the dielectric's share grows from 11 % to 29 %.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive fix for "too much loss" is "use a bigger cable": the conductor loss falls as 1/size. It works until the TE11 cut-off comes down to the operating frequency. A 22 mm (7/8-inch class) foam cable loses only 0.245 dB/m at 10 GHz on paper, but its TE11 cut-off is 5.49 GHz: at 10 GHz it is a multimode line and cannot be used. The 12 mm ½-inch foam cable has its cut-off at 10.06 GHz, right on top of the operating frequency. Size buys loss, and frequency limits size.

Why 50 Ω? For an air line of fixed outer size, conductor loss is lowest at 77 Ω (b/a = 3.6), and peak power before breakdown is highest at 30 Ω (b/a = 1.65). 50 Ω is the compromise between the two, which is why almost all RF hardware uses it. At 3 GHz in a 20 mm air line: 77 Ω loses 0.062 dB/m, 50 Ω 0.069 dB/m (11 % more), 30 Ω 0.092 dB/m.

How to choose size, dielectric and conductor

(1) The highest frequency sets the largest size: keep fmax ≤ 0.95 fc, so (a + b)√εr ≤ 0.95·c/(π fmax). At 10 GHz in foam PE that is D ≲ 11.5 mm. (2) Within that size, the loss budget (length × dB/m) picks the dielectric: foam or air-spaced for long runs, solid PTFE where flexibility, temperature range or stability matters more than loss. (3) The conductor: smooth (semi-rigid or corrugated) for low loss and good screening, braid only where it must flex. (4) If the best coax that fits still costs too many dB, use waveguide (Step 2) or move the active parts closer to the antenna (Step 9). Every 1 dB/m saved on a 20 m run is 20 dB of system budget.

Check yourself

A cable loses 0.30 dB/m at 2.5 GHz, nearly all conductor loss. Roughly what at 10 GHz?

Conductor loss scales as √f: √(10/2.5) = 2, so about 0.60 dB/m, plus a dielectric term that has grown 4× (small in this cable).

Why does doubling the cable diameter not halve the loss at 10 GHz in the foam table rows?

Only αc halves; αd (0.104 dB/m) does not depend on size. And the bigger cable's TE11 cut-off falls below 10 GHz, so it cannot be used anyway.

Step 2

Waveguide: cut-off, the dominant mode, bands, higher-order modes and loss

You already know

A coax has a centre conductor and carries a TEM wave at any frequency up to its first higher mode (Step 1). A hollow metal pipe has no centre conductor, so it cannot carry TEM at all: it carries only waves that bounce between its walls, and each such wave has a lowest frequency.

Definitions

Rectangular waveguide: a hollow metal tube of inside width a and height b (usually b ≈ a/2). Named by EIA "WR" numbers: WR-90 has a = 0.900 inch = 22.86 mm, b = 10.16 mm.

Mode TEmn: one field pattern the guide can carry, with m half-waves of field across the width and n across the height. TE means the electric field is entirely transverse (across the guide). TM modes have the magnetic field transverse.

Cut-off frequency: each mode only propagates above fc,mn = (c/2)·√((m/a)² + (n/b)²). Below it the mode dies away exponentially within a fraction of a wavelength.

Dominant mode TE10: the mode with the lowest cut-off, fc = c/(2a): half a wavelength just fits across the width. For WR-90, 6.557 GHz.

Recommended band: the frequency range where only TE10 propagates and the loss is reasonably flat, about 1.25 fc to 1.9 fc. For WR-90, 8.2 to 12.4 GHz. Below it the loss and dispersion rise steeply; above it the next modes (TE20 at c/a = 13.11 GHz, TE01 at c/(2b) = 14.75 GHz) can start.

Guide wavelength λg and group velocity vg: λg = λ0/√(1 − (fc/f)²) and vg = c·√(1 − (fc/f)²). In WR-90 at 10 GHz, λg = 39.7 mm (free-space λ0 = 30.0 mm) and vg = 0.755 c. Because vg depends on frequency, a waveguide is dispersive: different frequencies take different times to travel through it.

αc(TE10) = 8.686 · Rs / (η0 b √(1 − (fc/f)²)) · (1 + (2b/a)(fc/f)²) dB/m    group delay τ = L / vgThere is no dielectric (air), so only conductor loss. Loss rises steeply towards cut-off, has a broad minimum in the band, then rises slowly as √f. It falls with the guide size like coax, but here "bigger" also means lower cut-off, so each band has its own guide.

Worked example (WR-90 at 10 GHz, aluminium, σ = 3.5 × 107 S/m, Rs = 33.6 mΩ): (fc/f)² = 0.430, √(1 − 0.430) = 0.755, so α = 8.686 × 0.0336/(376.7 × 0.01016 × 0.755) × (1 + 2 × 0.444 × 0.430) = 0.140 dB/m. In copper, 0.108 dB/m. Compare 0.945 dB/m for the RG-213-class coax: the waveguide is 6.8 times better, because its "conductors" are large walls and there is no dielectric. The delay is 1/(0.755 c) = 4.42 ns/m. Across 9.5 to 10.5 GHz it changes from 4.61 to 4.27 ns/m, so a 20 m run delays the bottom of a 1 GHz chirp 6.8 ns more than the top.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

Naive 1: "use the bigger guide, it has lower loss". WR-137 at 10 GHz loses only 0.063 dB/m, but its TE20 cut-off is 8.60 GHz and TE01 9.49 GHz. At 10 GHz three modes can travel. Any bend, flange step or twist converts some power between them; they travel at different speeds and recombine with random phase, giving sharp transmission notches and phase jumps that move with temperature. The run becomes unrepeatable.

Naive 2: "use the guide below its band". WR-90 at 7.0 GHz is still above cut-off (6.557 GHz), so it "works". But f/fc = 1.07: vg = 0.35 c, the delay is 9.53 ns/m and changes fast with frequency, and the loss is 0.324 dB/m, 2.3 times the 10 GHz value.

How to choose the guide, the material and the run

(1) The operating band picks the guide: the whole band must sit between 1.25 fc and the next mode's cut-off. (2) The loss budget sets the material: aluminium is light and cheap, copper or silver-plated aluminium for the lowest loss. (3) For wide instantaneous bandwidth (a chirp, an ESM band) check dispersion: the delay difference across the band × run length must be small against the compressed pulse width, or be equalised (module 09). (4) Choose components (bends, twists, flexible sections) for the same band; a flexible waveguide section loses several times more than rigid guide and is used only where needed. (5) Waveguide is bulky and needs flanges and pressurisation (Step 4), so short, flexible or wideband links stay in coax.

Why is waveguide loss highest near cut-off, even though Rs is lowest there?

Near cut-off the wave bounces almost straight across the guide and advances slowly (vg → 0), so it hits the walls many more times per metre travelled.

Step 3

Cascaded VSWR: gain ripple, group-delay ripple and false echoes from multiple mismatches

You already know

From antenna Step 2: |Γ| = (VSWR − 1)/(VSWR + 1), and mismatch loss is small (VSWR 2:1 costs only 0.51 dB). From the filters module: group delay τg = −dφ/dω, and ripple in a chain's response creates spurious echoes after pulse compression.

Definitions

Re-reflection: a wave reflected at the far end of a line (ΓB, say the LNA input) travels back, is reflected again at the near end (ΓA, say the antenna port), and arrives at the far end a second time, delayed by the round trip T = 2L/(vpc) and weakened by the round-trip line loss.

Echo ratio ρ: the amplitude of that delayed copy relative to the direct wave: ρ = |ΓA| · |ΓB| · 10−2·IL/20, with IL the one-way line loss in dB. Example: VSWR 1.5 (|Γ| 0.200) and VSWR 2.0 (0.333) with 1 dB of line: ρ = 0.200 × 0.333 × 0.794 = 0.053, an echo at 20·log(0.053) = −25.5 dB.

Gain ripple: as frequency changes, the echo slides in and out of phase with the direct wave, so the transmission goes up and down: peak to peak 20·log((1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ)) dB. Example: 0.92 dB. The ripple repeats every Δf = 1/T = vpc/(2L): 39.5 MHz for 3 m at vp 0.79.

Group-delay ripple: the phase wobbles with the same period, so the delay does too: peak to peak about 2ρT. Example: T = 25.3 ns, so 2 × 0.053 × 25.3 = 2.7 ns.

Worst-case VSWR of a cascade: if the reflections line up in phase, the input VSWR approaches the product of the individual VSWRs (1.5 × 2.0 = 3.0); if they oppose, the ratio (1.33). Which you get depends on the line length, so it changes with frequency and temperature.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive approach specifies every part to "VSWR 2:1" because the mismatch loss is only 0.51 dB. Put a 2:1 antenna and a 2:1 LNA input at the two ends of 3 m of low-loss cable (1 dB, vp 0.79): ρ = 0.333 × 0.333 × 0.794 = 0.088. The gain ripples by 1.54 dB peak to peak every 39.5 MHz, the group delay by 4.5 ns, and every signal arrives with a copy of itself 25.3 ns later at −21.1 dB. After pulse compression that copy is a false target 3.8 m behind every real one (c·T/2), 21 dB down: far above a typical −35 dB range-sidelobe requirement. An ESM receiver measuring amplitude across the band sees a 1.5 dB comb on top of the antenna pattern.

The ripple is set by the product of the two end reflections, so the cheapest fix is usually to make one end very good rather than both ends moderately good. An isolator (module 06) or a balanced LNA gives VSWR 1.2 regardless of what the transistor wants. A pad works too, but in front of an LNA it costs its own value in noise figure; pads are fine on transmit drives and on test benches. Long lossy runs reduce ρ by themselves (20 m with 8 dB of loss: ρ = 0.011, ripple 0.18 dB) but the echo is then 169 ns late, so the group-delay ripple is still 3.6 ns, with a 5.9 MHz period.

How to choose the match of each part and the line lengths

(1) Start from the system limit: the allowed range sidelobe or amplitude flatness gives the largest ρ (−35 dB → ρ ≤ 0.018). (2) Split it between the two worst ends: |ΓA|·|ΓB| ≤ ρ / A². (3) Connectors and adapters in the middle make their own echoes with shorter delays; keep their count low and their VSWR ≤ 1.15. (4) The ripple period 1/T tells you what it does: a period much wider than the signal bandwidth looks like a slope (gain and delay tilt, correctable); a period narrower than the signal bandwidth produces distinct echoes (not correctable in general). (5) Do not trust the worst-case product VSWR to be rare: over a wide band and a temperature range, every phase alignment happens somewhere.

Step 4

Peak and average power handling: breakdown, heating and pressurisation

You already know

From the power amplifier module: peak power (during the pulse) and average power (peak × duty cycle) are different limits; the PA's devices fail on one or the other. A transmission line has the same two limits, with different physics.

Definitions

Duty cycle D: pulse width × pulse repetition frequency. Average power = peak × D. Example: 250 kW peak at 0.2 % duty → 500 W average.

Voltage breakdown (arcing): when the peak electric field in the gas inside a line exceeds the gas's breakdown strength, free electrons gain enough energy to ionise molecules and an arc forms, which reflects the power and pits the walls. Dry air at sea level breaks down at about 3 MV/m (30 kV/cm) in a uniform field. This sets the peak power limit.

Peak power of TE10: Pmax = Ebd²·a·b/(4η0) · √(1 − (fc/f)²). WR-90 at 10 GHz: (3 × 106)² × 0.02286 × 0.01016/(4 × 376.7) × 0.755 = 1.05 MW (theory, matched, sea level).

Standing-wave derating: with a mismatch the forward and reflected waves add to (1 + |Γ|) times the field at the standing-wave maxima, so the peak-power capability falls by (1 + |Γ|)². VSWR 1.5 (|Γ| 0.2): ÷ 1.44.

Heating: the power lost in the line, Pavg × (1 − 10−α/10) watts per metre, must leave through the outer surface. This sets the average power limit. Here: natural cooling of 10 W/m²K with a 50 °C rise allowed.

Pressurisation: filling the line with dry air or nitrogen above atmospheric pressure. The breakdown field rises roughly in proportion to gas pressure, so peak capability rises as pressure²; it also keeps moisture out (water films lower breakdown and raise loss). At altitude the opposite happens: at 10 km the pressure is 0.26 bar, and an unpressurised line holds only (0.26/1.013)² = 6.8 % of its sea-level peak power.

Ppeak,practical = Pmax · (p / 1.013 bar)² / ((1 + |Γ|)² · 2)    Pavg,max = qmax / ((1 − 10−α/10) · VSWR)The factor 2 is a safety margin for surface finish, flange joints and humidity. qmax is the heat a metre of line can shed (38 W/m for WR-90 with these cooling assumptions). Dividing average capability by VSWR is a simple derating for the hot spots at current maxima. The pressure law holds from about 0.05 bar to a few bar; at much lower pressure (high altitude, space) the breakdown changes character (Step 8, multipaction). All values illustrative.

Worked example (WR-90 aluminium at 10 GHz, 250 kW peak, 0.2 % duty, VSWR 1.5). Peak: 1.05 MW / 1.44 / 2 = 364 kW at sea level: 1.6 dB of margin over 250 kW. Average: 38.1 W/m ÷ (1 − 10−0.0140 = 0.0316) = 1.21 kW matched, ÷ 1.5 = 800 W: 2.1 dB of margin over 500 W.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive design reads "1.05 MW" from the formula and sees 6.2 dB of margin over a 250 kW transmitter. The VSWR derating and the safety factor take 4.6 dB of that. Then the same radar goes into an unpressurised aircraft bay at 10 km: (1.013/0.264)² costs another 11.7 dB, and the practical capability is 24.7 kW, 10 dB below the transmitter. The guide arcs on the first pulse at altitude; the arc reflects the full power back into the transmitter (module 06: circulator and load).

Step 5

Phase stability against temperature and flexing, and why arrays, interferometers and monopulse care

You already know

A line's insertion phase is its electrical length: φ = 360° × L·f·√εr/c. Anything that changes L (thermal expansion, stretching) or εr (temperature, density, bending) changes φ. From R2: the beam of an array is made by the relative phases of its elements.

Definitions

Electrical length: the line's insertion phase in degrees. 3 m of solid PTFE cable (εr 2.05) at 10 GHz: 360 × 3 × 1010 × 1.432/(3 × 108) = 51 580°, about 143 wavelengths. A tiny fractional change of a large number is a large phase change.

Phase change in ppm: the fractional change of electrical length, in parts per million. 100 ppm of 51 580° is 5.2°. Datasheets plot it against temperature relative to 25 °C.

The PTFE knee: solid PTFE goes through a crystal transition near 19 °C. Its density, and so its εr, changes by about 1 % over a few degrees, so the phase of a solid-PTFE cable jumps by several hundred ppm between about 15 and 25 °C. Phase-stable cables use low-density (expanded) PTFE, which has a much smaller knee, or silica (SiO2) dielectric, which has none.

Phase tracking: in a multi-channel system the absolute phase change matters less than the difference between channels. Two identical cables at the same temperature track: they change together. "Tracking mismatch" here means the fraction of the phase change that is not common to both channels (different lengths, routes in sun and shade, different batches).

Phase change on flexing: bending a cable squeezes its dielectric and moves its conductors; the phase does not quite come back when straightened. Specified as degrees at a stated frequency for a stated bend (for example ± 0.5° at 10 GHz for one 90° bend around a mandrel for a phase-stable cable, ± 3° for an ordinary one). It is random from flex to flex, so it does not track.

Interferometer direction finding: two antennas a distance d apart receive a wave from angle θ with a phase difference Δψ = 360°·(d/λ)·sin θ. A channel phase error δφ therefore reads as an angle error δθ = δφ/(360°·(d/λ)·cos θ) radians. With d = λ/2 at boresight, 5° of phase error is 1.6° of angle error.

δφT = φelec · ppm(T) · 10−6    δφchannel = √( (m · δφT)² + δφflex² )    δθ = δφchannel / (360° · d/λ) radm is the tracking mismatch (0 = perfect tracking, 1 = one cable changes and the other does not). The angle error is shown at boresight (cos θ = 1); off boresight it grows as 1/cos θ.

Worked example (two 3 m solid-PTFE cables at 10 GHz feeding a two-antenna interferometer, d/λ = 0.5, calibrated at 25 °C, now at 0 °C): the PTFE knee and the thermal slope together give +1 062 ppm, so each cable's phase moves by 51 580 × 0.001062 = 54.8°. If the two cables track to within 10 %, the channel error is 5.5°, and the DF error is 5.5/(360 × 0.5) rad = 1.74°. With phase-stable PTFE the same case moves 17.8° per cable, 1.78° between channels, 0.57° of DF error; with silica dielectric, 3.3°, 0.33° and 0.10°.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive approach calibrates the channels once, on the bench at room temperature, with ordinary solid-PTFE cables, and assumes "the cables are the same, so they track". Room temperature is about 22 °C, right on the PTFE knee: a few degrees either way moves each 3 m cable by tens of degrees. On a mast one cable runs in the sun and one in shade; the tracking mismatch is easily 30 %, so at 0 °C the channel error is 16°, and a λ/2 interferometer reads 5.2° off. For an ESM system that hands a bearing to a jammer or to another sensor, that is a miss.

(4) Flexing: put the flexing part (gimbal, cable wrap) in phase-stable cable and keep it as short as possible. (5) Calibrate in operation (injected calibration tone), and note that calibration only removes what is common and slow; flex errors are random.

Step 6

The rotary joint: wow, loss that changes with angle, and multi-channel joints

You already know

From Step 2: a waveguide carries a mode only above its cut-off, and two modes travelling together recombine with a phase that depends on how they were excited. From Step 5: monopulse reads angle from the ratio of a difference channel Δ to a sum channel Σ, so the two channels must stay matched in gain and phase.

Definitions

Rotary joint: a two-port (or multi-port) that lets one half rotate continuously relative to the other while passing RF. It needs a mode whose field pattern looks the same from every rotation angle: TEM in coax, or TM01 in circular waveguide. Rectangular TE10 is converted to that mode on each side ("door-knob" or E-plane transitions), passes through the rotating gap, and is converted back.

Contacting and non-contacting joints: the rotating gap either has sliding contacts or, more often at microwave, a choke: a quarter-wave groove that presents an RF short across the gap without touching, so there is no wear and no contact noise.

Spurious path level s: the unwanted part of the signal that does not have rotational symmetry, in dB below the wanted signal. In a TM01 joint it is the residual TE11 mode that the transitions also excite and that the mode filter does not fully remove; in a coax joint it is mainly the eccentricity of the rotating contact or choke. Its amplitude ratio is r = 10−s/20: 30 dB gives r = 0.032.

Wow: the variation of insertion loss (amplitude wow) and of insertion phase (phase wow) as the joint rotates through 360°. The spurious path adds to the wanted one with a phase that turns with the rotation angle, so the transmission is 1 + r·ej(nθ + φ): amplitude wow p-p = 20·log((1 + r)/(1 − r)) and phase wow p-p = 2·asin(r). n is 1 for the TE11 residual (once per revolution) and 2 for an eccentric contact (twice per revolution).

Multi-channel joint: several independent RF paths through one joint: for example a coax channel on the axis inside a waveguide channel around it (two channels), or Σ, ΔAz and ΔEl of a monopulse antenna plus IFF. Each channel has its own wow, and channels leak into each other: channel isolation, typically 40–60 dB.

H(θ) = 1 + r · ej(nθ + φ)    wowIL,pp = 20·log((1+r)/(1−r))    wowφ,pp = 2·asin(r)    r = 10−s/20Worked numbers: s = 20 dB → r = 0.100 → 1.74 dB and 11.5° p-p. s = 30 dB → 0.55 dB and 3.6°. s = 40 dB → 0.17 dB and 1.15°. In a radar the Σ channel's wow is crossed twice (transmit and receive), so the echo is modulated by twice the dB.

Worked example (single-channel waveguide joint at X band, mean IL 0.15 dB, TE11 suppressed by 30 dB): amplitude wow 0.55 dB p-p, phase wow 3.6° p-p. The radar's echo varies by 2 × 0.55 = 1.1 dB with antenna bearing, so the detection range at the worst bearings is 10−1.1/40 = 0.939 of that at the best: a 6 % range ripple around the horizon that has nothing to do with the targets.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive approach specifies only the mean insertion loss ("IL ≤ 0.3 dB") and the VSWR. A joint with a weak mode filter (s = 20 dB) meets both, yet its loss swings by 1.74 dB p-p as it turns: 3.5 dB of two-way echo modulation, a range ripple of 18 % with bearing. In a tracking radar with a monopulse joint, the Σ and Δ channels have different spurious paths, so their gain ratio and differential phase change with angle. With s = 20 dB in each channel, the Σ–ΔAz differential phase swings by 18.7° p-p and the ΔAz/Σ gain ratio by about ±1.4 dB, so a target 1° off boresight reads anywhere between about 0.84° and 1.16° depending on the antenna's bearing. The gain ratio does most of the damage (the phase alone would only scale the angle by cos 9.5° = 0.986). The track wobbles once per revolution.

Step 7

Connector families: frequency limits, repeatability and torque

You already know

From Step 1: a coax supports only TEM below its TE11 cut-off, fc ≈ 2c/(π(D + d)√εr) with D and d the outer and inner diameters. A connector is a short piece of coax with a joint in the middle, so the same limit applies, set by its own diameters.

Definitions

Connector family: a standardised interface (Type N, TNC, SMA, 3.5 mm, 2.92 mm, 2.4 mm, 1.85 mm, 7-16 DIN, 4.3-10 and others). The "mm" families are named after the inside diameter of the outer conductor. Smaller connectors reach higher frequencies but handle less power and are more delicate.

Rated frequency: the highest frequency at which the family is specified, set below its TE11 cut-off. 3.5 mm (air, D 3.5 mm, d 1.52 mm): fc = 2 × 3 × 108/(π × 5.02 mm) = 38 GHz, rated to 26.5 GHz. SMA (PTFE-filled): fc ≈ 24.4 GHz, rated to 18 GHz.

Mated-pair VSWR: the reflection of one plug–jack pair, mostly from the small changes of diameter at the interface and the support bead. Rises with frequency.

Repeatability: how much the insertion loss and phase change when the same pair is disconnected and reconnected. Example: SMA about 0.05 dB and 1.5° at 18 GHz; 3.5 mm about 0.01 dB and 0.3° at 26.5 GHz.

Coupling torque: the specified tightening torque of the coupling nut, applied with a calibrated torque spanner: SMA 0.56 N·m (5 in-lb) brass, 0.9 N·m stainless; 3.5/2.92/2.4/1.85 mm 0.9 N·m (8 in-lb); N 1.36 N·m (12 in-lb); 7-16 DIN 25–30 N·m. Under-torque leaves a gap at the reference plane (higher VSWR, poor repeatability, PIM in Step 8); over-torque deforms the interface and damages every later mate.

Intermateability: SMA, 3.5 mm and 2.92 mm mate with each other mechanically (with some loss of performance); 2.4 mm and 1.85 mm mate with each other but deliberately not with SMA. A 75 Ω N and a 50 Ω N look alike but have different pin sizes and damage each other.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

Naive 1: "SMA is the standard, use it everywhere." A K-band ESM receiver covering 18–26.5 GHz built with SMA: above about 24 GHz the SMA's PTFE section can carry TE11, and the harness shows sharp, temperature-sensitive notches (in the model the pair's VSWR jumps above 4 at 26 GHz). Naive 2: finger-tight. The same 6 SMA pairs at 18 GHz finger-tight: each pair's reflection about 2.5 times larger and repeatability about 4 times worse, so the phase moves 15° between installations. A calibrated DF array or a phase-matched set is no longer matched.

How to choose the family, the count and the procedure

(2) Power and PIM may override it (7-16 DIN or 4.3-10 for high-power, low-PIM paths). (3) Count the mated pairs in each path and budget their loss, reflection (√N typical, N worst) and repeatability; remove adapters and bulkheads where you can. (4) Phase-critical paths get precision families and are never disconnected after calibration, or are recalibrated each time. (5) Write the torque and the gauging (pin depth) checks into the assembly and maintenance procedures; a torque spanner costs less than one failed calibration.

Step 8

Passive intermodulation (PIM) and multipaction: nonlinearity and discharge in "linear" parts

You already know

From the LNA and mixer modules: two tones f1, f2 through a nonlinearity make third-order products at 2f1 − f2 and 2f2 − f1, rising 3 dB for each 1 dB of input. Passive parts are supposed to be linear; this step is about when they are not.

Definitions

Passive intermodulation (PIM): intermodulation produced by passive parts: connectors, cables, joints, antennas. Sources are metal-to-metal contacts with thin oxide layers (tunnelling junctions), ferromagnetic materials (nickel plating, steel, some stainless steels), loose or under-torqued joints, corrosion and metal particles. It is extremely weak, but it is generated after all the filters, at full transmit power, in a path shared with the receiver.

PIM specification: the third-order product level with two carriers of +43 dBm (20 W) each, in dBc below one carrier. −150 dBc means the product is at 43 − 150 = −107 dBm.

Order and slope: products of order 2k+1 fall at f1 − k(f2 − f1) and f2 + k(f2 − f1). The third order rises 3 dB per dB of carrier power in theory; measured PIM often rises 2 to 3 dB/dB. Higher orders are weaker, here about 10 dB per step of order.

Receiver noise floor in a bandwidth B: −174 dBm/Hz + NF + 10·log B. With NF 3 dB and B = 1 MHz: −111 dBm.

Multipaction: a resonant electron avalanche in vacuum (space, or very high altitude). A free electron in an RF gap is accelerated across it; if it arrives at the far wall after an odd number of half RF periods, with enough energy that each impact releases more than one secondary electron, the number of electrons doubles every crossing. The discharge detunes filters, raises noise and loss, and can damage surfaces. Inside a gas at low pressure the related failure is corona or ionisation breakdown, worst at pressures of the order of 100 Pa (roughly 40–50 km altitude for centimetre gaps, a range crossed during launch).

PIM(2k+1) = 43 + PIMspec + slope · (Pcarrier − 43) − 10·(k − 1) dBm    fIM = f1 − k·Δ, f2 + k·ΔΔ = f2 − f1. Multipaction: the first-order resonance needs a crossing in half a period, so the electron's impact velocity is about 2·f·d and its energy grows as (f·d)². At f·d = 10 GHz·mm it is of the order of 1 keV; at f·d = 1 GHz·mm only about 10 eV, below the 20–50 eV needed for a metal surface to emit more than one secondary electron, so very small f·d gaps cannot multipact in first order. (Illustrative; space practice uses measured secondary-emission data and margins of about 6 dB.)

Worked example (a shared antenna feed carrying two transmit carriers at 1 800 and 1 840 MHz, +43 dBm each, with a receive band at 1 700–1 780 MHz; NF 3 dB, 1 MHz channels): IM3 at 2 × 1 800 − 1 840 = 1 760 MHz, inside the receive band. A good silver-plated connector chain at −150 dBc puts it at −107 dBm, 4 dB above the −111 dBm noise floor: if the carriers are modulated, the product is spread like noise and desensitises that channel by 10·log(1 + 100.4) = 5.5 dB. IM5 at 1 720 MHz is at −117 dBm, 6 dB below the floor.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive design assumes passive parts are linear and filters the transmitter's own intermodulation at the PA output. Then a nickel-plated adapter, a steel bracket in the near field or one under-torqued connector in the shared path gives −110 dBc: IM3 at −67 dBm, 44 dB above the noise floor, and IM5 at −77 dBm. An ESM receiver reports strong false emitters at 1 760 and 1 720 MHz whenever the co-sited transmitter keys; a radar sees a raised noise floor in those channels. No filter can help, because the products are created after the last filter, inside the receive band.

How to choose materials, connectors and the frequency plan

(1) Frequency plan first: compute where the products of every transmit carrier pair fall and keep the low orders out of the receive bands (module 05 does the same for mixer spurs). (2) Materials: no ferromagnetic metals in the RF path or the antenna near field (avoid nickel underplate and steel hardware); silver or white-bronze plating. (3) Junctions: fewer interfaces, large contact pressure (low-PIM families need 25–30 N·m for a reason), clean and dry mating faces, no metal swarf. (4) Specify PIM with the test level and slope, and test the assembled path, not only the parts. (5) For space hardware, design gaps and power levels with multipaction margin (about 6 dB), vent or fill cavities, and avoid the critical pressure range for gas discharge during launch.

Step 9

System effects: radar range (loss counted twice), G/T and noise, EIRP, and DF error

You already know

From antenna Step 8: Tsys = TA + (L − 1)·290 + L·TLNA for a loss L before the LNA, referred to the antenna port, and G/T = G − 10·log Tsys. From R1: the radar equation, R4 ∝ PtG²λ²σ/(kTsysB·L).

Definitions

Transmit loss Lt: all the loss between the PA and the antenna port: line, rotary joint, connectors. EIRP = Pt − Lt + G (in dB units). Example: 1 kW (60 dBm), 33 dBi, Lt 3.39 dB → 89.6 dBm.

Receive loss and noise: the same run, crossed by the echo, both attenuates it and adds noise (Step 0). Referred to the antenna port, Tsys = TA + (Lr − 1)·290 + Lr·(TLNA + T2/GLNA). Here TA = 100 K, LNA NF 1.5 dB (TLNA = 120 K), gain 30 dB, the stage after it NF 6 dB (865 K): lossless Tsys = 220.5 K.

Range factor: the detection range relative to a lossless installation, (Tsys,0/(Lt·Tsys))1/4. The transmit loss and the receive noise each enter once: that is "counted twice".

Mast-top LNA: an LNA placed at the antenna (above the rotary joint), behind only about 0.3 dB of connectors and protection. The long run then comes after 30 dB of LNA gain and hardly adds noise. Transmit power still has to go up the run, unless the PA moves too (an active array, module 10).

ESM sensitivity change: a receive-only system pays only the Tsys increase: 10·log(Tsys/Tsys,0) dB.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive budget counts the run's loss once, as "3.4 dB, so 18 % of range" (10−3.39/40 = 0.82), and forgets both that the echo crosses it again and that loss before the LNA adds noise at 290 K. The real range factor is 0.615: a 38 % loss, not 18 %. With the RG-213-class cable of Step 0 the same arithmetic gives 0.091, and the noise temperature 36 000 K.

How to choose the architecture

(1) Write the budget both ways: transmit loss to EIRP, receive loss to Tsys, and their product to range. (2) If the receive budget fails, move the LNA towards the antenna first: it is cheap and fixes Tsys almost completely. (3) If the transmit budget fails, use lower-loss line (waveguide, larger size, pressurised) or move the PA too (solid-state transmitters at the antenna, active arrays). (4) Budget the phase stability, ripple, connector repeatability and PIM of the runs in the same document as their loss; they are the same parts. (5) Repeat at the top of the band and the hottest temperature, where every loss is largest.
