Power, grounding, spurs and EMC: how the supply becomes a signal

Every RF part on a board runs from a DC rail, returns its current through a ground, and sits in a metal box next to other parts and other antennas. None of these is ideal. The rail carries ripple and noise, the ground has inductance, the box has slots and resonances, and the platform carries its own transmitters. Each imperfection reaches the RF path through a component property you already know from datasheets: PSRR, VCO pushing, gain and phase sensitivity to drain voltage, isolation. This page follows each one from the rail to the radar or ESM display, where it turns up as a spur, extra phase noise, a pulse-to-pulse error, a false target or a false emitter. Every term is defined before it is used, every step has worked numbers, and every control in a demo is explained before you can move it.

Step 0

Where power, ground and shielding enter an RF system

You already know

A datasheet gives a supply voltage range, a supply current and sometimes a PSRR or "supply sensitivity" line. You decouple every pin with a capacitor and connect the ground pads to a plane through vias. You have seen a spur on a spectrum analyser that moved when you touched a cable or swapped a bench supply.

Definitions

Rail: one regulated DC supply net, for example "+5 V VCO". Bus: the raw platform supply that feeds the regulators, here 28 V.

Ripple: the periodic part of the rail voltage, at the switching frequency of a regulator and its harmonics, given in mV peak-to-peak (mV p-p). Noise: the random part, given as a density in nV/√Hz or as µV rms over a band.

Spur (spurious signal): a discrete, unwanted spectral line. It is given in dBc (dB relative to the carrier it sits beside) or in dBm (absolute power). Sideband: a spur pair at carrier ± modulating frequency, produced when something modulates the carrier.

Coupling path: the route by which an unwanted voltage or current gets from its source to the RF signal: conducted (along a wire or rail), common impedance (through a shared ground), near-field (magnetic or electric coupling between nearby loops or nodes) or radiated (as a wave, through apertures or between antennas).

EMC (electromagnetic compatibility): the property that a unit neither disturbs other units (emissions) nor is disturbed by them (susceptibility). Co-site interference: the EMC problem between transmitters and receivers on the same platform.

An RF engineer usually treats the supply as "DC" and the ground as "zero volts". At system level both are signals. A rail with 20 mV of ripple at 500 kHz is a 500 kHz signal applied to every pin it feeds. A ground connection with 20 nH of inductance turns a 1 A current step into a 0.4 V spike. The RF part does not know that these voltages are not wanted: it converts them into amplitude modulation, phase modulation, frequency modulation or an extra input signal, exactly as its datasheet sensitivities say.

The PA hangs on a 1 000 µF bank behind a slow charger, its return current shares 20 mm of ground track with the ADC, and the box is one open cavity with a 30 mm lid gap. Step 1

Supply ripple and noise, and how they reach the RF path: PSRR

You already know

An amplifier's gain and phase change a little when you change its drain or collector voltage: you see it when you sweep VDD on a network analyser. An LDO datasheet has a PSRR plot that is high at low frequency and falls off above some tens of kHz.

Definitions

PSRR (power-supply rejection ratio): how much a circuit attenuates a disturbance on its input supply before it appears at its output. For a regulator, PSRR = 20·log10(Vripple,in / Vripple,out) dB. It is a function of frequency, not one number.

Gain sensitivity kG: change of an amplifier's gain per volt of supply, in dB/V. Phase sensitivity kφ: change of its insertion phase per volt, in °/V. Typical small-signal values: 0.2 to 1 dB/V and 2 to 10 °/V.

AM index m: the peak fractional change of carrier amplitude. PM index β: the peak phase change in radians. Each produces a sideband pair at ± the ripple frequency.

Error-amplifier bandwidth: an LDO regulates by comparing its output with a reference in a feedback loop. Above that loop's bandwidth the loop cannot correct, PSRR falls, and only the output capacitor helps.

A ripple of peak amplitude a (half the p-p value) on an amplifier's supply changes its gain by kG·a dB and its phase by kφ·a degrees, at the ripple frequency. A small amplitude change of x dB is a fractional change of x/8.686 in voltage, so the AM index is m = kG·a/8.686. The phase change in radians is β = kφ·a·π/180. AM sidebands sit in phase with the carrier and PM sidebands in quadrature, so their powers add:

sideband (each side) = 20·log10( ½·√(m² + β²) ) dBc    m = kG·a/8.686    β = kφ·a·π/180    aout = ain·10−PSRR/20a is the peak ripple on the pin, in volts. The sideband is the same in dBc for every signal passing through the stage: a strong signal and a weak one both get copies at ± the ripple frequency, at the same relative level.

Worked number: an LNA with kG = 0.5 dB/V and kφ = 5 °/V on a rail with 20 mV p-p at 500 kHz (a = 10 mV). m = 0.5 × 0.01/8.686 = 5.8×10−4 (AM sideband −70.8 dBc), β = 5 × 0.01 × π/180 = 8.7×10−4 rad (PM sideband −67.2 dBc), together −65.6 dBc on each side.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive approach connects the LNA directly to the buck output because "it is only an LNA, it has no oscillator". −65.6 dBc looks small. But it is applied to every signal, including a strong clutter return or a nearby emitter. A clutter return 60 dB above noise gets sidebands at −5.6 dB relative to noise, 500 kHz away, which in a pulse-Doppler or a channelised ESM receiver is a new signal in a different bin (Step 10). The PSRR of a wire is 0 dB.

This page uses 80 dB as the practical layout limit.

How to choose the regulator and the part

(1) Start from the system: which sideband level is allowed? For a radar with 60 dB clutter-to-noise, sidebands must be about −75 dBc or lower to stay 15 dB under noise. (2) From kG and kφ, find the largest allowed ripple on the pin: here −75 dBc needs a ≤ 3.4 mV peak. (3) Divide the regulator ripple by that to get the PSRR needed at the switching frequency and its harmonics, read off the PSRR curve, not the DC number: 20 mV p-p down to 6.8 mV p-p is 9.4 dB, so even the basic LDO passes here, with 10.7 dB to spare. (4) If you design the amplifier, a lower kφ (a bias point where phase varies less with VDD, an on-chip regulator, a cascode) relaxes everything upstream by the same number of dB.

Step 2

VCO pushing: ripple becomes FM sidebands, and supply noise becomes phase noise

You already know

A VCO has a tuning sensitivity KV in MHz/V on its tune pin. Its datasheet also lists "pushing" in MHz/V: how far the frequency moves when the supply changes. You know from the synthesiser module that a PLL corrects slow VCO errors inside its loop bandwidth and cannot correct fast ones.

Definitions

Pushing KP: change of VCO frequency per volt of supply, in MHz/V. The supply pin acts as a second, unwanted tuning port. Typical values: 0.5 to 5 MHz/V for microwave VCOs, below 0.1 MHz/V for well-regulated integrated ones.

Frequency deviation Δf: the peak frequency change, Δf = KP·a. FM index β: Δf/fm, where fm is the ripple frequency. For β ≪ 1 each sideband is β/2.

Phase noise L(f): noise power in 1 Hz at offset f from the carrier, relative to the carrier, in dBc/Hz (defined in the synthesiser module). Supply noise density en: the regulator's output noise in V/√Hz.

Loop bandwidth fL: the offset below which the PLL removes VCO frequency errors. Inside it a pushing error is reduced by about 40 dB per decade; outside it passes untouched.

Pushing is the most powerful way a supply gets into an RF system, because a VCO integrates: a frequency error becomes a phase error that grows for as long as the error lasts. A slow ripple therefore produces a large phase swing. The FM index divides by the ripple frequency, so the same ripple at a lower frequency gives larger sidebands. Random supply noise works the same way: each 1 Hz slice of noise at offset f produces phase noise at that offset.

sideband = 20·log10( KP·a / (2·fm) ) dBc    Lsupply(f) = 20·log10( KP·en(f) / (√2·f) ) dBc/HzKP in Hz/V, a in volts peak, en in V/√Hz, fm and f in Hz. Both terms are then reduced by the PLL inside its loop bandwidth. A multiplier after the VCO raises both by 20·log10(N), as explained in the synthesiser module.

Worked number: KP = 2 MHz/V, 20 mV p-p (a = 10 mV) at 500 kHz. Δf = 2×106 × 0.01 = 20 kHz peak, β = 20 kHz/500 kHz = 0.04, sideband 20·log(0.02) = −34.0 dBc. The same ripple on the LNA in Step 1 gave −65.6 dBc: the VCO is 31.6 dB more sensitive to the same rail.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive design runs the VCO from the switching regulator, trusting the PLL to "clean it up". At 500 kHz the PLL (loop bandwidth 100 kHz) is five times too slow to help, so the −34 dBc spurs appear on the LO and are copied onto every received signal by the mixer: a target return gets two ghost copies 500 kHz away only 34 dB below it, and a strong clutter return produces ghosts far above noise. The noise is as bad: a buck output with 500 nV/√Hz gives L = −83.0 dBc/Hz at 10 kHz, 4 dB worse than the free-running VCO's own −87 dBc/Hz there; inside the loop the PLL fixes that, but at 100 kHz the supply term (−103.0 dBc/Hz) is 7 dB above the VCO (−110 dBc/Hz), and there the PLL can no longer help.

How to choose the VCO supply

(1) Take the spur limit for the LO, for example −80 dBc. (2) The allowed ripple on the VCO pin is a ≤ 2·fm·10spur/20/KP: at 500 kHz and 2 MHz/V that is 50 µV peak. (3) The PSRR needed is 20·log(10 mV / 50 µV) = 46 dB at 500 kHz, which only the low-noise LDO meets. (4) For noise, require Lsupply to be at least 6 dB below the VCO's own L(f) at every offset outside the loop bandwidth; that sets the en needed: at 100 kHz, −116 dBc/Hz needs en ≤ 110 nV/√Hz. (5) A VCO with lower KP relaxes both: every halving gives 6 dB. (6) A wider loop bandwidth suppresses more of both terms, but only if the reference and PLL floor allow it (synthesiser module).

Step 3

Switching-regulator spurs: frequency, harmonics, and how they land on carriers and in the IF

You already know

A buck regulator chops its input with a switch at a fixed frequency fsw and smooths the result with an inductor and capacitor. The output ripple you measured in Steps 1 and 2 is the smoothed part. You know that a square wave contains odd and even harmonics, and that faster edges contain more high-frequency energy.

Definitions

Switch node: the junction between the switch and the inductor. Its voltage jumps between 0 and Vin every cycle: a trapezoid of amplitude Vin, duty D = Vout/Vin and edge (rise) time tr. It is the strongest RF source on most boards.

Harmonic n: the spectral line at n·fsw. Its amplitude is set by the trapezoid: flat up to about 1/(πDT), falling 20 dB per decade up to 1/(π tr), then 40 dB per decade.

Coupling isolation: the loss, in dB, between the switch node and the victim input (here the receiver IF input) through all paths: near-field, ground and radiation. 80 to 120 dB is typical for a regulator on the same board.

Spread-spectrum switching: the regulator's clock is swept by ±δ (for example ±5 %) around fsw. Each harmonic is smeared over a width 2δ·n·fsw. Synchronised switching: fsw is locked to a system clock so that its harmonics fall on known, harmless frequencies.

RBW (resolution bandwidth): the bandwidth of the receiver's narrowest filter or FFT bin. Noise power in it is −174 dBm/Hz + NF + 10·log10(RBW).

A switching regulator makes two kinds of trouble. The first is the ripple on its output (Steps 1 and 2): a low-frequency signal that modulates carriers. The second is the switch-node edge itself: a comb of harmonics every fsw that reaches tens or hundreds of MHz. Those harmonics do not need a carrier to modulate. Wherever they fall inside a receiver band, an IF, an ADC's first Nyquist zone or a reference input, they are signals. A harmonic that couples onto the LO path also mixes with the LO and appears at LO ± n·fsw.

The worked example is a 12 V to 3.3 V buck at 500 kHz (D = 0.275) with 10 ns edges, 100 dB from a receiver whose IF is 60 to 80 MHz, NF 5 dB, RBW 10 kHz. The IF noise in one RBW is −174 + 5 + 40 = −129 dBm.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive approach picks a regulator for efficiency and size, with the fastest edges (lowest switching loss) and no thought for where its harmonics go. In the example, 23 of the 41 harmonics between 60 and 80 MHz sit above the IF noise; the worst, at 61 MHz, is −120.4 dBm, 8.6 dB above noise. Each one is a CW line in the IF that the detector and the display will treat as a signal (Step 10). Moving the regulator to 1 MHz "to make it smaller" makes it worse: 15.2 dB above noise, because each harmonic carries more energy when there are fewer of them.

Spread spectrum does not remove energy, it redistributes it: a CW line becomes a band of noise, which is excellent for a narrow-RBW receiver (gain up to 10·log(500 kHz/10 kHz) = 17 dB) and worth much less for a wide one: with RBW 100 kHz the gain is limited to 10·log(500/100) = 7 dB, and the worst level improves only from 1.4 dB to 10.7 dB below the (10 dB higher) noise. Synchronising keeps the lines but puts them where the processing can ignore them: between channels, outside the IF, or locked to the radar PRF so that they land at zero Doppler (Step 10).

How to choose fsw, edge rate and modulation

(1) Write the frequency plan first: list every IF band, ADC Nyquist zone and reference frequency, and compute which harmonics n·fsw fall in each. (2) Choose fsw so that no low harmonic (n below about 20) falls in any of them, and so that fsw is well above the PLL loop bandwidth (Step 2). (3) Estimate the worst harmonic in each band with the equation and the isolation you expect; require it to be at least 6 dB below noise in the narrowest RBW. (4) If it fails, slow the edges first (simple, costs efficiency), then add isolation (layout, can, filter), then choose between spread spectrum (narrow-RBW receivers, no coherent processing) and synchronisation (coherent radar, where a stable known line is better than a moving one). (5) Never let two regulators free-run at nearly the same frequency: their beat note (the difference) is a slow ripple that the PLL cannot remove.

Step 4

LDO post-regulation and LC or ferrite filtering, and their trade-offs

You already know

An LC low-pass section attenuates 40 dB per decade above its corner f0 = 1/(2π√(LC)). A ferrite bead is sold by its impedance at 100 MHz, for example "600 Ω". Real capacitors have ESR and ESL, and become inductive above their self-resonance.

Definitions

Post-regulation: putting a second, quiet regulator (normally an LDO) after an efficient but noisy switching regulator. Dropout: the smallest input-to-output voltage at which the LDO still regulates; headroom is the margin you give it above dropout. The LDO burns (Vin − Vout)·I as heat.

LC filter resonance: at f0 the filter has gain, not loss, of about Q = √(L/C)/R where R is the total series resistance. With ceramic capacitors (ESR a few mΩ) this peak can be +10 to +20 dB.

Damping: a resistor Rd in series with a larger capacitor (here 4·C) placed across the filter capacitor; it lowers the resonance peak without adding DC loss. The best Rd is close to √(L/C).

Ferrite bead: an inductor whose core loss makes it resistive at high frequency. At low frequency it is a small inductor (about 1.5 µH here), and its inductance falls as DC current saturates the ferrite. ESL: the series inductance of a capacitor, about 1 nH for a ceramic part with its vias; it sets a floor on attenuation at high frequency.

The VCO rail of Step 2 is fed from a buck at 1 MHz with 30 mV p-p ripple. It needs 5 V at 150 mA, and the VCO pushing is 2 MHz/V, so without filtering the spur is 20·log(2×106 × 0.015/(2 × 106)) = −36.5 dBc. Four tools are available, and each has a different frequency behaviour.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive fix is "put a ferrite bead in": a 600 Ω bead and a 10 µF ceramic capacitor. It does attenuate 1 MHz by 56.8 dB at 150 mA. But at 45 kHz the bead's inductance and the low-ESR capacitor resonate with a peak of +15.4 dB: any disturbance near 45 kHz, such as a load that switches at a PRF of a few tens of kHz, is amplified six times. And the bead is a current-dependent part: at 1 A its inductance has dropped so far that the 1 MHz attenuation is only 37.3 dB. The naive filter is tested at no load and specified at 600 Ω at 100 MHz, a frequency where the problem is not.

(3) Check that f0 does not sit on a known disturbance (PRF, a fan, another regulator) and damp it with Rd ≈ √(L/C) = √(1 µH/22 µF) = 0.21 Ω. (4) Rate the inductor or bead for the full DC current, and look at its inductance at that current. (5) Put the LDO last, as close to the pin as possible, with at least its datasheet headroom: PSRR collapses near dropout. Raising L or C lowers f0 and improves attenuation at fsw, at the cost of size, DC drop and a lower, harder-to-damp resonance.

Step 5

Pulsed loads: PA drain droop, hold-up capacitance, and the pulse-to-pulse effect on MTI and Doppler

You already know

A pulsed PA draws a large current only during the pulse: 10 A at 50 V for 100 µs, then nearly nothing. The power amplifier module shows how its gain and phase change with drain voltage. You know Q = C·V, so a capacitor that delivers charge loses voltage.

Definitions

Hold-up (reservoir) capacitor C: the capacitor bank next to the PA that supplies the pulse current, so the power supply only needs to deliver the average current. Droop: the drain-voltage fall during one pulse, ΔV = I·τ/C. ESR step: the immediate drop I·ESR at the start of the pulse.

Recharge time constant τr: how fast the supply restores the bank between pulses (set by its loop bandwidth and current limit). PRI = 1/PRF, the time between pulses. Stagger: changing the PRI from pulse to pulse, used to remove blind speeds.

Intrapulse error: amplitude and phase that change within one pulse. Pulse-to-pulse error: differences between successive pulses. MTI improvement limit I: the best clutter cancellation a moving-target indicator can reach with a given pulse-to-pulse phase error σφ (radians, rms): I ≈ −20·log10(σφ) dB.

A radar does not care much about an error that is the same on every pulse: a constant phase offset is invisible to MTI and Doppler processing, because they compare pulses with each other. What matters is the difference between pulses. Clutter (ground, sea, rain) is a huge return that is the same on every pulse; MTI and Doppler filters remove it by subtracting pulses. Any pulse-to-pulse change in the transmitter makes the clutter look as if it moved, and that residue is not removed. With phase sensitivity kφ = 2 °/V, a 50 mV pulse-to-pulse change in drain voltage gives 0.1° of phase error, and I ≈ −20·log(0.1·π/180) = 55 dB.

With a constant PRI the bank settles into the same voltage at the start of every pulse, and all pulses are alike. With stagger, a pulse after a short PRI starts from a lower voltage than one after a long PRI, because the bank has had less time to recharge. That is how a "DC" design choice, the size of a capacitor and the speed of a charger, becomes a radar clutter-cancellation limit.

Worked number: 10 A for 100 µs from 1 000 µF is ΔV = 10 × 100×10−6/10−3 = 1.0 V (2 % of 50 V), plus 0.1 V ESR step (10 mΩ). At 2 °/V and 0.2 dB/V the pulse has a 2.0° phase ramp and droops 0.22 dB in amplitude. The energy per pulse is 50 × 10 × 100×10−6 = 0.05 J out of 1.25 J stored. With a 1 ms charger and a slow supply, the bank sags by 2.0 V on average before it reaches balance.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive design sizes the bank for droop alone ("2 % is fine for a PA") and tests it at a constant PRF. Every pulse looks the same and the MTI limit is 81 dB. The radar then turns on ±10 % stagger: the pulse-to-pulse voltage changes by 50 mV rms, the phase by 0.10°, and the MTI limit falls to 53.5 dB. Against 60 dB ground clutter, the residue is 6.5 dB above noise at every range cell with clutter, which shows up as false targets near hills and buildings or a loss of sensitivity there.

(5) Separately, check the droop against the intrapulse limit from the pulse-compression sidelobe budget. (6) Alternatives that change the problem: regulate the drain voltage locally after the bank (a "pulse regulator"), or correct the known droop digitally with pre-distortion.

Step 6

PA modulators: gate pulsing and drain pulsing

You already know

A GaN or GaAs FET can be turned off two ways: by taking the gate below pinch-off, or by removing the drain voltage. You have seen ringing on a scope when a fast current step flows through a long wire: V = L·di/dt.

Definitions

Modulator: the circuit that turns the PA on and off for each pulse. Gate pulsing: the drain stays at full voltage; a small, fast switch moves the gate between pinch-off and bias. Drain pulsing: a high-current switch (a series FET) connects the drain supply only during the pulse. Drain + gate: the drain is switched first, the gate later, once the drain has settled.

Interconnect inductance L: the inductance of the path from the bank (Step 5) to the drain pin: about 1 nH per mm of track or wire. Drain-node capacitance C: the local decoupling right at the PA. Together they ring at fring = 1/(2π√(LC)).

Snubber: Rd in series with a capacitor 4·C, across the drain node; it absorbs the ringing. Settling time: the time until the drain stays within 0.1 V of its final value. Quiescent current IDQ: the bias current with no RF drive.

The modulator decides three things: how fast the pulse can start, what transient the drain voltage has at the start of the pulse (which becomes a phase transient at kφ °/V, as in Step 5), and how much noise and power the PA produces when it should be off. The last one is easy to forget. A PA that stays biased between pulses keeps amplifying its own input noise, and that noise leaks into the receiver through the circulator or T/R switch during the listening time.

Worked number for the off-state: a PA with 30 dB gain and 6 dB noise figure, biased on, puts −174 + 6 + 30 = −138 dBm/Hz of noise on its output. Through 20 dB of circulator isolation, −158 dBm/Hz reaches the receiver, whose own noise (NF 2 dB) is −172 dBm/Hz. The receiver noise rises by 10·log(1 + 101.4) = 14.2 dB. Pinch the gate off and the PA output noise falls to the thermal floor: no rise.

ΔVstep ≈ I·√(L/C)    fring = 1/(2π√(LC))    best Rd ≈ √(L/C)    phase transient = kφ·ΔVpeakThe first expression is the peak dip for a current step much faster than the ringing period. A slower edge (rise time comparable to 1/fring) excites less ringing. A drain switch that rises fast into an undamped LC overshoots up to twice the step.

Worked number: 10 A drawn in 20 ns through 20 nH into a 100 nF drain node. √(L/C) = 0.45 Ω, so the dip is about 4.5 V (the demo gives 4.36 V), ringing at 3.56 MHz. At 2 °/V the pulse starts with a phase transient of 8.7°.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive choice is gate pulsing with the bank on the power board and a 20 mm path to the PA: it is simple, fast and needs no high-current switch. The drain node rings by 4.36 V and is still outside ±0.1 V after 10 µs, which is a tenth of the pulse: the first part of every pulse has a phase wobble of several degrees. The opposite naive choice, a fast drain switch (200 ns) on the same LC, overshoots by 14.8 V, which can exceed the device's drain rating, and takes 4.2 µs to settle.

(6) Start RF only after the settling time; that time is lost duty cycle and, in a monostatic radar, a slightly longer blind range.

Step 7

Grounding: star versus plane, return currents and ground loops

You already know

A via has about 0.5 nH, a millimetre of track about 1 nH. At RF you always put a solid plane under a microstrip line, because the line's characteristic impedance depends on it. You have seen a "ground bounce" spike on a digital scope trace.

Definitions

Return current: every current that leaves a source on a track comes back to it. "Ground" is just the conductor it comes back on, and it has resistance and inductance like any other.

Path of least impedance: at DC the return spreads to the lowest-resistance path; above about 100 kHz on a board it follows the lowest-inductance path, which is the plane area directly under the outgoing track, because that minimises the loop area.

Common-impedance coupling: when two circuits share a piece of return path with impedance Z, the noisy circuit's current I puts a voltage I·Z on the quiet circuit's reference: V = L·di/dt + R·I.

Star ground: each circuit has its own return wire to one point. Ground plane: one continuous copper layer used by all circuits. Slot: a cut in the plane (a split, a row of clearances); a return current must go around it.

Ground loop: a closed loop formed when two units are grounded at two places and also joined by a cable shield. A potential difference between the two grounds, or a magnetic field through the loop, drives current along the shield and adds a voltage to the signal.

A ground is a component. On a mixed board the noisy currents are the PA drain return (amperes, pulsed), the regulator switch currents (Step 3) and digital logic; the quiet references are the ADC input, the LNA bias and the VCO tuning voltage. Whether they share impedance is decided by the layout, not the schematic: the schematic draws one ground symbol for all of them.

Vvictim = Lshared·dI/dt + Rshared·I    track ≈ 1 nH/mm, 0.5 mΩ/mm (1 mm wide, 35 µm copper)    XL = 2π·f·LExample: a 1 A current with 50 ns edges. Through 20 mm of shared track (20 nH, 10 mΩ) the spike is 20×10−9 × 1/50×10−9 = 0.40 V, and the flat part 10 mV. On an ADC with 1 V peak full scale, the spike is −8 dBFS.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

There are two opposite naive approaches. The first is a daisy chain: one ground track running from the PA past the ADC to the connector, so the ADC reference sees the PA's return: −8 dBFS spikes at every pulse edge, repeated at the PRF, which become a comb of spurs in the ADC output. The second is the "audio" fix, a star ground: every circuit gets its own wire to one point. That removes the shared impedance (0.5 nH left at the star point, −40 dBFS) but gives each RF part a 20 mm, 20 nH ground lead, which is 377 Ω at 3 GHz: the RF stage no longer has a ground at all and may oscillate. A star is a low-frequency idea.

The usual "split analog and digital ground" advice creates exactly this when a track crosses the split. Keep one plane, and separate circuits by placement (noisy parts and their return paths in one region, quiet ones in another) rather than by cuts.

Ground loops are the system version. Two units, each bonded to the airframe, joined by a coax: 1 A of 400 Hz power-return current flowing along the airframe divides into the shield. With 10 mΩ of shield resistance, 10 mV of 400 Hz appears in series with the signal, which is a hum spur at 400 Hz on every video or baseband line using that cable. The fixes are a balanced or differential interface, a single-point bond at low frequency (a capacitor to chassis at the far end keeps the RF bond), or a fibre link.

How to choose the grounding

(1) Use one continuous plane under all RF and mixed-signal parts; put no cuts under any track. (2) Place parts so that each high-current loop (regulator switch, PA drain, decoupling of fast logic) is small and away from the quiet references; the plane then keeps them apart. (3) Give every RF ground pad its own vias next to the pad (0.5 nH each, several in parallel). (4) Where you must cross between regions, give the return a path right next to the signal: a stitching capacitor or the plane continuing under the track. (5) Between boxes, decide the low-frequency grounding scheme (one bond point) and keep the RF shield bonded at both ends with low inductance. Shorter shared paths and slower edges both reduce L·dI/dt in proportion.

A digital bus switches 0.2 A in 2 ns, and its return shares 3 mm of track with an LNA bias circuit. What spike does the LNA see?

L ≈ 3 nH, dI/dt = 0.2/2×10−9 = 108 A/s, so V = 3×10−9 × 108 = 0.3 V. Even 3 mm of shared track is too much for a fast digital edge; it must have its own return path under its own tracks.

Step 8

Shielding and cavity isolation, and conducted and radiated EMC

You already know

RF modules sit in milled aluminium housings with walls between stages. You have seen a module whose isolation was fine with the lid off and poor with the lid on, or the reverse. Waveguide has a cut-off frequency set by its width.

Definitions

Shielding effectiveness (SE): the ratio, in dB, of the field without the shield to the field with it. A solid metal wall gives well over 100 dB; in practice SE is set by the holes in it.

Aperture (slot) leakage: a slot of longest dimension L is a good antenna once L approaches half a wavelength. Below that, SEslot ≈ 20·log10(λ/(2L)) dB; at L ≥ λ/2 it is about 0 dB. n similar slots lower it by about 10·log10(n).

Cavity resonance: a closed metal box of sides a × b × d is a resonator. Its lowest mode is at f = (c/2)·√((1/a)² + (1/b)²) for the two largest sides. At a resonance the field inside builds up by roughly the box Q, and isolation between two points in the box collapses. Absorber: lossy material on the lid that lowers the Q.

Conducted emission (CE): unwanted current or voltage on power and signal leads, measured into a standard 50 Ω network (LISN) in dBµV. Radiated emission (RE): unwanted field in dBµV/m at a set distance. Susceptibility: the reverse: how much field or lead noise the unit tolerates.

The housing does two jobs: it keeps other things out (and your own emissions in), and it separates stages from each other inside. Both fail in the same two ways. Holes leak when they are electrically long, and closed boxes resonate. A slot that is harmless at 1 GHz can be an open window at 10 GHz, and a compartment that isolates 60 dB at 3 GHz can isolate nothing at its first resonance.

The model in this step adds three terms: a 30 dB baseline from geometry and distance between the two points, plus the slot SE, minus the build-up near each box resonance, about 20·log10(Q) at the peak. With no absorber Q ≈ 100 (40 dB); a thin absorber gives Q ≈ 20 (26 dB), a thick one Q ≈ 5 (14 dB).

SEslot = 20·log10(λ/2L) − 10·log10(n)    fmnp = (c/2)·√((m/a)² + (n/b)² + (p/d)²)    isolation ≈ 30 dB + SEslot − 20·log10(Q)·(near resonance)Box 60 × 40 × 15 mm: lowest mode TE110 at (c/2)·√(1/0.06² + 1/0.04²) = 4.50 GHz, then 6.25, 7.90, 8.38 and 9.01 GHz. What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive housing is one 60 × 40 mm cavity with a lid held by four screws, leaving 30 mm gaps between screws. At 10 GHz the gaps give no SE at all (λ/2 = 15 mm), and the cavity's modes near 10 GHz eat the rest: isolation 5.0 dB. At 4.50 GHz, the first resonance, it is 0 dB: whatever is radiated by the LO at that frequency arrives at the LNA input at full strength. A filter with 70 dB rejection placed in such a box measures 70 dB on the bench with the lid off, and much less in the box.

The slot term and the resonance term are independent fixes. Closer screws (or a conductive gasket) shrink the slot; absorber lowers the resonance peak; walls make the cavities smaller, which pushes the first mode above the frequencies present in that cell. A compartment whose first mode is above every frequency inside it behaves like a waveguide below cut-off: fields decay instead of propagating. That is why X-band modules are full of narrow channels.

Conducted and radiated EMC follow the same rules at the unit's boundary. A 500 kHz buck draws its input current in pulses; if 2 mA rms of that reaches the 28 V leads, the LISN sees 2 mA × 50 Ω = 0.1 V = 100 dBµV, about 40 dB above a typical conducted-emission limit of the order of 60 dBµV at that frequency (illustrative). The fix is an input filter of 40 dB or more at fsw (Step 4, now on the input side) and the input capacitor right at the switch. For radiation, every connector hole, vent and seam is a slot: the same 20·log(λ/2L) rule says a 3 mm seam keeps 14 dB at 10 GHz, a 30 mm one keeps none. A unit that fails radiated emission usually has a seam or a cable shield problem, not a thin wall.

How to choose the housing

(1) List the frequencies present in each compartment (LO, its harmonics, IF, clocks, regulator harmonics). (2) Size each compartment so its first mode is above all of them by at least 20 %: for a 10 GHz LO, keep a × b small enough that (c/2)·√(1/a² + 1/b²) > 12 GHz. (3) Keep every seam and slot below λ/20 at the highest frequency of concern (1.5 mm at 10 GHz gives about 20 dB); use screws at that pitch or a gasket. (4) Where a compartment must be large, add absorber on the lid to bring Q down. (5) Filter every wire that crosses a wall (feed-through capacitors), because a wire through a wall is an antenna on both sides. (6) At the unit boundary, filter power inputs for CE and treat cable shields as part of the enclosure (360° bonding at connectors).

Step 9

Co-site interference: your own transmitter in your own receiver

You already know

Free-space path loss between two antennas is 20·log10(4π·d/λ) dB. An LNA has an input 1 dB compression point, and its noise figure sets the receiver's noise floor. A transmitter has a noise floor too: its output is not perfectly clean away from the carrier.

Definitions

Co-site: transmitters and receivers on the same platform (ship, aircraft, vehicle). Antenna-to-antenna isolation: path loss minus the antennas' gains toward each other (usually sidelobe or backlobe gains, often −10 dBi each).

Transmitter broadband noise: the noise the transmitter puts out at frequencies away from its carrier, in dBc/Hz. At the victim's frequency it adds to the victim's noise floor. Desensitisation: the rise of the victim's noise floor in dB, 10·log10(1 + Ntx/Nrx).

Blocking: the transmitter's carrier itself overloads the victim's front end (compresses the LNA or trips the limiter, see the limiter module). Blanking: switching the victim off, or flagging its data, while the own transmitter is on.

Co-site is the platform-level version of everything above. A 1 kW radar or jammer 2 m from an ESM antenna is 100 dB stronger than any signal the ESM is trying to find. Two separate things can go wrong: the carrier blocks the front end (that needs filtering or blanking), and the transmitter's own noise, which no filter at the receiver can remove because it is in the receiver's band, raises the floor.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive platform layout places antennas where there is room and treats each system as if it were alone. The ESM, specified for −65 dBm sensitivity, sees −18 dBm from its own radar, 7 dB above its −25 dBm input compression point: its front end is compressed for the whole pulse, and between pulses its floor is 7 dB higher, which costs 7 dB of sensitivity. For an ESM, whose detection range goes as the square root of received power, 7 dB is a range factor of 10−6.95/20 = 0.45: less than half the intercept range, caused entirely by its own platform.

(4) Noise check: Ntx at the victim must be at least 10 dB below Nrx for under 0.4 dB desensitisation; that sets the transmitter's noise in dBc/Hz at the victim's frequency, which becomes a transmit filter or exciter requirement. (5) Where nothing else fits, plan blanking and accept its duty-cycle cost.

Step 10

How a spur becomes a false target or a false ESM detection

You already know

A radar integrates N pulses coherently in a Doppler FFT: a steady target gains 10·log10(N) dB of signal-to-noise. A detector declares a target when a cell exceeds a threshold, typically 13 dB above the noise for a low false-alarm rate (radar module R3). An ESM receiver reports an emitter when a channel exceeds its threshold.

Definitions

Doppler bin: one output of the Doppler FFT, PRF/N wide. A tone at offset foff from the carrier appears at the Doppler frequency foff folded into ±PRF/2 (aliased), and is read as a radial velocity v = fD·λ/2.

Coherent spur: a spur whose phase is stable across the N pulses (a fixed regulator line, a clock spur). It integrates exactly like a target. Incoherent spur: one whose phase changes randomly from pulse to pulse (spread spectrum); it integrates like noise and gains nothing.

Clutter notch: the Doppler bins around zero that the MTI or clutter map removes. False target: a detection with no real object. False emitter: an ESM report of an emitter that does not exist; a CW line above threshold looks like a CW transmitter.

Every step above ends here. A spur that reaches the receiver is a signal, and the processing cannot tell it from a real one if it has the properties of a real one. A fixed, stable spur inside the radar's band is the worst case: it is coherent, so Doppler integration gives it the same 10·log(N) gain as a target, and it appears in every range cell (it is there all the time, not at one delay), at one Doppler, as a line of false targets all moving at the same speed. In an ESM receiver, a line above threshold is reported as an emitter, and dozens of regulator harmonics become dozens of false emitters, which overload the operator and the emitter library.

A spur of −120 dBm is −10 dB per pulse and +8.1 dB after 64 pulses: below a 13 dB threshold. At −115 dBm it is +13.1 dB: a false target in every range cell.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive check looks at the spur on a spectrum analyser at the receiver input: "−115 dBm is 5 dB below the noise, it cannot be seen". That is true for one pulse. After 64 coherent pulses it is 13.1 dB above the noise in its Doppler bin, at the threshold, and with a free-running regulator at 503.3 kHz it lands at −700 Hz Doppler: a target moving at 10.5 m/s at every range. In the ESM, a −90 dBm regulator harmonic is 14 dB above the −104 dBm channel noise, one dB under a 15 dB threshold; a slightly warmer day or a 1 dB gain change makes it an emitter.

How to set the spur requirement

(1) Find the largest integration gain the processing can give a coherent spur: 10·log(N) for the longest Doppler FFT, plus any further integration. (2) Require the spur at the receiver input to be below noise − 10·log(N) + threshold − margin: for N = 64, 13 dB threshold and 6 dB margin, Pspur < −110 − 18.1 + 13 − 6 = −121.1 dBm. (3) Carry that number back through the chain: coupling isolation (Steps 3, 7, 8), filtering (Step 4), and the regulator's own spectrum. (4) For an ESM, the requirement is per channel: below threshold − margin, at the highest gain setting. (5) Choose synchronised switching for coherent radars and keep the regulator frequency a multiple of the PRF if the PRF is fixed; with staggered PRF, use spread spectrum or more isolation.
