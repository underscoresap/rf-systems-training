The pulse radar, end to end

R1 to R3 treated the radar as an equation, a beam and a detector. This page opens the box. It follows one pulse from the trigger, through every block on the transmit side, out to the target, back through every block on the receive side and into the range cells of R3. Along the way it explains the problems a pulse radar must solve (the blind zone at short range, the huge spread of echo strengths, echoes from beyond the next pulse) and how pulse-to-pulse phase turns a pulse radar into a pulse-Doppler radar that can see moving targets in clutter.

Step 0

One pulse repetition interval, on a timeline

You already know

From R1: pulse width τ, PRI (pulse repetition interval) and PRF = 1/PRI, and that an echo from range R arrives 2R/c after the pulse. From R3: after each pulse the ADC samples continuously, and each sample is one range cell.

A pulse radar does one thing over and over, once every PRI. Everything in its hardware is organised around that rhythm. Before looking at any block, follow the time line of one PRI:

  1. Trigger (t = 0). A timing generator, driven by the radar's master clock, starts the cycle. Every other event in the PRI is timed from this edge.
  2. Switch to transmit. The T/R switch or duplexer connects the antenna to the transmitter. The receiver is protected (Step 2).
  3. Transmit (0 to τ). The power amplifier sends the pulse: for example 1 kW for 1 µs.
  4. Switch back and recover (τ to τ + trec). The switch returns to receive, and the protection circuits (limiter, LNA) take a short time to recover from the transmit leakage. During this time the radar cannot hear anything.
  5. Listen (τ + trec to PRI). The receiver and ADC record echoes. Sample n is range cell n. Most of the PRI is spent here: with τ = 1 µs and PRI = 1 ms, the radar listens for 99.7 % of the time.
  6. Next trigger. The cycle repeats. Anything still on its way back now arrives during the next PRI, which causes a problem in Step 5.

Definitions

Trigger: the timing edge that starts each PRI.
Listening window: the part of the PRI when the receiver is connected and recording.
Recovery time trec: time after the transmit pulse until the receiver works normally again.
Duty cycle: τ / PRI, the fraction of time spent transmitting (R1 step 6).

Things to check: make τ long and the blind zone grows (Step 3); move the target beyond the PRI and its echo arrives in the next PRI (Step 5).

Step 3

The blind zone at short range

The problem. The radar listens with the same antenna it transmits from. While the pulse is going out, and while the limiter and LNA recover from the transmit leakage, the receiver hears nothing. An echo from a target close enough to arrive in that time is simply lost. This is called eclipsing, and the range it covers is the blind range.

Rblind = c · (τ + trec) / 2
Caseτ + trecBlind range
Short pulse, fast limiter1 + 2 µs450 m
Long pulse for energy (no compression)50 + 2 µs7.8 km
Short pulse, slow limiter recovery1 + 20 µs3.1 km

Why not simply use a long pulse for more energy? R1 showed that SNR grows with pulse energy, so a long pulse is tempting. But the blind range grows with it (7.8 km for 50 µs), and the range resolution gets worse (cτ/2 = 7.5 km). Radars escape this trap with pulse compression (a long, frequency-coded pulse that is squeezed into a short one by the matched filter, covered in R6), and by using a short "fill-in" pulse for close range.

What sets trec. It is pure component behaviour: the T/R switch's switching time, the limiter diodes' recovery after being driven hard (charge stored in PIN diodes takes time to clear), and the LNA's recovery if it was pushed into compression. A limiter that recovers in 20 µs instead of 2 µs blinds the radar out to 3 km instead of 450 m.

Step 4

The huge spread of echo strengths, and STC

First, what this step is about, and what it is not about. Step 4 answers one narrow question: can every echo, strong or weak, be recorded by the ADC without overflowing? It does not separate targets from clutter. STC turns target and clutter down by exactly the same amount, so it never makes a target stand out from clutter. What it does is stop the clutter from wrecking the recording, so that the later steps (Doppler in Steps 6–7, CFAR in R3) can separate them. Part 4.6 explains how a target buried under clutter still gets through.

The step builds up in six parts: 4.1 how much the ADC can hold, 4.2 how strong echoes are at each range, 4.3 what goes wrong when the two are put together, 4.4 why the obvious fixes fail, 4.5 how STC fixes it and how it is chosen, and 4.6 how a target still gets through strong clutter. All numbers use R1's example radar (1 kW, 30 dBi, 10 GHz, 1 µs pulse).

4.1 The ADC is a window with a floor and a ceiling

Think of the ADC as a measuring jug with a fixed height. Anything smaller than its smallest step is lost in its own quantisation noise. Anything bigger than the brim overflows.

  • The floor is set by the receiver's thermal noise. In R1's radar the noise in a 1 MHz bandwidth with 4 dB noise figure is −110 dBm at the antenna. The receiver gain is chosen so that this noise just fills the lowest few ADC steps: weaker signals then still show up, because the noise carries them over the steps.
  • The ceiling is the ADC's full scale. A 12-bit ADC gives roughly 65 dB of usable range above that noise. So full scale sits 65 dB above −110 dBm: at −45 dBm, referred back to the antenna.

"Referred to the antenna" just means: we divide out the receiver gain so that echo powers and ADC limits can be compared in one unit. Any echo between −110 dBm and −45 dBm fits. Anything above −45 dBm clips.

Where "12 bits ≈ 65 dB" comes from

Step 1: count the steps. 12 bits give 2¹² = 4 096 output codes. The largest signal the ADC can record without clipping swings over all 4 096 codes. The smallest change it can record is one code (1 LSB, least significant bit).

Step 2: turn the ratio into dB, using voltage. The ADC measures voltage, so the ratio of largest to smallest is a voltage ratio: 4 096. A voltage ratio in dB is 20·log10, not 10·log10 (power goes as voltage squared): 20 × log10(4 096) = 20 × 3.61 = 72 dB. Each extra bit doubles the codes, and doubling a voltage is 20·log10(2) = 6 dB per bit. That is the rule of thumb.

Step 3: the textbook ideal. Rounding to the nearest code adds a small error, quantisation noise, of rms value LSB/√12. Compare a full-scale sine wave with that noise and you get the standard formula SNR = 6.02 × N + 1.76 dB = 6.02 × 12 + 1.76 = 74 dB for a perfect 12-bit ADC.

Step 4: subtract what real life costs.

  • A real ADC has its own thermal noise, clock jitter and nonlinearity, so it behaves like an ideal ADC with fewer bits: the effective number of bits (ENOB), typically 10.5–11 for a 12-bit part. That is about 65–68 dB.
  • The receiver noise must not sit exactly at the bottom code. It is placed a few codes up, so that weak signals riding on it are still recorded. That uses a few dB of the bottom.
  • Echoes are not steady sine waves: noise and clutter have peaks several dB above their rms value, so a few dB of headroom are kept at the top.

After those deductions about 65 dB of the 74 dB remain usable between the receiver's noise and the clipping level. That is the window used below.

From power to ADC code, and what "resolution" means

The ADC does not see power, it sees voltage. To connect the two, take a typical ADC: full scale 2 V peak-to-peak (±1 V), 50 Ω input, 12 bits.

  1. Full scale as a power. A sine that just reaches ±1 V has peak Vpk = 1 V. Its power is Vpk² / (2R) = 1 / 100 = 10 mW = +10 dBm.
  2. One code (1 LSB) as a voltage. The 2 V range is split into 4 096 codes: 1 LSB = 2 V / 4 096 = 0.49 mV. This is the ADC's voltage resolution, and it is the same everywhere on the scale.
  3. Power to code. A sine of power P has Vpk = √(2RP). Divide by the LSB: peak code = Vpk / LSB. For example −20 dBm → Vpk = 31.6 mV → ±65 codes.
  4. The smallest sine. A sine that swings just ±1 code has Vpk = 0.49 mV: power (0.49 mV)² / 100 = −56 dBm, 66 dB below full scale.
  5. Quantisation noise. The rounding error has power LSB² / 12 / R = −64 dBm. A full-scale sine (+10 dBm) is 74 dB above it: the 74 dB of the table above.

Resolution in dB is not constant. The voltage step is always 1 LSB, but a one-code change is a big change for a small signal and a tiny one for a big signal. Going from ±1 to ±2 codes is +6 dB. From ±1 000 to ±1 001 codes it is +0.009 dB. So the ADC measures strong echoes very finely and weak ones coarsely, which is one more reason the receiver noise is placed a few codes up (the noise then "dithers" weak echoes, and averaging over many samples recovers them).

Connecting to the antenna. The receiver gain G between antenna and ADC moves the whole window. With full scale at +10 dBm at the ADC and −45 dBm wanted at the antenna, G = 10 − (−45) = 55 dB. The thermal noise (−110 dBm at the antenna) then arrives at −55 dBm at the ADC: an rms voltage of √(50 Ω × 3.2 nW) = 0.40 mV, about 0.8 codes rms, with peaks of 2–3 codes. It just exercises the bottom few codes, as intended.

4.2 How strong are the echoes at each range?

A target (an aircraft) gets 12 dB weaker every time the range doubles. That is the R⁴ law from R1: double R, and 2⁴ = 16 times less power, which is 12 dB. Ten times further is 40 dB weaker.

Ground clutter gets only 9 dB weaker per doubling (R³). The reason: clutter is not one object but the patch of ground lit inside one range cell. That patch is one cell deep (150 m) and one beamwidth wide, and the width of the beam grows with range (2° at 1 km is 35 m wide, at 10 km it is 350 m). So a cell further away contains more ground, which partly makes up for the R⁴ loss: 16 times less per doubling, but 2 times more ground, leaves 8 times less, which is 9 dB.

4.3 What happens when the strongest echoes overflow

Close-in clutter arrives first, right after the pulse, and it is the strongest thing the radar ever sees. When it exceeds full scale the ADC clips: the tops of the waveform are cut flat. Clipping is not a harmless loss of a few cells:

  • A flattened waveform contains harmonics and intermodulation products that were never in the echo. They appear as false signals in other range cells and Doppler bins.
  • A clipped cell no longer has the right amplitude or phase, so the Doppler processing of Step 7 cannot separate clutter from targets in it.
  • The CFAR of R3 measures the background from neighbouring cells. Clipped cells give it a wrong background, so it sets wrong thresholds near them.

4.5 The fix: STC, a gain that rises with time after the pulse

Notice one thing about 4.2: the echo strength depends mostly on range, and range is just time after the pulse. The radar knows exactly what time it is. So it can turn its own gain up as the PRI goes on, on a fixed, known schedule:

  • Right after the pulse, close echoes arrive: they are strong, so the gain is low (high attenuation).
  • As time passes, echoes come from further away and are weaker, so the attenuation is reduced.
  • After a chosen time (the knee range, R0) the attenuation is zero and the radar has its full sensitivity for far targets.

This is sensitivity time control (STC). Because the schedule is fixed and known, it does not confuse CFAR or Doppler processing the way AGC does.

STC attenuation = 40 · log10(R0 / R) dB for R < R0, and 0 beyond   (R⁴ law)The attenuation falls by 12 dB every time the range doubles, exactly as fast as a target's echo rises when it comes closer. Use 30 instead of 40 for an R³ law, which follows ground clutter.

Why the target stays at −91 dBm: two changes that cancel. Follow the target as it comes closer, from 5 km to 2.5 km (half the range):

  • Its echo gets 12 dB stronger (R⁴ law: 2⁴ = 16 times, which is 12 dB). From −91 dBm it would rise to −79 dBm.
  • The STC attenuation at 2.5 km is 40 × log10(5 000 / 2 500) = 40 × 0.30 = 12 dB.
  • −79 dBm − 12 dB = −91 dBm.

Halve the range again, to 1.25 km: the echo gains another 12 dB and the STC adds another 12 dB. The same at every range inside the knee. In formula form: echo = −91 + 40·log(5 000/R), STC = 40·log(5 000/R), so echo − STC = −91 dBm, whatever R is. That is exactly why the law is called "R⁴": it is chosen to rise at the same rate as the echo does.

Look at the last column. So with the R⁴ law, a 1 m² target inside the knee always arrives at −91 dBm, the same level as at 5 km. That is 19 dB above the noise: still easy to detect. STC only takes away sensitivity that short-range targets did not need, because they were far too strong anyway. In exchange the clutter now fits the ADC.

Choosing the STC.

  1. Knee range R0: the range out to which clutter would otherwise overflow the ADC, plus some margin. Further out, STC must be zero so that no sensitivity is lost where targets are weak.
  2. Law: R⁴ keeps a point target at constant level (good for aircraft). R³ follows ground clutter more closely, and attenuates less at short range. Many radars use something between the two.
  3. Maximum attenuation: enough for the strongest clutter at the shortest range the radar must work at (just beyond the blind range of Step 3).

STC is an analogue attenuator (often a PIN-diode or digital step attenuator) before the mixer or the ADC, driven from the radar's timing. Its curve must be smooth and repeatable: a step or ripple in it looks to CFAR like a change in the background.

4.6 If clutter is so strong, how does a target get through?

This is the right question, and the honest answer has three parts.

(a) STC does not change who is stronger. At 1 km the 1 m² target is −63 dBm and the clutter in the same cell is −46 dBm: the target is 17 dB weaker than the clutter beside it. After 28 dB of STC they are −91 and −74 dBm: still 17 dB apart. A threshold on amplitude alone (R3) cannot find this target. STC's only job was to make sure both are recorded correctly, without clipping.

(b) Clutter only competes inside the same cell. Ground clutter is spread over the ground; a target is a point. Several things reduce how much clutter shares the target's cell:

  • Short cells (short pulses or pulse compression) put less ground in each cell, so less clutter competes. Halving the cell length halves the clutter in it (−3 dB).
  • A narrow beam, pointed above the ground, puts only its sidelobes on the ground. An aircraft at altitude may sit in a cell where the clutter is 20–40 dB weaker than the table above, which assumed the main beam on the ground.
  • Large targets (a 100 m² ship or airliner) are 20 dB stronger than the 1 m² example and simply rise above the clutter.

(c) The main tool: motion (Steps 6 and 7). The ground does not move; the target does. In a pulse-Doppler radar the FFT across pulses puts the clutter in the zero-Doppler bin and the target in its own Doppler bin. In the target's bin the clutter is gone (only the small residue from phase noise is left), and the target competes only with thermal noise again. That is how a target 17 dB, or even 50 dB, below the clutter is found.

And this is why clipping matters so much. Doppler separation works only if every pulse records the clutter and the target with the right amplitude and phase. If the clutter clips, the recording is distorted, the clutter spreads across all Doppler bins, and the target is lost even after the FFT. STC protects the recording, and the Doppler processing then pulls the target out of it.

Step 5

Echoes from beyond the next pulse

The problem. The radar measures range by timing an echo from the most recent pulse. But it cannot tell which pulse an echo belongs to. If a target is so far away that its echo arrives after the next pulse has gone out, the radar times it from the wrong pulse and reports it far too close. This is a second-time-around (or multiple-time-around) echo.

Rua = c · PRI / 2 = c / (2 · PRF)    apparent range = true range − k · RuaRua = maximum unambiguous range. k = how many PRIs late the echo is.
PRFRuaTarget at 20 km appears at
1 kHz150 km20 km (correct)

Why not just use a low PRF? For range alone, that works. But a high PRF is needed for other reasons: more pulses per dwell (R2), higher average power for the same peak power (R1), and above all an unambiguous Doppler measurement (Step 7). Pulse-Doppler radars deliberately run at PRFs where range is ambiguous.

How the radar spots a second-time-around echo: PRF stagger. Change the PRI slightly from pulse to pulse (say ±10 %). A real near target is timed from its own pulse, so it stays at the same range every time. An echo from a far target is timed from a pulse it does not belong to, and as the PRI changes, its apparent range jumps. The radar rejects (or resolves) anything that jumps. Solving for the true range from several PRFs is covered in R6.

Things to check: with a constant PRI, both look like steady targets and the far one is reported at the wrong range; with stagger, the near target stays in a straight column while the far one jumps from row to row.

Step 6

Coherence: why the phase from pulse to pulse matters

You already know

A phasor, phase noise, and that mixing with an LO transfers the LO's phase onto the IF signal.

The problem. A target flying over the ground is buried in ground clutter that may be 40–60 dB stronger in the same range cell (Step 4). Amplitude alone cannot separate them. What differs is motion: the target moves, the ground does not.

6.1 What "the phase of an echo" is

The radar's carrier is a sine wave with wavelength λ. At 10 GHz, λ = 3 cm (to keep the numbers simple we use exactly 3 cm). Every 3 cm of path, the wave goes through one full cycle: 360°.

The echo travels from the antenna to the target and back: a path of 2R. Count how many wavelengths fit into that path. The whole number of wavelengths does not matter: each whole cycle brings the wave back to where it started. Only the leftover fraction matters, because it decides at which point of its cycle the wave arrives back at the antenna. That fraction, times 360°, is the echo's phase.

echo phase = (fractional part of 2R / λ) × 360°

The receiver measures this phase by mixing the echo with the STALO (the same reference the transmitter used) into I and Q: I = A·cos φ and Q = A·sin φ. Together, I and Q give both the amplitude A and the phase φ, the phasor drawn in the demo below.

The phase moved from 240° to 150°: a change of 90°. Notice what happened: the target moved only 3.75 mm, but the path changed by 7.5 mm, which is exactly a quarter of a 30 mm wavelength. A quarter of a cycle is 90°.

The shortcut skips the big numbers, because only the change matters:

phase change = 360° × (2 · ΔR) / λ = 360° × (2 × 3.75 mm) / 30 mm = 360° × 0.25 = 90°

On pulse 3 the target is another 3.75 mm closer and the phase is 60°; on pulse 4, 330°; on pulse 5, 240° again. Every 4 pulses the phasor goes once round. A rotation of one turn every 4 pulses = 4 000 / 4 = 1 000 turns per second: that is the Doppler frequency, 1 kHz. The Doppler shift is nothing more than this steady turning of the echo phase, observed once per pulse.

Two things this explains.

  • Why a millimetre matters. The radar cannot measure 3.75 mm of range (its range cells are 150 m), yet it sees the 3.75 mm movement clearly, as a 90° phase step. Phase is an extremely sensitive ruler, but only for changes, and only modulo one wavelength.
  • Why it can be fooled. If the target moved 15 mm per pulse (60 m/s), the path changes by 30 mm, exactly one wavelength: 360°, which looks the same as 0°. The target looks stationary: a blind speed. At 30 m/s the step is 180°, and the radar cannot tell whether the phasor turned forward or backward. That is the limit of unambiguous speed (Step 7).

In general: a target closing at speed v moves ΔR = v · PRI between pulses, so the phase step is 360° × 2v·PRI/λ, and the rate of turning is fd = 2v/λ turns per second.

Summary of how motion shows up. Between two pulses the target moves ΔR = v · PRI. The round trip changes by 2ΔR, so the echo phase changes by 360° × 2ΔR/λ. A stationary target gives the same phase every pulse; a moving one gives a phase that turns by a fixed step every pulse. That steady rotation is the Doppler shift, fd = 2v/λ, sampled once per pulse.

The catch: the radar must know the phase it transmitted. The measured echo phase is (target phase) − (transmitted phase). If the transmitter starts every pulse at a random phase, the 90° steps are hidden under a random number and nothing can be measured. That is the situation with a magnetron, a simple oscillator-type transmitter that starts each pulse at a random phase (a non-coherent radar).

The fix: a coherent radar. Build the transmit pulse by amplifying a slice of a continuous, stable reference (the STALO), and use that same STALO as the receiver's LO. Now every pulse starts at a known phase, and any change in echo phase is due to the target. This is why Step 1 drew one master clock feeding both chains. (Older magnetron radars stored each pulse's phase and used it as a reference: "coherent on receive".)

What limits coherence: your components. Anything that changes the phase from pulse to pulse adds a false Doppler: STALO phase noise, PA phase changing with supply voltage or temperature (pulse-to-pulse phase stability), timing jitter. Step 7 shows how this spreads clutter over the Doppler bins.

Try it: the echo phasor in one range cell on 8 successive pulses, numbered. Left: the ground (stationary). Right: the moving target. Things to check: coherent, 15 m/s, 4 kHz gives exactly 90° per pulse; switch to magnetron and both phasors jump randomly, so motion can no longer be seen; set 30 m/s at 4 kHz and the step is 180°: at 60 m/s it is 360°, which looks exactly like a stationary target (a blind speed, Step 7).

Step 7

Pulse-Doppler: sorting echoes by speed

The idea. Collect N pulses (a coherent processing interval, CPI). In each range cell you now have N complex samples, one per pulse: exactly the slow-time column of R3 Step 0. A steady phase rotation across those samples is a sinusoid at fd. An FFT across the N samples separates the cell's contents by Doppler: stationary clutter lands in the zero-Doppler bin, a moving target in its own bin. Doing this for every range cell gives a range-Doppler map, and the CFAR of R3 then runs on that map.

The trade this creates. Unambiguous speed needs a high PRF; unambiguous range needs a low PRF (Step 5). At 10 GHz no single PRF gives both 150 km and ±300 m/s. Radars choose a low, medium or high PRF regime and use several PRFs to resolve the ambiguities (R6).

Where phase noise hurts. Clutter is not a perfect zero-Doppler line. The STALO's phase noise modulates the clutter echo, so a copy of the phase-noise spectrum appears around the clutter bin. A Doppler bin at offset f holds the clutter power × L(f) × bin width. Example: clutter 60 dB above noise, L(1 kHz) = −90 dBc/Hz, bin 62.5 Hz (+18 dB): residue = 60 − 90 + 18 = −12 dB, below noise. With a poorer oscillator at −70 dBc/Hz the residue is +8 dB above noise in the target's bin, and the target is masked. The radar's ability to see small movers in heavy clutter is set directly by the synthesiser's phase noise.

Try it: range cells across, Doppler bins up the side (zero Doppler in the middle), brightness in dB. Clutter fills range cells 0–39 at zero Doppler; the target (10 dB SNR per pulse, at range cell 30) sits in its own Doppler bin. Things to check: raise the phase noise and a skirt grows out of the clutter until it covers the target; set the speed to a blind speed (60 m/s at 4 kHz) and the target lands on the clutter; set 40 m/s at 4 kHz and the target appears at −20 m/s (Doppler aliasing).

Step 8

Where your components show up in a pulse radar

Where your component shows up

  • Limiter: recovery time sets the blind range (Step 3); flat and spike leakage decide whether the LNA survives; insertion loss adds directly to the noise figure.
  • T/R switch / duplexer: isolation sets how much transmit power the limiter must absorb; switching time adds to the blind range.
  • LNA: NF sets the noise floor; recovery after overload adds to the blind range.
  • STC attenuator: attenuation range and law set the short-range dynamic range; any step or ripple in its curve appears as a false change in the background that CFAR sees.
  • STALO / synthesiser: phase noise sets how much clutter spills into the Doppler bins (Step 7); frequency switching time limits frequency agility.
  • PA: pulse-to-pulse phase and amplitude stability limit coherence; droop across the pulse distorts the matched filter output.
  • ADC: number of bits and full scale decide how much of Step 4's echo range fits; clipping corrupts CFAR and the Doppler FFT.
  • Master clock and timing: trigger jitter appears as range jitter and as phase noise on the samples.
