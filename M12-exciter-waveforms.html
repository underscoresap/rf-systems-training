Exciters and waveform generation, from a phase accumulator to a jammer

The exciter is the part of a radar, SAR or jammer that creates the signal before the power amplifier makes it big. Everything the receiver later does with that signal, pulse compression, Doppler filtering, clutter cancellation, imaging, assumes the transmitted waveform was exactly what the designer drew on paper. Every departure from that drawing, a spur, a bent chirp, a phase that wanders from pulse to pulse, a synthesiser still settling, an image left by the modulator, shows up later as a false target, a raised sidelobe, a smeared target or a target hidden under clutter. This page builds the exciter from first principles: what it is, which waveforms it makes, how a DDS and a DAC make them, and then, one impairment at a time, how a component choice becomes a datasheet line and how that line becomes a system number. Every term is defined before it is used, every step has worked numbers, and every bench is explained before you touch it.

Step 0

What an exciter is and where it sits

You already know

A synthesiser makes a clean carrier at a programmable frequency; a mixer moves a signal from one frequency to another; a DAC turns numbers into a voltage; a power amplifier (PA) makes the signal big. The exciter is simply these parts arranged so that the signal leaving it is the exact waveform the radar wants, at the right frequency, at a level the PA can take.

Definitions

Waveform: the exact shape in time of the transmitted signal: its envelope (how the amplitude rises, stays and falls), its frequency against time and its phase against time. A radar is designed around one waveform; the receiver's processing is the mirror image of it.

Exciter: everything between the frequency reference and the PA input that creates the waveform: waveform generator, upconverter, filters and driver amplifier. It also hands a copy of its local oscillators to the receiver so that transmit and receive share one time and phase base.

Waveform generator: the block that makes the waveform at a low frequency, today almost always digital: a DDS (direct digital synthesiser, Step 2) or an arbitrary waveform generator (AWG), which is a DAC playing samples from memory.

Coherent system: one in which every oscillator (DAC clock, local oscillators, ADC clock) is derived from one reference, so that the phase of each transmitted pulse is known exactly at the receiver. Without coherence there is no Doppler processing and no SAR (Step 7).

Purity: how close the real waveform is to the ideal one. It is measured as spurs and phase noise (unwanted frequency content), phase and frequency error against time, and amplitude and phase ripple across the band.

A modern exciter works in four stages. (1) A reference (typically a 100 MHz oven-controlled crystal oscillator) sets the time base. (2) A clock and LO synthesiser derives from it the DAC clock (say 1 GHz) and the local oscillators (say 9.4 GHz). (3) A waveform generator (DDS or AWG plus DAC) makes the waveform at a low frequency, for example a 50 MHz-wide chirp centred at 100 MHz. (4) An upconverter (mixer or IQ modulator, then filters) moves it to the transmit frequency, here 9.5 GHz, and a driver brings it to the PA's input level. The same LO goes to the receiver's downconverter, so the receiver measures phase against the very same clock that made the pulse.

A worked number for why purity matters: a radar sees a ground clutter return 60 dB stronger than a small aircraft at the same range (Step 7). If the exciter adds anything to the transmitted signal that is only 50 dB below the wanted signal, a spur, an image, a phase wobble, the clutter carries that unwanted part too, 60 − 50 = 10 dB above the aircraft. The aircraft is lost, and no receiver improvement can recover it, because the fault was transmitted.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The simplest transmitter, and the oldest, is a free-running power oscillator (a magnetron, or a VCO followed by a PA) switched on and off for each pulse. It makes a pulse, but: (1) the starting phase of every pulse is random, uniformly anywhere in 0–360°, so the receiver cannot compare one pulse with the next and clutter spreads over all Doppler filters (with 64 pulses the clutter floor is only 10·log1064 = 18 dB below the clutter peak, Step 7); (2) the only waveform it can make is a fixed-frequency pulse, so range resolution and energy fight each other (Step 1); (3) its edges are as fast as the switch, so its spectrum splatters into neighbouring bands (Step 6). A coherent, digitally generated exciter removes all three, and then each of its own parts must be good enough not to bring the problems back.

How to think about exciter specs

Work backwards from the receiver: (1) the processing (pulse compression, Doppler filtering, SAR focusing) promises a sidelobe level or a clutter rejection; (2) every exciter impairment adds an error term below that level; (3) give each block a budget so that the sum stays, say, 6 dB under the promise. The next ten steps give you the formula, the worked number and a bench for each term. Phase noise itself is covered in the synthesiser module (02); DAC sampling in the ADC and DAC module (07); pulse compression in the digital receiver module (09). This page uses their results and does not repeat them.

Where your component shows up

  • Synthesiser and reference designers: your phase noise, spurs and lock time are copied onto every transmitted pulse.
  • DAC and FPGA designers: your bits, clock and look-up tables are the waveform.
  • Mixer, filter and amplifier designers: your image rejection, leakage, ripple and AM-PM decide how much of the ideal waveform survives to the antenna.

Check yourself

Why can no receiver fix a spur that the exciter transmitted?

Because the spur is reflected by every target and clutter patch exactly like the wanted signal. It arrives at the receiver as part of the echo, and processing designed for the wanted waveform cannot tell it from a real return.

What makes a radar "coherent"?

All its clocks and LOs come from one reference, so the phase of each transmitted pulse is known at the receiver. That allows pulse-to-pulse phase comparison: Doppler filtering, MTI and SAR.

Step 1

Waveform types: CW pulse, LFM chirp, phase codes, FMCW ramps, stepped frequency

You already know

A pulse of length τ occupies a bandwidth of about 1/τ. Radar measures range from the round-trip delay: R = c·t/2, with c = 3×108 m/s. The pulse page (R4) and the digital receiver module (09) explain the matched filter; here we only need its result.

Definitions

Range resolution ΔR: the smallest separation at which two equal targets show as two peaks. ΔR = c/(2B), where B is the bandwidth the waveform occupies. 1 MHz → 150 m; 10 MHz → 15 m; 100 MHz → 1.5 m. We measure it on the bench as the width of the compressed peak at −3 dB.

Pulse energy: peak power × pulse length. Detection range depends on energy, not peak power. A 10 µs pulse has 10 times (10 dB) the energy of a 1 µs pulse at the same peak power.

Matched filter / pulse compression: the receiver correlates the echo with a copy of the transmitted waveform. For a waveform of bandwidth B the output peak is about 1/B wide, whatever the pulse length. That is how a long pulse (energy) can still give fine range resolution (bandwidth).

Time-bandwidth product TB: τ × B. It is the compression ratio: a 10 µs, 10 MHz chirp has TB = 100, so it compresses 100 times, from 10 µs to 0.1 µs.

Sidelobes, PSLR: the compressed output has small peaks either side of the main one. The peak sidelobe ratio (PSLR) is the highest of them relative to the peak, in dB. A strong target's sidelobes can hide a weak neighbour.

The five families: CW pulse: a fixed frequency switched on for τ; B = 1/τ. LFM chirp (linear frequency modulation): the frequency sweeps linearly through B during the pulse. Phase code: the pulse is cut into equal chips and each chip's phase is set to 0° or 180° by a code; the 13-chip Barker code is the best known; B = 1/chip. FMCW ramp (frequency-modulated continuous wave): the transmitter never switches off and the frequency ramps repeatedly through B; the echo is mixed with the transmit signal and the difference frequency gives range (Step 4). Stepped frequency: a burst of narrow pulses, each at a frequency Δf higher than the last; after the burst the receiver combines them into a profile with the resolution of the total span B = M·Δf.

The exciter's first job is to make one of these shapes exactly. Each family trades something: the CW pulse is trivial to make but ties resolution to length; the chirp needs a precise sweep; a phase code needs fast, clean 180° phase steps; FMCW needs a precise ramp and a transmitter that is on all the time; stepped frequency needs a synthesiser that hops and settles quickly (Step 8) and a target that stays still for the whole burst.

ΔR = c / (2B)    E = Ppeak·τ    TB = τ·B    stepped: B = M·Δf, Runamb = c / (2Δf)With B in MHz, c/2B in metres is 150/B. For stepped frequency, M steps of Δf give the fine resolution of the full span, but the profile repeats every c/(2Δf): 32 steps of 3.125 MHz give 1.5 m resolution and a 48 m window.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive waveform is a plain CW pulse. To see a small target at long range the radar needs energy, so it uses a long pulse: 10 µs. Its bandwidth is then 0.1 MHz and its −3 dB range resolution is 878 m, so two aircraft 500 m apart are one blob. Shortening the pulse to 1 µs gives 88 m, but the energy falls by 10 dB, and getting it back needs 10 times the peak power from the PA. A 10 µs chirp of 10 MHz keeps the 10 dB of energy and gives 13.3 m.

How to choose the waveform

(1) Set B from the resolution you need: ΔR = 150/B(MHz). (2) Set τ from the energy you need, limited by the PA's duty cycle and by the eclipse (the radar cannot receive while it transmits: a 10 µs pulse blinds the first c·τ/2 = 1.5 km). (3) If TB is large, use an LFM chirp: it tolerates Doppler shift and is easy to generate digitally. (4) If the PA must be on continuously and the range is short (automotive, altimeters, missile seekers), use FMCW. (5) If the instantaneous bandwidth of the hardware is small but the synthesiser can hop, use stepped frequency. (6) Use phase codes when you want low sidelobes without weighting, or many different codes for different pulses.

Check yourself

A 20 µs chirp of 5 MHz and a 1 µs chirp of 5 MHz: which resolves better?

Neither: both give c/2B = 30 m. The 20 µs chirp has 13 dB more energy.

Why does stepped frequency need a stationary target?

The profile is built from 32 separate pulses. If the target moves a significant fraction of a wavelength between them, the phases are wrong and the profile smears.

Step 2

DDS and DAC-based generation: clock, phase accumulator, truncation spurs

You already know

From the ADC and DAC module (07): a DAC clocked at fclk can produce frequencies up to fclk/2 (the Nyquist limit); its output steps are held for one clock period, which multiplies the spectrum by a sin(x)/x shape (sinc roll-off) and leaves copies (images) at fclk ± fout, 2fclk ± fout and so on. The synthesiser module (02) introduced the DDS as a frequency source; here we look inside it as a waveform generator.

Definitions

Phase accumulator: an N-bit register (N = 32 here) that adds a fixed number every clock and wraps round on overflow. Its value is the phase of the output, as a fraction of one cycle: 0 = 0°, 232 = 360°.

Frequency tuning word (FTW): the number added each clock. fout = FTW·fclk/2N. With fclk = 1 GHz and N = 32, one step of FTW is 0.233 Hz: the frequency resolution. To make a chirp, a second accumulator adds a fixed amount to the FTW every clock, so the frequency ramps exactly linearly.

Phase-to-amplitude look-up table (LUT): a memory that turns phase into sin(phase). A 32-bit address would need 232 entries, so only the top P bits are used (P = 12 to 16). Dropping the lower bits is phase truncation.

Truncation spurs: the dropped bits make a small, periodic phase error (a sawtooth up to 360°/2P). A periodic error makes discrete spurs. The worst spur is about −6.02·P dBc: 12 bits → −72 dBc. If the FTW has no ones in its lowest N − P bits, there is no truncation error and no truncation spur.

DAC bits D and SFDR: the amplitude is rounded to D bits. An ideal D-bit DAC has a signal-to-quantisation-noise ratio of 6.02·D + 1.76 dB over the Nyquist band. Spurious-free dynamic range (SFDR) is the distance from the carrier to the highest spur; in real DACs it is set more by analogue nonlinearity and glitches (typically 70–80 dBc) than by the bit count.

Reconstruction filter: the low-pass filter after the DAC that removes the images. Its job gets harder as fout approaches fclk/2, because the first image, at fclk − fout, comes closer.

A DDS makes the waveform from arithmetic. Because the phase is a number, the frequency is exact to 0.233 Hz, a chirp is exactly linear, a phase code is an exact 180° jump, and the starting phase of every pulse can be reset to the same value: the DDS is coherent by construction. Its errors come from finite numbers (truncation, rounding) and from the analogue DAC. An AWG is the alternative: precomputed samples played from memory; it can make any shape, including pre-distorted waveforms (Step 9), but needs memory proportional to pulse length × clock rate (10 µs at 1 GS/s = 10 000 samples per pulse).

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive DDS keeps only the top 8 bits of phase to save look-up memory. The worst truncation spur is then −48.5 dBc (measured on the bench at 123.4 MHz). After upconversion that spur is transmitted with the wanted signal; a clutter return 60 dB above a target carries the spur 11.5 dB above the target. The second naive mistake is to run the DDS up to 0.45 fclk to get more bandwidth: at 400 MHz the first image (600 MHz) is only 3.5 dB below the wanted signal and 200 MHz away, so the reconstruction filter must climb 60 dB in 200 MHz, and the sinc has already cost 2.42 dB at the top of the band (this droop is also amplitude ripple across a chirp, Step 9).

How to choose fclk, P and D

(1) fclk ≥ 2.5 × the highest output frequency, so the useful band ends near 0.4 fclk, the first image is ≥ 0.2 fclk away and sinc droop stays under 2.4 dB (equalise it digitally if the chirp spans much of the band). (2) P from the spur budget: a −70 dBc requirement needs P ≥ 12 (−72 dBc) plus margin, or dither. (3) D: 12–14 bits; buy the DAC on its SFDR at your output frequency, not on its bit count. (4) The DAC clock's phase noise and spurs go straight onto the output, scaled by fout/fclk (module 02).

Check yourself

Why can a DDS chirp be perfectly linear when a ramped VCO cannot?

The DDS frequency is FTW·fclk/2N and the FTW is ramped by adding an exact number each clock; the only references are the clock and arithmetic. A VCO's frequency depends on a non-linear tuning curve, temperature and the drive circuit.

Which FTWs give no truncation spur with P = 12, N = 32?

Those whose lowest 20 bits are zero: multiples of 220, i.e. output frequencies that are multiples of fclk/4096 = 244.14 kHz.

Step 3

Chirp linearity and phase error: what they do to range sidelobes and resolution after compression

You already know

From Step 1: a chirp compresses to a peak about 1/B wide with −13.4 dB sidelobes, or −42.7 dB with Hamming weighting. From the filters module (04): a filter's group-delay ripple puts paired echoes on a compressed chirp. This step adds the exciter's own errors.

Definitions

Instantaneous frequency: the rate of change of phase: f(t) = (1/2π)·dφ/dt. For an ideal LFM chirp it is a straight line from −B/2 to +B/2 in τ, so the phase is a parabola: φ(t) = π·K·t², with chirp rate K = B/τ (here 100 MHz/10 µs = 10 MHz per µs).

Phase error φe(t): the difference between the transmitted phase and the ideal parabola, after removing any constant and any straight-line part (a constant is an arbitrary phase; a straight line is only a small range shift). It is the quantity that matters to the matched filter.

Chirp linearity: how straight the frequency-against-time line is. A frequency error δf(t) integrates into a phase error: φe = 2π∫δf dt. So a small but slow frequency error over a long pulse is a big phase error.

Quadratic phase error (QPE): φe = a·u², where u runs from −1 at the start of the pulse to +1 at the end, so a is the error at the pulse edges. It is the phase of a wrong chirp rate (the frequency line is straight but too steep or too shallow). Its effect: a wider main lobe and filled-in nulls.

Cubic phase error: φe = a·u³: the frequency line is bowed (curved). Its effect: asymmetric sidelobes, one side raised.

Sinusoidal phase ripple: φe = a·sin(2π·n·t/τ), n cycles over the pulse. Its effect: a pair of paired echoes, small copies of the main peak at ±n range cells (n·c/2B), each 20·log10(a/2) dB below the peak.

ISLR: integrated sidelobe ratio: all the energy outside the main lobe, relative to the energy inside it. It matters for distributed clutter and SAR images, where many weak sidelobes add up.

Why the phase of a chirp maps onto range sidelobes: during a chirp, frequency and time are tied together (at time t the frequency is K·t). Pulse compression lines up all frequencies so they add in phase at one instant. Any error that bends one part of the pulse out of phase leaves that part's energy uncancelled at other instants: sidelobes. A slow error (quadratic) spreads the energy just beside the peak and widens it; a periodic error of n cycles puts energy exactly n range cells away, as echoes. The measure engineers use is the peak phase error in degrees.

φe(t) = 2π ∫ δf(t) dt    echo level = 20·log10(a/2)    echo position = ±n·c/(2B)Worked: a sinusoidal phase ripple of a = 0.1 rad (5.7°) peak with 5 cycles in the pulse puts echoes at 20·log(0.05) = −26.0 dB, at ±5 × 1.5 m = ±7.5 m. On the bench (6°, the nearest slider step: predicted −25.6 dB) Hamming weighting gives a PSLR of −25.1 dB: the echoes, not the window, now set the sidelobe level. A frequency error of only 10 kHz held for 5 µs is 2π × 104 × 5×10−6 = 0.31 rad = 18°.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive chirp is made by ramping the tuning voltage of a free-running VCO. VCO tuning curves are curved: a "1 % linear" VCO is 1 MHz off at the middle of a 100 MHz sweep. Integrated over the 10 µs pulse that bow is about 240° of cubic-shaped phase error after removing the straight-line part, and with Hamming weighting the bench gives a PSLR of −14.7 dB instead of −42.7 dB: the weighting has been thrown away. To stay at 2–3° the frequency must be straight to about 10 kHz in 100 MHz, 0.01 %: only a DDS, an AWG or a closed-loop (PLL) chirp gets there.

How to choose the phase-error budget

(1) Start from the PSLR or ISLR the system needs, for example −35 dB. (2) Paired echoes from all ripple sources add; give the exciter, say, an echo budget of −41 dB (6 dB below), so a ≤ 2 × 10−41/20 = 0.018 rad = 1.0° peak for any periodic term. (3) For slow (quadratic) errors, keep the peak below about 45°: the width grows only 1.5 % but PSLR is already −34.7 dB. (4) Convert to a frequency spec with φe = 2π∫δf dt and allocate it to the synthesiser, DAC filter, mixer and PA. (5) If the hardware cannot meet it, measure the transmitted chirp through a loop-back path and pre-distort the DDS or AWG phase (Step 9).

Why is a linear (straight-line) phase error harmless?

A phase proportional to time is a constant frequency offset; for a chirp it only shifts the compressed peak by δf/K in time (a tiny range offset). The shape is unchanged.

Step 4

FMCW ramp nonlinearity and target smearing

You already know

From Step 1: an FMCW radar transmits a continuous frequency ramp. The FMCW explainer shows the beat-frequency idea in pictures. A mixer's output contains the difference frequency of its two inputs (module 05).

Definitions

Ramp: one linear sweep of B in time Tr. Here B = 150 MHz in Tr = 100 µs, so the slope is K = B/Tr = 1.5 MHz per µs. Range resolution c/2B = 1.0 m.

Beat frequency fb: the echo from range R is a copy of the ramp delayed by τ = 2R/c. Mixing it with the ramp being transmitted gives the difference frequency fb = K·τ = 2KR/c. At 1000 m: τ = 6.67 µs, fb = 10.0 MHz. An FFT of the beat signal is the range profile: frequency is range.

Ramp nonlinearity δ: the largest departure of the ramp's frequency from a straight line, as a fraction of B. δ = 0.1 % of 150 MHz = 150 kHz.

Nonlinearity shape: a bow (frequency bulges in the middle, from a curved VCO tuning law), an S-curve (bulges one way then the other), or a ripple (5 wiggles in the ramp, from a loop or a supply interacting with the ramp).

Smearing: because the error is in the transmitted frequency, the beat frequency is fb = K·τ + τ·dδf/dt: it varies during the ramp by τ times the slope error. The FFT then shows a spread of frequencies instead of one line: the target's peak becomes wider and lower.

The key fact: the smear grows with range. A target at 100 m has τ = 0.67 µs, so its beat barely notices the ramp's error; a target at 1000 m has ten times the delay and ten times the smear. For a bow of size δ, the slope error swings between ±4δB/Tr, so the beat swings by ±4δ·fb and the target spreads over about 8δ·R in range: 0.1 % at 1000 m is 8 m, eight resolution cells. Pulse radars see a fixed error at every range (Step 3); FMCW sees an error that scales with range.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive FMCW source is a VCO driven by a linear voltage ramp. A typical VCO tuning law is bowed by a few per cent; even after a one-time correction table, temperature leaves 0.1 %. The bench gives: near target (100 m) −3 dB width 1.46 m, unchanged; far target (1000 m) 2.65 m instead of 1.55 m and 2.6 dB lower; at 3000 m, 7.0 m wide and 5.9 dB lower. Step 5

Upconversion: image and LO leakage, and the filtering that removes them

You already know

From the mixers module (05): a mixer driven by an LO at fLO turns an input at fIF into two outputs, fLO + fIF and fLO − fIF, and leaks some LO straight through. An IQ (quadrature) mixer can cancel one of the two outputs.

Definitions

Upconverter: the mixer or modulator that moves the waveform from the DAC's frequency (fIF, here 100 MHz) to the transmit frequency (fLO + fIF = 9.5 GHz with a 9.4 GHz LO).

Wanted sideband and image: fLO + fIF is wanted; fLO − fIF is the image. A plain (real, double-sideband) mixer makes both at equal level. For a chirp the image is the mirrored chirp, sweeping the opposite way.

IQ modulator, single sideband (SSB): two mixers driven 90° apart, fed with the waveform's I (cosine) and Q (sine) parts from two DACs. Ideally the image cancels. A gain imbalance g (dB) or phase imbalance φ (degrees) between the two paths leaves part of it.

Image rejection ratio (IRR): wanted / image, in dB: IRR = 10·log10[(1 + 2g·cos φ + g²)/(1 − 2g·cos φ + g²)], with g as a voltage ratio. 0.5 dB and 3° → 28.2 dB; 0.1 dB and 1° → 39.6 dB.

LO leakage (carrier feedthrough): the LO itself at the output, in dBc. It comes from the mixer's LO-to-RF isolation and, in an IQ modulator, from DC offsets on the I and Q inputs. Typical: −30 to −40 dBc before calibration.

Post-mixer filter: a band-pass filter centred on the wanted signal. Its attenuation at the image (2fIF away) and at the LO (fIF away) adds to the mixer's own rejection.

The image and the LO leak are transmitted and reflected like the wanted signal. The image chirp, compressed by a filter matched to the wanted chirp, does not compress: it spreads into a low floor of about −IRR − 10·log10(TB) dB, which with TB = 500 and a 28 dB IRR is −55 dB under every strong return. In FMCW and in pulse radars whose LO runs during reception, the LO leak is also a strong unmodulated signal that sits in the receiver at zero beat frequency. Both also fall into neighbours' bands: spectrum rules usually demand that every unwanted output is below a limit; here −60 dBc.

Step 6

Pulse shaping and the spectral mask

You already know

A rectangular pulse of length τ has a sin(x)/x spectrum with nulls every 1/τ, whose peaks fall at 20 dB per decade. Faster edges mean more spectrum far from the carrier. The filters module (04) treats the transmit output filter; here we shape the pulse at its source, in the DAC.

Definitions

Envelope: the slowly varying amplitude of the pulse. Rise time tr: the time the envelope takes from 10 % to 90 % of full amplitude (fall time likewise).

Spectral mask: a limit line of allowed emission against frequency offset, relative to the peak. The illustrative mask used here: 0 dB out to f1 = 2/τ + B/2, then falling 40 dB per decade, with a floor at −70 dB. For a 1 µs CW pulse, f1 = 2 MHz and the mask is −40 dB at 20 MHz.

Edge shapes: rectangular (as fast as the DAC, about 1 ns here); trapezoidal (straight-line edges); raised-cosine (edges shaped as half a cosine, smooth at both ends); Gaussian (edges as if the rectangle passed through a Gaussian filter, smoothest of all).

Why the shape matters: a straight edge has a corner at each end. Each corner (a sudden change of slope) produces spectrum that falls at only 40 dB per decade beyond 1/(π·tr); a sharp step (no edge at all) falls at only 20 dB per decade. Smooth edges with no corners fall faster still.

Energy loss: softer edges remove part of the pulse's energy. Loss = 10·log10(∫a²dt / τ), where a is the envelope (rectangle = 0 dB).

In a digital exciter the envelope is simply a multiplication in the FPGA before the DAC, so shaping costs nothing in hardware. It matters for two reasons: the mask (other users of the spectrum) and the radar's own receiver, where a neighbouring channel's splatter is interference. It also matters for the PA (module 03): a PA switched on with a fast drive edge produces its own transient; a shaped drive edge lets it settle smoothly, though in saturation the PA squares the edges up again, which is why some systems shape the PA's supply or bias as well.

The same pulse with 1 ns (DAC-limited) rectangular edges fails by 19.2 dB at 113 MHz.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive pulse is gated on and off by the DAC at full speed: a rectangle with 1–2 ns edges. Its spectrum falls only at 20 dB per decade: −40 dB is not reached until 63 MHz either side (−40 dB bandwidth 63 MHz for a signal whose useful bandwidth is 1 MHz), and it breaks the mask by 19.2 dB at 113 MHz. Adding an output filter to fix this at RF is difficult at high power (module 04); shaping in the DAC is free.

Step 7

Pulse-to-pulse phase coherence: its effect on MTI and Doppler

You already know

A moving target shifts the echo's frequency by the Doppler frequency fd = 2v/λ (at 10 GHz, λ = 3 cm, so 100 m/s gives 6.7 kHz). The detection page (R3) and the digital receiver module (09) explain Doppler filtering; the synthesiser module (02) explains phase noise.

Definitions

Pulse-to-pulse phase: the phase of each transmitted pulse, compared with the reference. A radar measures Doppler by comparing the echo phase from one pulse to the next; any change of the transmitted phase is read as motion.

Phase jitter σφ: the random, rms pulse-to-pulse variation of the transmitted phase, in degrees. Causes: the reference and synthesiser phase noise integrated over the pulse interval, DDS reset errors, PA supply ripple through AM-PM (Step 9), modulator timing jitter.

Clutter: echoes from the ground, sea or weather. It is huge (here 60 dB above the receiver noise) but nearly still, so it sits at zero Doppler.

Coherent processing interval (CPI): the N pulses analysed together. Doppler filtering: an FFT across the N pulses, giving N Doppler bins; still clutter falls in bin 0, a moving target in another.

MTI (moving target indication) and improvement factor I: the simplest Doppler filter subtracts each pulse from the last, cancelling anything still. I is the clutter rejection it achieves. Phase jitter limits it to I ≈ 1/σφ² (σ in radians): 1° rms → 35.2 dB.

Jitter floor: in a Doppler FFT, jitter spreads a fraction σφ² of the clutter's power evenly over all bins. Relative to the clutter peak, the floor in each bin is σφ² × (Σw²/(Σw)²), where w is the window: for a Hann window over 64 pulses that factor is 1.5/64 = −16.3 dB, so 1° rms gives −35.2 − 16.3 = −51.5 dB.

Sub-clutter visibility (SCV): how far below the clutter a target can be and still be detected. Here we call a target visible if it stands 13 dB above the floor in its bin.

Phase coherence is the exciter's most important property for any radar that looks for moving targets among clutter, and for SAR, which is a coherent sum over thousands of pulses (Part 3). The jitter acts like extra noise injected with the clutter: σφ = 1° is equivalent to a noise 35 dB under the clutter. Because clutter is often 60 dB above the noise, that "jitter noise" is 25 dB above the receiver noise, and the receiver's real sensitivity is wasted in every Doppler bin.

Step 8

Frequency agility and settling

You already know

From the synthesiser module (02): a PLL's loop bandwidth sets how fast it follows a change; a wide loop is fast, a narrow one is slow but cleans up the VCO's noise less far out. A DDS changes frequency in a few clock cycles.

Definitions

Frequency agility: changing the transmit frequency from one pulse (or one burst of pulses) to the next, by a hop of size H. It defeats narrow-band jammers (Step 10), decorrelates sea clutter, and spreads interference.

Settling time ts: the time from the hop command until the frequency stays within a tolerance of the new value, here 1 kHz unless stated.

Dead time: the time between the hop command and the start of the next pulse; whatever error remains then is transmitted.

Acquisition and ringing: for a big hop a PLL first slews (its phase detector is saturated, the frequency moves at a fixed rate, here 20 MHz/µs for a 100 kHz loop and 200 MHz/µs for a 1 MHz loop), then rings into lock with the second-order loop response, decaying as e−ζωnt, ζ = 0.707, ωn = 2π·BW/2.06.

Ping-pong synthesiser: two PLLs, one in use while the other is tuned to the next frequency; a fast switch picks the settled one. Its hop time is the switch's (here 20 ns time constant), as long as the idle PLL had a whole pulse interval to settle.

A residual frequency error Δf at the start of a pulse does three things. (1) It adds a phase drift across the pulse of 360°·Δf·τ: 1 kHz over 10 µs = 3.6°. (2) It looks like Doppler: Δf = 1 kHz at 10 GHz is the Doppler of a target moving at λ·Δf/2 = 15 m/s. If the error differs from pulse to pulse, clutter gets that spread of false Doppler (Step 7). (3) For a chirp it shifts the compressed peak by Δf/K in time; for a 10 µs, 50 MHz chirp, 1 kHz moves it only 3 cm, harmless. So agility is limited by Doppler and coherence, not by range.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive agile exciter uses the radar's existing low-noise integer-N synthesiser (100 kHz loop) and hops 500 MHz between pulses at a PRF of 10 kHz (100 µs between pulses). It needs 61.8 µs to settle to 1 kHz, so 62 % of every pulse interval is lost; if the pulse is sent after only 10 µs, it is transmitted while the frequency is still 300 MHz off and slewing. Widening the loop to make it faster raises the phase noise further from the carrier (module 02), so the radar trades clutter rejection for agility.

How to choose the agile source

(1) Tolerance: from the Doppler budget, e.g. Δf ≤ 1 kHz (15 m/s false velocity at X band) and from Step 7's phase budget. (5) Keep each Doppler CPI at one frequency: agility from pulse to pulse within a CPI destroys Doppler processing; most radars hop from CPI to CPI.

Step 9

Amplitude and phase flatness across the band, and the effect of PA AM-PM

You already know

From Step 3: a phase ripple of a radians with n cycles across a chirp makes paired echoes at ±n range cells, 20·log(a/2) dB down. From the power-amplifier module (03): AM-PM is the change of a PA's output phase with its input level, in degrees per dB, largest near saturation.

Definitions

Amplitude flatness: the variation of the chain's gain across the signal band, in dB peak-to-peak (pp). It comes from filter passband ripple, sinc droop (Step 2), mismatch ripple between components and the PA's gain slope.

Phase flatness: the variation of the chain's phase across the band after removing a straight line (a straight line is just delay), in degrees pp. Equivalent to group-delay ripple.

Ripple cycles n: how many ripple periods fit in the signal band. A double reflection between two mismatches a length ℓ apart gives a ripple period of c/(2ℓ·√εr) in frequency, so longer cables give more cycles.

Why frequency ripple becomes time ripple: in a chirp, frequency and time are tied (f = K·t), so a gain that varies with frequency becomes an envelope that varies with time across the pulse, and a phase that varies with frequency becomes a phase that varies with time: exactly Step 3's ripple.

Echo levels: amplitude ripple of pp dB is a voltage ripple ε = (10pp/20 − 1)/(10pp/20 + 1); echoes at 20·log(ε/2). 0.5 dB pp → ε = 0.0288 → −36.8 dB. Phase ripple of pp degrees has peak a = pp/2: 5° pp → −33.2 dB.

AM-PM conversion and PA drive: if the PA input has an amplitude ripple of A dB, the output gets a phase ripple of k·A degrees, where k is the AM-PM coefficient (°/dB) at the operating point. A compressed PA also squeezes the amplitude ripple: here the output keeps 100 % (linear), 50 % (1 dB compression) or 10 % (saturated) of it.

This is the step where amplifier and filter specs meet the radar's sidelobes. A saturated PA removes most of the amplitude ripple, which seems good, but turns it into phase ripple, which is not removed: a 0.5 dB pp input ripple through a 4 °/dB saturated PA becomes 2° pp of phase ripple and echoes at −41.2 dB, plus −56.8 dB from the 10 % of amplitude ripple left. Echoes from all the ripple sources (filter, PA, cables) add, in the worst case in phase.

ε = (10pp/20 − 1)/(10pp/20 + 1)    echoAM = 20·log(ε/2)    echoPM = 20·log(a/2)    aAM-PM = k·(pp/2)·π/180Amplitude and phase ripple in phase with each other give echoes of combined size √(ε² + a²)/2. Worked: 0.5 dB pp amplitude ripple into a linear PA: −36.8 dB echoes; with Hamming the bench PSLR is −34.6 dB instead of −42.7 dB, because the echoes add to the window's own sidelobes nearby.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive chain is built from parts that each meet "flatness ±0.5 dB" (1 dB pp) on their datasheets, with a long cable between driver and PA that gives 5 ripple cycles across 100 MHz. Three such parts in series can add to 3 dB pp in the worst case: echoes near −22 dB, far above the −42.7 dB the Hamming weighting promised. A datasheet flatness that is fine for a communications link is not fine for a pulse-compression radar.

How to choose the flatness budget

(1) Start from the PSLR target, e.g. −35 dB, and give the exciter-plus-PA chain an echo budget of −41 dB. (2) From echo = 20·log(ε/2): ε ≤ 0.018 → about 0.3 dB pp amplitude; a ≤ 0.018 rad → 2° pp phase. (3) Split between filters, mismatch ripple (keep VSWR low, keep long cables matched at both ends), sinc droop (equalise digitally) and the PA. (4) Choose the PA operating point knowing its AM-PM: near saturation, amplitude flatness at its input becomes phase flatness at its output. (5) Where hardware cannot reach it, measure the chain's response with a loop-back coupler at the antenna port and pre-distort: the DDS or AWG transmits a waveform with the inverse ripple. Calibration typically wins 10–20 dB, until temperature changes the chain.

Step 10

Jammer waveform generation basics: noise, spot and swept

You already know

A radar's receiver passes only its own bandwidth, Br (here 5 MHz, a 0.2 µs pulse). Noise in that bandwidth sets its sensitivity. A jammer is a transmitter whose waveform is designed to raise that noise.

Definitions

J/S: jamming-to-signal ratio: the jammer power inside the radar's receiver bandwidth divided by the target echo power, in dB. Above about 10 dB the target is lost. Here, for illustration, 1 W of jammer power inside the radar's 5 MHz gives J/S = +20 dB at the geometry chosen.

Barrage noise: noise spread over a wide band Bj (here up to 2 GHz) to cover all the radar's possible frequencies. Only the fraction Br/Bj enters the radar.

Spot noise: noise in a narrow band Bj placed on the radar's frequency. Much more of it enters the radar, but the jammer must know the frequency, and a frequency-agile radar (Step 8) escapes it.

Swept spot: a spot of width Bj swept across a span S. It hits the radar only a fraction (Bj + Br)/S of the time, but very hard when it does.

PAPR: peak-to-average power ratio. Gaussian noise has occasional peaks far above its average; to pass them a PA must run its average power PAPR dB below saturation. With a 100 W PA and a 10 dB PAPR, the average noise power is 10 W.

Clipping: limiting the noise peaks digitally to a lower PAPR, so more average power fits through the PA. The clipped-off part becomes distortion: spectrum outside Bj and a less noise-like amplitude. Distortion relative to the noise: 10 dB PAPR → −57.0 dB; 6 dB → −27.5 dB; 3 dB → −16.7 dB.

A jammer exciter is the same hardware as a radar exciter, DDS, DAC, upconverter, used to make deliberately "bad" waveforms well. Noise is generated digitally (random numbers filtered to Bj) and played through a DAC; spot noise can also be a VCO frequency-modulated by noise; a swept spot is a DDS whose FTW ramps. The purity questions of Steps 2–9 turn into "where does my power go": every watt outside the radar's bandwidth, or outside the time the radar is listening, is wasted.

J/S = J/S1 W + 10·log Pavg + 10·log(min(1, Br/Bj))    Pavg = Psat/10PAPR/10    swept duty = (Bj + Br)/SWorked: 100 W PA, PAPR 10 dB → 10 W average. Barrage over 1 GHz: 5/1000 → −23 dB → J/S = 20 + 10 − 23 = +7 dB. Spot over 10 MHz: −3 dB → +27 dB. Swept 10 MHz spot over 1 GHz: +27 dB for 1.5 % of the time, +8.8 dB averaged.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive jammer spreads its noise over the whole band the radar might use, 1 GHz, because it does not know the radar's frequency, and passes full Gaussian noise (10 dB PAPR) through its 100 W PA. Only 10 W average leaves the PA, only 0.5 % of it falls in the radar's 5 MHz, and J/S is +7 dB: below the 10 dB needed. Concentrating the power (spot, or tracking the radar's frequency) and reducing the PAPR are the two levers.

(1) If the radar's frequency is known and stable, spot noise gives the most J/S. (2) If it hops within a band, barrage covers it at a cost of 10·log(Bj/Br). (3) Swept spot works against radars that integrate over several pulses or have slow automatic gain control, because each hit saturates them. (4) Clip to about 6–8 dB PAPR: 2.5 times the power for distortion about 27 dB down at 6 dB PAPR. (5) Against coherent, pulse-compression radars, noise is inefficient: the radar's processing gain (TB, Doppler integration) works against noise but not against a copy of its own pulse. That copy is what a DRFM (digital radio frequency memory) makes: it records the radar's pulse and replays it, delayed and Doppler-shifted, as false targets.
