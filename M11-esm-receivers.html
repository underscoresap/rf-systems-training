Wideband ESM receivers: crystal video, superhet, IFM and channelisers

A radar receiver knows the frequency, the pulse width and the timing of the echo it is waiting for, because it sent the pulse. An ESM receiver knows none of these. It must watch a band many gigahertz wide, catch pulses from emitters it has never met, and write down for every pulse when it arrived, how long it lasted, its frequency, its strength and its direction. This page builds the receiver architectures that do this, one at a time, starting from what has to be measured and why: the probability of catching an emitter at all, the crystal video receiver, the scanning superhet, the instantaneous frequency measurement (IFM) receiver, analogue and digital channelisers, and the compressive receiver. It then compares them on one example, shows the penalty paid for watching a wide band (the kTB penalty), what happens when a strong and a weak signal arrive together, and what each architecture hands on to signal identification. Every term is defined before it is used, every step has worked numbers, and every datasheet line is traced from your design choice to the system effect.

Step 0

What an ESM receiver must measure: the pulse descriptor word

You already know

A pulse radar transmits short bursts of RF (pulses) at a carrier frequency, repeated at a fixed or patterned interval. A receiver turns RF power into a voltage and compares it with a threshold to decide whether a signal is present. The noise floor of any receiver is kTB plus its noise figure: −174 dBm/Hz + NF + 10·log B (see the LNA and receiver noise module).

Definitions

ESM (electronic support measures): passive listening equipment that detects, measures and identifies other people's radar and communication signals. It never transmits. The radar warning receiver (RWR) in an aircraft is a small, fast ESM; a ship or ground ESM is larger and more sensitive.

Emitter: any transmitter the ESM hears, here usually a radar.

Pulse descriptor word (PDW): the record the receiver writes for each detected pulse, a row of numbers. Every later stage works only from PDWs, never from the RF.

TOA (time of arrival): the time the pulse's leading edge crossed the detection threshold, stamped by a clock, typically with 1 to 50 ns resolution.

PW (pulse width): the time the pulse stayed above the threshold (or above half its peak).

RF (carrier frequency): the frequency of the RF inside the pulse.

PA (pulse amplitude): the received power of the pulse, in dBm.

AOA (angle of arrival): the direction the pulse came from, measured by comparing amplitudes or phases between several antennas (this belongs to the antenna and DF pages; on this page AOA is just one more field the receiver must fill).

PRI (pulse repetition interval): the time between successive pulses of one emitter; PRF = 1/PRI. PRI is not measured on one pulse: it is the difference of two TOAs.

Deinterleaving: sorting the mixed stream of PDWs from all emitters back into one list per emitter. It is done by clustering PDWs with similar RF, PW and AOA, then checking that their TOAs form a repeating PRI.

Picture a ship at sea. In the 2–18 GHz band there are perhaps twenty radars within range: navigation radars, air search radars, fire control radars, aircraft radars. Each sends 300 to 100 000 pulses per second, so the receiver may see a million pulses per second, all mixed together. A pulse is not labelled with its owner. The only way to know which radar sent it is to measure everything that can be measured about it, write it into a PDW, and let a processor sort the PDWs by similarity. That makes the receiver's job very specific: it is not to "hear" signals, it is to produce accurate PDWs, one per pulse, with no pulse lost, no pulse doubled and no pulse invented.

Each field exists because it separates emitters. Frequency is the strongest separator, because two radars a few tens of MHz apart almost never share AOA and PRI as well. Direction (AOA) is the second, because emitters on different bearings cannot be confused. PW separates a 0.2 µs fire-control pulse from a 1 µs search pulse. TOA does not separate anything on one pulse, but the differences of TOAs give the PRI, which is the fingerprint used later to name the radar. Amplitude tells range and the emitter's antenna scan (the pulses rise and fall as the beam sweeps past).

A sorter that splits at the midpoint puts a pulse on the wrong side when its error exceeds Δf/2. A PRI is the difference of two TOAs, each with error σTOA, so its error is √2 times larger.

Worked example: the reference scenario used on every step

Throughout this page the receiver watches 2–18 GHz (a 16 GHz span) with a front end of noise figure 10 dB and needs 15 dB signal-to-noise ratio to detect a pulse reliably. Three radars are in the scene. Radar A: 9.40 GHz, PW 1 µs, PRI 1 ms, a rotating search radar whose main beam sweeps over the ESM for 20 ms every 4 s. Radar B: 15 MHz above A, PW 0.5 µs, PRI 370 µs. Radar C: 15 MHz above B, PW 0.2 µs, PRI about 250 µs with ±10 % jitter. If the receiver measures frequency with σf = 3 MHz rms, A and B are 15/3 = 5 standard deviations apart and the midpoint split misassigns Q(2.5) = 0.62 % of pulses. With σf = 6 MHz (a typical channelised receiver, Step 5) it is Q(1.25) = 10.6 %. For PRI: if two emitters on the same frequency have PRIs 0.5 µs apart and the TOA clock has σTOA = 50 ns, σPRI = 71 ns and Q(0.25/0.071) = Q(3.54) = 0.02 %.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive receiver detects pulses but does not measure frequency at all (Step 2 shows such a receiver). Its PDWs carry TOA, PW and amplitude only. Radars A, B and C then differ only in PW and PRI. A sorter working on TOA alone must try every pair of pulses as a candidate PRI: with 3 emitters and 5 000 pulses per second in total there are 12.5 million pulse pairs per second to test, and a jittered PRI like C's hardly shows up at all. Measuring frequency even coarsely, for example to ±10 MHz, splits the stream into piles before any PRI search, and the number of pairs drops by the square of the number of piles.

How to choose the accuracy targets

(1) Start from the closest emitter spacing you must resolve, Δf. For a 1 % sorting error you need Δf/(2σf) ≥ 2.33, so σf ≤ Δf/4.65; for 15 MHz spacing that is 3.2 MHz rms. (2) Do the same for PRI: the smallest PRI difference ΔPRI between modes you must separate gives σTOA ≤ ΔPRI/(4.65·√2). For 0.5 µs, σTOA ≤ 76 ns. (3) The shortest pulse you must report sets the video rise time, and so the video bandwidth (Step 2). (4) Every field must survive simultaneous pulses: a field measured perfectly on a lone pulse but corrupted when two pulses overlap is wrong exactly when the scene is dense (Steps 4, 8).

Step 1

Probability of intercept and scan strategy

You already know

A receiver that is narrower than the band it must cover has to be retuned, step by step, to look at all of it. A narrower receiver has a lower noise floor: each halving of bandwidth gains 3 dB.

Definitions

Instantaneous bandwidth (IBW): the part of the band the receiver can see at one moment.

Dwell time Td: how long a scanning receiver stays on one frequency step.

Number of steps M: how many dwells are needed to cover the band, M = ⌈span / IBW⌉ (⌈ ⌉ means round up).

Revisit time Tr: the time before the receiver comes back to the same step, Tr = M·Td.

Illumination time Ton: how long the emitter's main beam points at the ESM on each antenna rotation. A search radar rotating every Ts = 4 s with a 1.8° beam illuminates for Ts·1.8/360 = 20 ms.

Probability of intercept (POI): the probability that the receiver is looking at the emitter's frequency while the emitter's beam is on it, long enough to collect the pulses it needs.

Pulses needed N: the number of pulses the receiver needs in one look to confirm a detection and measure the PRI; at least 2 (one PRI), usually more.

Two windows have to overlap: the emitter's beam is on the ESM for Ton, once every Ts; the receiver is on the emitter's frequency for Td, once every Tr. The overlap must be at least N·PRI long, or the receiver collects fewer than N pulses. If the two timings are not synchronised (they never are), the start of the illumination falls at a random point of the receiver's revisit cycle. The illumination start times that give enough overlap form a window of length Ton + Td − 2·N·PRI. Its fraction of Tr is the POI per emitter scan.

M = ⌈span / IBW⌉    Tr = M · Td    p = min(1, (Ton + Td − 2·N·PRI) / Tr)    Pcum(t) = 1 − (1 − p)⌊t/Ts⌋Valid when N·PRI ≤ Td and N·PRI ≤ Ton; otherwise p = 0 because no single look can collect N pulses. Pcum assumes each emitter scan is an independent try, true when Tr and Ts are not in a simple ratio. A receiver with IBW ≥ span (M = 1) is always looking: p = 1 whenever Ton ≥ N·PRI.

Worked example: radar A, N = 2 pulses, dwell 4 ms

A narrow superhet with a 20 MHz IBW needs M = 16 000/20 = 800 steps. With a 4 ms dwell the revisit time is 800 × 4 ms = 3.2 s. The capture window is 20 + 4 − 2 × 2 = 20 ms, so p = 20/3 200 = 0.63 % per emitter scan. Radar A scans every 4 s, so in 60 s there are 15 tries: Pcum = 1 − (1 − 0.0063)15 = 9 %. A receiver split into 8 bands of 2 GHz needs only 8 steps: Tr = 32 ms, p = 20/32 = 63 %, and after 60 s Pcum is 100 % to the shown precision.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive design picks the narrowest, most sensitive receiver and scans it. It is excellent on paper (−76 dBm at 20 MHz against −47 dBm wide open) and catches radar A in only 9 % of one-minute watches. Worse, a dwell shorter than the PRI may contain no pulse at all: at a 0.5 ms dwell and a 1 ms PRI the receiver sees one pulse at most half the time, and with N = 2 it can never collect two. Short dwells do not help; they just stop the receiver seeing pulse trains.

How to choose the IBW, the dwell and the scan

(1) The dwell must hold the pulses you need: Td ≥ N·PRImax for the longest PRI you care about; for 2 pulses of a 1 ms PRI, at least 2 ms, plus tuning and settling time. (2) The revisit must be short against the illumination: aim for Tr ≤ Ton + Td − 2N·PRI for 100 % POI per scan; with 20 ms beams and a 4 ms dwell, Tr ≤ 20 ms means M ≤ 5 steps. (3) That M fixes the IBW: span/M. For 16 GHz and M = 5, IBW ≥ 3.2 GHz. (4) If the sensitivity at that IBW is not enough, you cannot fix it by scanning: you need a receiver with a wide IBW and narrow detection cells (Steps 5, 6). (5) Scan schedules in real systems are not uniform: they visit the bands where threat radars live more often, and they avoid scan periods that are simple multiples of known radar rotation periods.

Step 2

The crystal video receiver: tangential sensitivity and video bandwidth

You already know

A Schottky or tunnel diode biased near zero is a square-law detector at low power: its output voltage is proportional to input power. An RC low-pass after it removes the RF and keeps the envelope (the "video"). A single-pole low-pass of bandwidth B has a 10–90 % rise time of 0.35/B.

Definitions

Crystal video receiver: a band-pass filter covering the whole band (or a few wide sub-bands), an optional RF amplifier, a detector diode, a video amplifier and a threshold comparator. "Crystal" is the old name for the detector diode. There is no mixer, no local oscillator and no frequency measurement.

Video bandwidth Bv: the bandwidth of the amplifier after the detector. It sets how fast the video follows the pulse edges and how much noise reaches the comparator.

RF bandwidth BR: the bandwidth of the RF path before the detector, here 16 GHz.

Tangential sensitivity (TSS): the input pulse power at which, on an oscilloscope, the bottom of the noise on top of the pulse is level with the top of the noise on the baseline: the pulse just "touches" the noise. It corresponds to a video signal-to-noise ratio of about 8 dB. It is the traditional figure of merit of a detector or crystal video receiver.

Detector-limited: no (or little) RF gain before the diode, so the diode's own noise and the video amplifier noise dominate.

Noise-limited: enough RF gain that amplified RF noise, beating with itself in the square-law diode, dominates.

The crystal video receiver is the simplest wideband receiver and has 100 % POI in its band: it never tunes, so it never looks away. It measures TOA, PW and amplitude very well, because its video path can be fast. It does not measure frequency (except which sub-band the pulse came through), and when two pulses overlap it reports their sum as one pulse.

Its sensitivity has two regimes. Without an RF amplifier the detector limits: a good zero-bias Schottky gives about −52 dBm TSS at 2 MHz video bandwidth, worsening as √Bv (5 dB per decade) because the output voltage is proportional to power. With RF gain in front, amplified noise takes over. In the square-law diode, noise beats with itself across the whole RF bandwidth and the video filter keeps only the part within Bv. The result, in the form given by Tsui, depends on √(BR·Bv), not on BR: a wide RF bandwidth costs only 5 dB per decade in a crystal video receiver, not 10.

TSSnoise ≈ −114 + NF + 10·log(6.31·Bv + 2.5·√(2·BR·Bv − Bv²)) dBm    TSSdet ≈ −52 + 5·log(Bv/2) − GRF dBm    tr = 0.35 / BvB in MHz; −114 dBm is kT in 1 MHz. Working sensitivity is taken here as TSS + 4 dB, a common rule for reliable detection (video SNR about 16 dB). A single-pole video filter reaches 1 − e−2π·Bv·PW of the full pulse height.

Worked example: 2–18 GHz, Bv = 10 MHz

Without a preamplifier: TSSdet = −52 + 5·log 5 = −48.5 dBm. With a 30 dB, NF 10 dB preamplifier: TSSdet drops to −78.5 dBm referred to the input and TSSnoise = −114 + 10 + 10·log(63 + 2.5·√319 900) = −114 + 10 + 31.7 = −72.3 dBm; combined, −72.2 dBm, working sensitivity −68.2 dBm. Adding 10 dB more gain gains only 0.1 dB: the receiver is now noise-limited. The video rise time is 0.35/10 MHz = 35 ns, so a 1 µs pulse is measured cleanly, and a 50 ns pulse reaches only 1 − e−π = 96 % of its height.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive improvement is to narrow the video bandwidth for sensitivity. Going from 10 MHz to 1 MHz gains about 5 dB of TSS, but the rise time becomes 350 ns. A 100 ns pulse now reaches only 1 − e−0.63 = 47 % of its height, which costs another 3.3 dB at the threshold (since the video is proportional to power), its measured PW comes out as about 0.17 µs instead of 0.1 µs, and its TOA is late by about 40 ns: the PW field can no longer tell a 0.1 µs pulse from a 0.15 µs one. The opposite naive choice, no preamplifier "to keep it simple", leaves the receiver 24 dB less sensitive, which is about 16 times less detection range.

How to choose the video bandwidth and the RF gain

(1) Choose Bv from the shortest pulse: for 90 % height, 2π·Bv·PW ≥ 2.3, so Bv ≥ 0.37/PWmin; for 50 ns, about 7 MHz, and for good PW measurement roughly twice that. (2) Add RF gain until the receiver is noise-limited: past that point (about 30 dB here) more gain buys nothing and only reduces the largest signal the detector can take before it leaves square law. (3) If more sensitivity is needed, narrow BR by splitting the band (four 4 GHz sub-bands gain 3 dB, because the noise term goes as √BR), not Bv. (4) Use a logarithmic video amplifier when the signal range is large: a square-law diode with linear video handles only about 30–40 dB before it compresses.

Step 3

The scanning superheterodyne receiver

You already know

A superhet mixes the input with a local oscillator (LO) to a fixed intermediate frequency (IF), where a fixed filter sets the bandwidth. Tuning the LO tunes the receiver. Mixers, images and LO spurs are covered in the mixers module, the IF chain in the IF and AGC module.

Definitions

Scanning superhet: a superhet whose LO is stepped or swept across the band, so the narrow IF window moves over the band in time.

IF bandwidth B: the width of the IF filter; it is the receiver's IBW and its detection bandwidth.

Sweep time Tsw: the time to sweep the whole 16 GHz; the sweep rate is μ = span / Tsw in Hz/s.

Time in pass band: how long a fixed-frequency emitter stays inside the moving window, B/μ.

Filter response time: a filter of bandwidth B needs about 1/B to build up its output. If the signal passes through the window faster than that, the output never reaches full height. The condition for the filter to keep up is B/μ ≥ 1/B, that is B²/μ ≥ 1.

The superhet gives what the crystal video receiver lacks: sensitivity (a narrow detection bandwidth) and a frequency measurement (the LO setting tells you where the signal is, to within the IF bandwidth). It also rejects everything outside its window, so a strong signal elsewhere in the band does not disturb it. Its weakness is Step 1: it is looking at one narrow window at a time.

A swept superhet has a second trap. A pulsed emitter is not "on" all the time; it is on for 1 µs every 1 ms. When the window passes over the emitter's frequency in a time B/μ, the receiver catches a pulse only if one happens to fall inside that time. If B/μ is shorter than the PRI, it usually catches nothing.

μ = span / Tsw    Tin = B / μ    pulses per pass = Tin / PRI    S = −174 + NF + 10·log B + SNR    σf ≈ B / √12B/√12 is the rms error when the receiver reports the window centre and the signal is anywhere in the window with equal probability. Losses are added when B²/μ < 1 (sweep too fast, first-order loss 20·log(B²/μ)) and when PW·B < 1 (the pulse is shorter than the filter's response time, loss 20·log(PW·B)).

Worked example: 20 MHz IF, sweeping 2–18 GHz

Sweep the 16 GHz in 100 ms: μ = 160 GHz/s, and radar A stays in the 20 MHz window for 20/160 000 s = 125 µs. With a 1 ms PRI that is 0.125 pulses per pass: the receiver sees a pulse on only 12.5 % of passes. Slow the sweep to 1.6 s: the window holds the emitter for 2 ms, two pulses per pass, but the band is revisited only every 1.6 s, so a 20 ms illumination is caught on (20 + 2)/1 600 = 1.4 % of scans. Sensitivity in both cases is −174 + 10 + 73.0 + 15 = −76.0 dBm and the frequency error is 20/√12 = 5.8 MHz rms.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive design sweeps fast to keep the revisit short. At a 10 ms sweep (1 600 GHz/s), a 20 MHz window holds radar A for 12.5 µs: it catches a pulse on 1.25 % of passes. It also misses short pulses for a second reason: a 0.1 µs pulse through a 20 MHz IF has PW·B = 2, fine, but through a 1 MHz IF (chosen "for sensitivity") PW·B = 0.1 and the output is 20 dB down, more than the 13 dB the narrower filter gained.

Step 4

The IFM receiver: measuring frequency with a delay line

You already know

A signal at frequency f delayed by τ is shifted in phase by φ = 2π·f·τ. A phase can be measured by multiplying two signals and low-pass filtering: the product of the signal and its delayed copy has a DC part proportional to cos φ. Phase is only known modulo 360°.

Definitions

IFM (instantaneous frequency measurement) receiver: a wide-open receiver that measures the frequency of each pulse from the phase shift through a delay line, within a fraction of a microsecond, without any filter bank or tuning.

Delay-line discriminator (phase correlator): the signal is split; one half passes a delay line τ; a correlator (hybrids and four detector diodes) produces two video outputs proportional to cos(2πfτ) and sin(2πfτ). Their ratio gives the angle φ, and f = φ/(2πτ).

Unambiguous bandwidth: φ repeats every 360°, so one line measures f without ambiguity only over 1/τ. For 16 GHz, τ ≤ 62.5 ps.

Multi-line IFM: several lines in ratio n (for example 62.5 ps, 250 ps, 1 ns, 4 ns for n = 4). The shortest gives a coarse, unambiguous frequency; each longer one is n times finer but ambiguous, and the coarser answer picks which of its n possible values is right.

Limiting amplifier: an amplifier driven into hard saturation so its output amplitude is constant; it makes the correlator outputs independent of signal level. With two signals, it strengthens the stronger and suppresses the weaker (the capture effect, about 6 dB extra suppression of a small signal).

The IFM answers the crystal video receiver's missing field. It is wide open over 2–18 GHz, so POI is 100 %, and it measures frequency on every pulse to a few MHz. Accuracy comes from the longest delay: σf = σφ/(2πτL). A 5° phase error on the 62.5 ps line alone is (5/360)/62.5 ps = 222 MHz; on a 4 ns line it is 3.5 MHz. The phase error has two parts: noise, which falls as the signal rises, and hardware error (line dispersion, correlator imbalance, temperature drift), which does not.

The correlator is a square-law device over the full 16 GHz, like the crystal video receiver. Its noise is integrated down by the video filter, so the video SNR is SNRRF²·(BR/Bv)/(1 + 2·SNRRF): large even when the RF SNR in 16 GHz is near 0 dB.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive IFM uses one long line for accuracy: 4 ns gives 2.45 MHz, but its unambiguous range is 1/4 ns = 250 MHz, so a pulse at 9.40 GHz and one at 9.65 GHz read the same. The other naive IFM uses one short line for the full band: unambiguous over 16 GHz but σf = (3.53/360)/62.5 ps = 157 MHz, useless for sorting. Simultaneous signals: why the IFM fails

The correlator multiplies the input by its own delayed copy. With two signals present, a strong one (amplitude 1) and a weak one (amplitude r), the product has four terms: the strong signal with itself (angle φ1), the weak with itself (angle φ2, size r²), and two cross terms of size r that rotate at the difference frequency Δf. The video filter removes the cross terms only if Δf is well above Bv. What is left is the sum of two phasors, and its angle is neither φ1 nor φ2. The error is largest when the two phasors are 90° apart: arcsin(r²) in phase.

Worked example on a 1 ns line: a weak signal 10 dB below the strong one. Without a limiter r² = 0.1, worst phase error arcsin 0.1 = 5.7°, which is 15.9 MHz on 1 ns. A hard limiter before the correlator suppresses the weak signal further, to about r/(2 − r) = 0.19 (−14.5 dB), so r² = 0.035 and the worst error drops to 5.6 MHz. When the two are within a few dB of each other, the reported frequency is somewhere between them, or anywhere: the IFM silently produces a wrong PDW.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive view is that an IFM "reports the strongest signal". It does, only when the weaker one is more than about 10–15 dB down and far enough away in frequency for the video filter to remove the beat. With radar A at −50 dBm and a pulse from radar C overlapping at −58 dBm, 30 MHz away, the beat at 30 MHz is only partly removed by a 10 MHz video filter, and A's PDW frequency wobbles by several MHz during the overlap. In a dense scene with 1 µs pulses at 200 000 per second, 1 − e−0.4 = 33 % of pulses overlap another (Step 9 of the filters module): a third of the IFM's PDWs are at risk.

Step 5

Channelised receivers: analogue filter banks and digital polyphase channelisers

You already know

A band-pass filter passes its band and rejects the rest, with a skirt whose steepness depends on its order and family (see the filters module). An FFT of N samples splits a sampled band into N frequency bins. An ADC turns a band of up to half its sample rate into numbers (see the ADC module); a DDC and decimating filters move and narrow it (see the digital receiver module).

Definitions

Channelised receiver: a bank of contiguous filters (channels) covering the IBW side by side, each with its own detector. Every channel is watched all the time, so POI is 100 % and each detection bandwidth is narrow.

Channel spacing Δ: the frequency step between channel centres, here 20 MHz.

Crossover level: the response of two neighbouring channels at the frequency halfway between them. At −3 dB, a signal there loses 3 dB in both; deeper crossovers leave holes; shallower ones overlap more.

Channel overlap: channels wider than their spacing, so the responses overlap and a signal anywhere is well inside at least one channel. Overlap is what makes a signal appear in two channels at once.

Splitting: one signal reported in two (or more) adjacent channels. It happens near a crossover, and for short pulses whose spectrum (main lobe 2/PW wide) is wider than a channel.

Digital channeliser: an ADC followed by a digital filter bank that forms all channels at once. The efficient form is the polyphase filter bank (PFB): one low-pass prototype filter of P·N taps, split into N branches of P taps (the "polyphase" parts), followed by an N-point FFT. It costs little more than the FFT, but each channel has the shape of the prototype filter instead of the FFT's sinc.

Taps per channel P: the length of each polyphase branch. P = 1 is a plain FFT; P = 4 to 16 gives steep channel skirts.

Oversampled PFB: channel outputs sampled at twice the channel spacing (2× oversampled) so channels can overlap without aliasing; a critically sampled bank (1×) needs channel skirts that stop within one spacing.

The channeliser is the direct answer to the dilemma of Step 1: many narrow receivers side by side give both 100 % POI and the sensitivity of a narrow cell. An analogue channelised receiver for 2–18 GHz with 20 MHz channels would need 800 filters, detectors and comparators; real analogue designs use two levels (for example 16 sub-bands of 1 GHz, each down-converted to a common IF and split into 50 channels). A digital channeliser does the second level in an FPGA: a 1 GHz IF sampled at about 2.5 GS/s gives 50 channels of 20 MHz, all formed by one PFB.

Frequency accuracy is set by the channel width if the receiver reports only "which channel": Δ/√12 = 20/√12 = 5.8 MHz rms. A digital channeliser can do much better by measuring the phase change of the channel output from sample to sample (instantaneous frequency within the channel), reaching about 1 MHz rms on strong pulses. Two signals in different channels are reported separately; two in the same channel are not.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive digital channeliser is a plain FFT (P = 1). Its channels are sinc-shaped: the crossover is −3.9 dB (fixed, not chosen), the response 1.5 channels away is only −13.4 dB and the sidelobes stay around −18 dB several channels out. A −30 dBm emitter then shows up at −48 dBm in channels 40–100 MHz away, far above the −76 dBm sensitivity: one strong radar lights up many channels and the PDW stream fills with false emitters. With P = 8 the response 1.5 channels away is below −100 dB, and what remains in distant channels is the pulse's own spectrum splatter, not the filter.

Step 6

The compressive (microscan) receiver

You already know

A chirp is a signal whose frequency sweeps linearly in time. A dispersive delay line (DDL) delays each frequency by a different amount; matched to a chirp, it compresses it into a short pulse (pulse compression, see the filters module Step 4 and the radar pulse page). Weighting (a window) lowers sidelobes at the cost of a wider main lobe.

Definitions

Compressive receiver: a receiver whose LO sweeps (chirps) rapidly across an IBW; the mixer output goes through a DDL whose delay-versus-frequency slope is the opposite of the sweep. Any input frequency becomes a chirp at the mixer output, and the DDL compresses it into a short pulse whose time of exit tells the input frequency. It is a real-time analogue Fourier transform.

Dispersion time Td: the difference in delay across the DDL's bandwidth; also the time the receiver needs to analyse one sweep.

DDL bandwidth B: the frequency range the DDL disperses, here 500 MHz; it is the compressive receiver's IBW.

Time-bandwidth product Td·B: the number of independent frequency cells across B, and the compression ratio.

Frequency resolution: about 1/Td for uniform weighting (−3 dB width 0.89/Td), wider with weighting; for a pulse shorter than Td, about 1/PW.

The compressive receiver sits between the scanning superhet and the channeliser. Like a superhet it sweeps, but so fast (500 MHz in 1 µs, that is 500 THz/s, far beyond the B²/μ limit of an ordinary IF filter) that every frequency in the 500 MHz is visited once per microsecond: for pulses longer than the sweep, POI is effectively 100 %. Like a channeliser, it resolves many signals at once: a 1 µs, 500 MHz DDL gives 500 cells of about 1 MHz. Its output is a stream of 2 ns compressed pulses, one per signal per sweep, whose timing is the frequency: the hard part is the very fast logarithmic video and digitiser needed to read them.

Step 7

Sensitivity versus instantaneous bandwidth: the kTB penalty

You already know

Thermal noise power is kTB: −174 dBm in 1 Hz at 290 K, so −114 dBm in 1 MHz and −72 dBm in 16 GHz. A receiver adds its noise figure on top, and needs a certain SNR to detect. In free space, received power falls as 20·log(4πR/λ).

Definitions

Detection bandwidth (noise bandwidth of a cell): the bandwidth of the noise that competes with the signal at the decision point: the IF filter of a superhet, one channel of a channeliser, one cell of a compressive receiver. It need not equal the IBW.

kTB penalty: the loss of sensitivity from detecting in a wide bandwidth: 10·log(B1/B2) dB between two bandwidths, 10 dB per decade, 3 dB per doubling.

ERP (effective radiated power): the emitter's transmit power plus its antenna gain toward the ESM, in dBm. A 1 MW (90 dBm) peak radar with a 30 dBi antenna has 120 dBm ERP in the main beam; its sidelobes might be 30–40 dB lower.

ESM range advantage: the ESM receives the radar's signal one way (∝ 1/R²), while the radar receives its echo two ways (∝ 1/R⁴), so an ESM can usually hear a radar well beyond the range at which the radar sees it.

Every architecture on this page can be placed on one line: sensitivity = −174 + NF + 10·log Bcell + SNR. What distinguishes them is Bcell: 16 GHz for a wideband detector, 20 MHz for a superhet or channel, 1.36 MHz for a compressive cell. Two do not quite fit the line: the crystal video receiver and the IFM, which are square-law over 16 GHz but integrate in a 10 MHz video bandwidth, so they lie about 20 dB better than "−174 + NF + 10·log 16 GHz + 15" would suggest, though still 8–10 dB worse than a 20 MHz channel.

Sensitivity converts to range through the one-way link: Pr = ERP + GESM − 20·log(4πR/λ). Setting Pr equal to the sensitivity S gives R = (λ/4π)·10(ERP + G − S)/20. Each 6 dB of sensitivity doubles the range, so each factor of 4 in bandwidth halves it.

A compressive cell at −87.7 dBm reaches about 1 940 km in free space, in practice limited by the horizon.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive comparison quotes every receiver's sensitivity at the same SNR and forgets the detection bandwidth. A "−70 dBm IFM" and a "−76 dBm channeliser" look 6 dB apart; but the channeliser holds that sensitivity for many signals at once, and the compressive receiver at −88 dBm is 12 dB better again in each 1.3 MHz cell. The other naive error is to narrow the cell for sensitivity below what the pulses allow: a 1 MHz cell is 13 dB better than 20 MHz on paper but loses 20 dB on a 0.1 µs pulse (Step 3).

Step 8

Dynamic range: a strong and a weak signal at the same time

You already know

Any amplifier, mixer or ADC is non-linear at high drive. Two tones f1 and f2 produce third-order products at 2f1 − f2 and 2f2 − f1, rising 3 dB for each 1 dB of input. IIP3 is the input level where those products would equal the tones. AGC and switched attenuators move the range but do not widen it for signals that arrive together (see the IF and AGC module).

Definitions

Instantaneous dynamic range (IDR): the ratio between the strongest and the weakest signals the receiver handles correctly at the same moment.

Two-tone spur-free dynamic range (SFDR): the range over which two equal strong signals produce third-order products below the noise floor: ⅔·(IIP3 − noise floor).

Ghost (false signal): a spur, intermodulation product or filter leakage that the receiver reports as an emitter.

Skirt leakage: the part of a strong signal that a channel or IF filter tuned elsewhere still passes. It can hide a weak signal in that channel, or be reported as one.

ADC SFDR: the level of the ADC's largest spur below a full-scale tone, in dBc; spurs from a strong signal land in other channels of a digital channeliser.

A ship's own radars and a nearby warship can put −10 dBm into the ESM while a distant aircraft radar arrives at −75 dBm: 65 dB apart, at the same moment, perhaps 60 MHz apart. Each architecture fails this case in its own way. The crystal video receiver and the IFM cannot separate the two at all: one sums them, the other reports the strong one (with an error). The frequency-resolving receivers separate them only as well as their filter skirts, sidelobes and linearity allow: the weak signal must be above the strong signal's skirt at its frequency, and the strong signals' intermodulation must stay below the threshold or it is reported as an emitter that does not exist.

Worked example: −10 dBm and −20 dBm, 60 MHz apart, IIP3 +10 dBm, 20 MHz channels

The noise floor of a 20 MHz cell is −174 + 10 + 73 = −91 dBm, so SFDR = ⅔ × (10 + 91) = 67.3 dB. The two signals are well within the channeliser's separation (60 MHz is three channels; a 5-pole Butterworth channel is 78 dB down there). But their IM3 product at f1 − 60 MHz is 2(−10) + (−20) − 20 = −60 dBm, 16 dB above the −76 dBm sensitivity: the receiver reports a third emitter that does not exist. Drop the strong signal to −30 dBm with a weak one at −70 dBm: IM3 is −150 dBm, no ghost, and the weak signal is detected 6 dB above sensitivity.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive design gives the front end as much gain as possible for sensitivity. Every 10 dB of extra gain ahead of a fixed mixer or ADC lowers the input-referred IIP3 by 10 dB and the SFDR by 6.7 dB. With IIP3 at 0 dBm instead of +10 dBm, two −30 dBm signals already produce −90 dBm products, harmless; but two −15 dBm signals produce −45 dBm ghosts. In a digital channeliser the same over-drive pushes the ADC past full scale and every channel fills with spurs at once.

Step 9

How each receiver feeds signal identification

You already know

From Step 0: identification works on PDWs only. It sorts them into emitters (deinterleaving) by frequency, PW and AOA, then measures each emitter's PRI pattern and scan and compares them with a library. Part 5 of this series covers that processing.

Definitions

Missed pulse: a pulse that arrived but produced no PDW (too weak, or the receiver was looking elsewhere).

Merged pulse: two overlapping pulses reported as one PDW; the weaker one is lost and the PDW's fields may be wrong.

Corrupted PDW: a PDW whose frequency (or other field) is wrong because of an overlap.

Duplicate PDW: one pulse reported twice, for example in two adjacent channels.

Background pulses: pulses from all the other emitters in the band, which the sorter must separate from the ones of interest.

The sorter downstream can only be as good as the PDW stream it receives. Each architecture delivers a different kind of stream: the crystal video receiver gives excellent TOA and PW for every pulse but no frequency; the IFM gives a frequency for every pulse but corrupts it when pulses overlap; the scanning superhet gives accurate but intermittent PDWs; the channeliser gives frequency to a channel and handles overlaps, but splits pulses at the crossovers; the compressive receiver gives fine frequency but needs pulses at least as long as its sweep. Real ESM combine two or more: for example, a crystal video and IFM front end for fast, 100 %-POI warning, plus a digital channeliser for dense scenes and a superhet cued for fine analysis.

Worked example: radars A, B, C 15 MHz apart, 20 ms of PDWs

In 20 ms radar A sends 20 pulses, B about 54 and C about 80; add 100 000 background pulses per second (2 000 in 20 ms) spread over the 300 MHz shown. With a 20 MHz channeliser that reports channel centres, B and C, 15 MHz apart, often land in the same or adjacent channels: some of their PDWs are sorted to the wrong emitter. A digital channeliser with fine frequency (about 1–2 MHz) separates them cleanly. An IFM separates them too, except for the pulses that overlap a background pulse, which carry a wrong frequency.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive view is to judge a receiver by its sensitivity and accuracy on a single pulse. The stream view is different: an IFM with 2.5 MHz accuracy that corrupts 5 % of PDWs in a dense scene sends the sorter 5 % of pulses with random frequencies, each of which looks like a new emitter or pollutes a real one. A channeliser with 5.8 MHz rms but no corrupted PDWs may sort better in that scene.
