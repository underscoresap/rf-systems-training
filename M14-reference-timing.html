Frequency reference, clocks and timing: the oscillator that keeps a coherent system coherent

Every radar, SAR, ESM receiver and jammer has one small oscillator, usually a crystal in a box, from which every other frequency and every clock edge is made. Its accuracy decides where your signal sits in the spectrum. Its short-term stability decides whether pulses can be added up coherently, whether stationary clutter stays in the MTI notch and whether a SAR image focuses. Its sensitivity to vibration can ruin all of that on an aircraft. And the clocks it drives decide how well the system knows when something happened, which is what time-of-arrival and TDOA geolocation measure. This page builds those ideas from first principles: what coherence means, the oscillator types, Allan deviation against phase noise, g-sensitivity, multiplication to microwave LOs, clock distribution, coherence over a processing interval, timing for TOA and TDOA, and synchronising several channels or sites. Every term is defined before it is used, every step has worked numbers, and every choice is traced from your component to the system.

Step 0

One reference drives everything

You already know

A synthesiser makes its output frequency from a reference input, usually 10 MHz or 100 MHz. An ADC needs a sampling clock. A mixer needs an LO. You have probably seen a "10 MHz ref in" connector on the back of test equipment, and you know that two instruments locked to the same 10 MHz agree on frequency exactly.

Definitions

Reference oscillator (master oscillator): the one oscillator in the system whose frequency every other frequency is derived from. Typically a quartz crystal oscillator at 10 MHz or 100 MHz, sometimes steered by a rubidium cell or GPS.

Derived frequency: a frequency made from the reference by multiplying, dividing, phase-locking or synthesising (PLL, DDS). Every derived frequency is an exact ratio of the reference: if the reference is 1 part per million high, every LO, clock and PRF is also 1 part per million high.

Clock: a square-wave version of a derived frequency, used to time digital events: ADC sampling instants, DAC updates, the pulse repetition timer and the counters that time-stamp pulses.

ppm, ppb: parts per million (10−6) and parts per billion (10−9) of the nominal frequency. 1 ppm of 10 GHz is 10 kHz; 1 ppb of 10 GHz is 10 Hz.

The diagram shows a typical coherent radar or receiver. One reference feeds a distribution amplifier. From it, the transmit synthesiser makes the exciter LO and the DDS clock; the receive synthesisers make the first and second down-conversion LOs; a clock synthesiser makes the ADC and DAC sampling clocks; and a timing generator divides it down to make the pulse repetition frequency (PRF), the range gates and the time-stamp counter. An optional GPS receiver or rubidium cell steers the reference over long times.

Because everything is a ratio of one source, the transmitted carrier, the receive LOs and the sampling instants all move together. That shared movement is what makes the system coherent, the subject of Step 1. A component engineer meets this reference in three ways: as the oscillator itself (Steps 2 to 4), as the multiplier chain or PLL that inherits its noise (Step 5, and module 02 for the PLL), and as the clock buffers that carry it to every board (Step 6).

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive design gives each subsystem its own convenient oscillator: a TCXO on the exciter board, another on the receiver board, a crystal on the ADC card. Each one is "accurate to 1 ppm". At 10 GHz, 1 ppm is 10 kHz. The transmitter and the receiver LO can therefore differ by up to 20 kHz, and the difference wanders as each board warms. A stationary building now appears to move at up to 300 m/s (Step 1), the Doppler filters put clutter in the wrong place, and the ADC samples drift against the PRF. The fix costs almost nothing: one reference, distributed. The rest of this page is about how good that one reference must be, and how to carry it without spoiling it.

The reference is 2 ppm high. By how much is a 9.6 GHz LO high, and by how much is a 3.2 kHz PRF high?

Step 1

Coherence from first principles

You already know

A sine wave is A·cos(2πft + φ). Phase is the angle 2πft + φ, and frequency is how fast that angle turns: one full turn (360°) per cycle. Two signals at exactly the same frequency keep a constant phase difference; at slightly different frequencies the difference turns continuously, by 360° every 1/Δf seconds.

Definitions

Coherent system: one in which the phase of the transmitted signal is known when the echo comes back, so that the receiver can measure the echo's phase and compare it from pulse to pulse. In practice: the transmit carrier and the receive LO come from the same reference.

Pulse repetition frequency (PRF) and interval (PRI): the radar sends a pulse every PRI seconds; PRF = 1/PRI. Example: PRF 8 kHz, PRI 125 µs.

Doppler shift fD: a target moving towards the radar at radial speed v shifts the echo by fD = 2v/λ, where λ = c/fc is the wavelength. At 10 GHz, λ = 3.0 cm, so 30 m/s gives 2 kHz.

Pulse-to-pulse phase step: between one pulse and the next, the echo phase of a target with Doppler fD turns by 360°·fD·PRI. A stationary target should give a step of exactly 0°.

Doppler filter bank and MTI notch: an FFT across N pulses sorts echoes by their phase step, that is by Doppler. Stationary clutter (ground, buildings) lands at 0 Hz, where the processor puts a notch (MTI, moving-target indication) to remove it.

Fractional frequency offset y: y = Δf/f. Two references 1 ppm apart have y = 10−6.

Follow one pulse. The transmitter sends a burst of carrier at fTx. It returns after the round-trip delay τd = 2R/c and is mixed with the receive LO at fLO. The phase of the mixer output is the difference of the two phases at the moment of reception. If fTx = fLO exactly, the difference is −2π·f·τd: a constant for a stationary target, the same on every pulse. If the target moves, τd changes a little each pulse and the phase steps, which is Doppler.

Now suppose the transmitter and the receive LO come from different references that differ by Δf = y·fc. The difference phase now also turns by 360°·Δf·PRI every pulse, even for a stationary target. The receiver cannot tell this from Doppler. The stationary target appears to move at v = Δf·λ/2.

If both come from the same reference, Δf = 0 exactly, whatever the reference's own error. A reference that is 1 ppm high makes the transmitter and the LO both 1 ppm high; the difference is still zero. This is the most important idea on the page: coherence needs a common reference, not an accurate one. The reference's accuracy only matters for where the carrier sits in the spectrum and for systems whose transmitter and receiver are apart (Steps 7 to 9).

Δφpulse = 360° · (fD + y·fc) · PRI    vfalse = y·fc · λ/2 = y·c/2With a common reference y = 0 between transmitter and LO. The apparent Doppler is folded into −PRF/2 … +PRF/2 by the pulse sampling (aliasing): only the step modulo 360° is visible.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

Use two free-running TCXOs, one for the exciter and one for the receiver, which happen to be 1 ppm apart. At 10 GHz, Δf = 10 kHz, which on its own would look like 150 m/s. With PRF 8 kHz the phase step per pulse is 360° × 10 000 × 125 µs = 450°, which the processor sees as 90°: stationary clutter appears at +2 kHz, as if moving at 30 m/s. It sits outside the MTI notch, swamps the Doppler bins where real 30 m/s targets would be, and drifts as the TCXOs warm. The alternative extreme is a non-coherent transmitter, such as a magnetron, whose start phase is random on every pulse: then there is no phase step to measure at all.

How to choose the arrangement

(1) Derive the transmitter carrier, every receive LO and the ADC clock from one reference; that sets y = 0 between them and costs only distribution (Step 6). (2) If the transmitter and receiver cannot share a reference (bistatic radar, two-site TDOA, a jammer that repeats a victim's signal), the allowed offset follows from the Doppler error you can tolerate: y ≤ 2·verr/c. For a 1 m/s error that is 6.7 ppb, beyond any TCXO and at the edge of an OCXO; it needs rubidium or GPS discipline (Step 2). (3) Raising the carrier raises Δf in Hz but not the false velocity, which is y·c/2 at every frequency. Raising the PRF reduces folding but does not remove the offset.

Step 2

Oscillator types: accuracy, ageing, temperature and warm-up

You already know

A quartz crystal is a mechanical resonator: a thin plate of quartz that vibrates at a frequency set by its thickness and cut, with Q between 104 and 106. Its frequency changes a little with temperature. Module 02 explains why high Q gives low phase noise.

Definitions

Accuracy (frequency tolerance): how far the frequency is from nominal when delivered or after adjustment, in ppm or ppb.

Temperature stability (tempco): how much the frequency moves as the ambient temperature changes, usually given as ± ppm over the operating range (for example ±0.5 ppm over −40 to +85 °C). On this page it is expressed as an average slope in ppb per °C.

Ageing: the slow, steady drift of frequency over days and years as the crystal mounting relaxes and contaminants move, in ppb per day or ppm per year. It is fastest when new and slows with time.

Warm-up and retrace: warm-up is the time after switch-on until the frequency is within a stated error; retrace is how close it comes back to its old value after being switched off and on.

XO: a bare crystal oscillator, no compensation. TCXO (temperature-compensated): a thermistor network measures temperature and pulls the crystal with a varactor to cancel its curve. OCXO (oven-controlled): an SC-cut crystal held in an oven at its turnover temperature (about 80 °C), where the curve is flat, so ambient changes hardly matter. Rubidium (Rb): an atomic frequency standard; a crystal oscillator is locked to the 6.834 682 611 GHz transition of rubidium-87 atoms, which does not age like quartz. GPSDO (GPS-disciplined oscillator): an OCXO steered by the GPS one-pulse-per-second (1 PPS) timing signal with a slow loop, time constant about 1 000 s. Holdover: a GPSDO that has lost GPS and coasts on its OCXO.

The frequency error of a real oscillator at time t after switch-on is the sum of four parts: the initial offset (accuracy), a warm-up transient that dies away, a temperature term that follows the ambient (here a daily swing), and an ageing term that grows with time. Each oscillator type attacks a different part. A TCXO attacks temperature with compensation, an OCXO with an oven, rubidium attacks ageing with an atomic reference, and a GPSDO attacks all slow terms by steering to GPS, which is ultimately steered to atomic clocks.

y(t) = y0 + ywarm·e−t/τw + kT·ΔT(t) + D·t    Δf = y·fc    time error x(t) = ∫ y dty0 initial offset, kT temperature slope (ppb/°C), D ageing rate (ppb/day). The last formula links this step to Step 8: a frequency offset is a clock that runs fast, so a time error builds up.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

A plain XO costs little and "only" has a 20 ppm tolerance. In a monostatic radar with a common reference this hardly matters for coherence (Step 1), but the carrier at 10 GHz is 200 kHz off nominal and moves another ±40 kHz with a 40 °C daily swing, which can put it outside its frequency allocation and outside a narrow preselector. In a two-site TDOA system it is far worse: 20 ppm is a clock gaining 20 µs every second, and 20 µs of timing error is 6 km of range difference (Step 8). Choosing the oscillator type is choosing which of these errors you can live with.

How to choose the oscillator type

(1) Write down the longest time over which the frequency must hold without correction: one CPI (ms), one SAR aperture (s), one TDOA measurement between synchronisations (s to hours), or the whole mission. (2) Write down the largest frequency error that is allowed over that time: from the spectrum allocation (absolute), from Doppler error y ≤ 2verr/c (separate sites), or from time error x = y·t (Step 8). (3) Pick the cheapest type whose accuracy + temperature + ageing over that time stays inside it. (4) Check warm-up against the required time from switch-on to operation: an OCXO or rubidium may need several minutes, and a GPSDO tens of minutes before it reaches its locked figure. Raising the requirement on accuracy pushes from XO to TCXO to OCXO; raising the hold time without GPS pushes towards rubidium.

Step 3

Short-term stability: Allan deviation against phase noise

You already know

Phase noise L(f), in dBc/Hz at an offset f from the carrier, describes the random wobble of an oscillator's phase seen as noise sidebands. Module 02 builds it from first principles and shows how to integrate it into rms jitter. Step 2 above covered slow, predictable drifts. This step covers the random part in between.

Definitions

Averaging time τ: the length of time over which you measure the average frequency. A counter with a 1 s gate measures the average over τ = 1 s.

Allan deviation σy(τ) (ADEV): measure the average fractional frequency over many consecutive intervals of length τ; take the differences between neighbours; σy2(τ) = ½·mean((ȳk+1 − ȳk)2). It answers: "if I measure the frequency over τ, how much does the next τ-long measurement differ?" Example: σy(1 s) = 5.5×10−13 means successive 1 s averages differ by about 5.5×10−13, that is 5.5 mHz at 10 GHz.

Noise types: the random fluctuations are usually a sum of power laws. In order of importance from short to long times: white PM and flicker PM (phase noise floor and flicker of the amplifier, ADEV falls as 1/τ), white FM (ADEV falls as 1/√τ), flicker FM (ADEV flat: the "flicker floor", the best the oscillator can do), random-walk FM (ADEV rises as √τ: the frequency wanders away).

Phase wander over an interval τ: the rms phase change accumulated over τ, Δφ ≈ 2π·fc·τ·σy(τ); and the time wander Δx ≈ τ·σy(τ).

Phase noise and Allan deviation describe the same random wobble in two languages. Phase noise is the frequency-domain view: how much noise power sits at each offset f. Allan deviation is the time-domain view: how much the frequency changes over each interval τ. A rough dictionary is f ≈ 1/(2πτ): noise at offsets around 1.6 Hz shows up in ADEV around τ = 0.1 s, and noise at 1.6 kHz around τ = 0.1 ms.

Which one to use depends on the time scale of the job. Doppler filters, pulse compression and MTI work over microseconds to milliseconds: their neighbours in frequency are hundreds of hertz to megahertz away, so phase noise is the natural language (module 02, Steps 9 and 10). A SAR aperture, a long coherent integration or a bistatic link works over 0.1 s to 10 s: phase noise below 1 Hz is hard to measure and to specify, so ADEV is the natural language. Beyond about 1 000 s the deterministic terms of Step 2 (temperature, ageing) take over.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive approach reads the "stability" line on a TCXO datasheet, 1×10−10 at 1 s, and concludes it is tiny. At 10 GHz it is about 1 Hz rms of frequency change between successive seconds, and over a 1 s interval the phase wanders by 360° × 1010 × 1 × 1.05×10−10 ≈ 377° (the model gives 1.05×10−10 once random walk is added): more than one whole cycle. A bistatic SAR whose receiver used that TCXO over a 1 s aperture would not focus. An OCXO at 5.5×10−13 wanders 2° over the same second. The reverse mistake is to specify only ADEV at 1 s and forget phase noise: two oscillators with the same σy(1 s) can differ by 20 dB at 1 kHz offset, which is where the Doppler filters live.

How to choose the specification

(1) List the time intervals that matter: the range delay τd (monostatic radar, Step 7), the CPI, the SAR aperture, the time between synchronisations. (2) For intervals shorter than about 10 ms, specify phase noise at the offsets 1/(2π·interval) and above, up to the Doppler bandwidth. (3) For intervals from 10 ms to 1 000 s, specify σy(τ) at those τ, from Δφallowed = 2π·fc·τ·σy: for 10° at 10 GHz over 2 s, σy(2 s) ≤ 1.4×10−12. (4) Beyond 1 000 s, use the Step 2 terms. Choosing a higher-Q, larger crystal lowers the flicker floor; choosing a lower-noise sustaining amplifier lowers white and flicker PM; steering with Rb or GPS removes the random walk at long τ but not below its loop time constant.

Step 4

g-sensitivity: vibration-induced phase noise on moving platforms

You already know

A quartz crystal's frequency depends on the stress in the plate: that is how it is pulled by its mounting and how it ages. Acceleration also stresses the plate. A sine-wave frequency modulation at rate fm with peak deviation Δf has modulation index β = Δf/fm and puts sidebands at ±fm at a level of about 20·log10(β/2) dBc when β is small.

Definitions

g: the acceleration of gravity, 9.81 m/s². Vibration levels are given in g.

g-sensitivity Γ: the fractional frequency change per g of acceleration, in ppb/g (10−9/g). It is a vector: the crystal is most sensitive along one axis. Typical values: 1–2 ppb/g for a standard SC-cut crystal, 0.1 ppb/g for a low-g design, 0.01–0.05 ppb/g with active compensation.

Static g-shift (2g tip-over): turning the oscillator upside down changes the acceleration by 2 g, so the frequency shifts by 2Γ. At Γ = 1 ppb/g, a 100 MHz OCXO moves 0.2 Hz.

Sine vibration: a single tone, amplitude A (g peak) at frequency fv, as from a rotor or propeller. Random vibration: broadband shaking described by its power spectral density G(f) in g²/Hz; its rms level grms is the square root of the area under G(f). A jet-aircraft profile of 0.04 g²/Hz from 15 to 2 000 Hz is 8.9 grms.

Vibration isolator: a spring-and-damper mount with natural frequency fn. Above about 1.4·fn it attenuates vibration; near fn it amplifies it (by about 5× here, damping ratio 0.1).

An acceleration a(t) changes the frequency by Γ·a(t)·ν0. A vibration at fv is therefore a frequency modulation at fv, and it puts phase-noise sidebands exactly at the vibration frequencies: tens of hertz to a few kilohertz from the carrier. That is the region where a static OCXO is at its best and where the radar's Doppler filters sit. On a bench the effect is invisible; on an aircraft it usually dominates the close-in phase noise.

For random vibration each slice of G(f) acts like a small sine. The resulting phase noise follows the profile shape and falls as 1/f2, because the same frequency deviation gives a smaller phase deviation at higher rates. Multiplying the reference by N to make the LO adds 20·log N to these sidebands exactly as to all other phase noise (Step 5).

Step 5

Multiplying the reference: 20·log N and the close-in floor of every LO

You already know

Module 02 (Step 3) shows why multiplying a frequency by N multiplies its phase wobble by N and its phase-noise power by N², so L(f) rises by 20·log10N: 40 dB from 100 MHz to 10 GHz. It also covers PLL loop bandwidth, VCO noise and the phase-detector floor in detail. This step uses those results to answer one system question: what floor does the reference set for every LO in the system, and how should the reference be chosen for it?

Definitions

N: the ratio fLO/fref. 10 GHz from 100 MHz is N = 100 (40 dB); from 10 MHz it is N = 1 000 (60 dB).

Loop bandwidth BL: inside it the PLL output follows the (multiplied) reference; outside it the output follows its own VCO.

Phase-detector floor: the PLL's own in-band noise, LPD = FOM + 10·log10fPFD + 20·log10N, where FOM is the PLL chip's normalised figure of merit (−230 dBc/Hz here) and fPFD the phase-detector comparison frequency (here the reference frequency). With 100 MHz and N = 100: −230 + 80 + 40 = −110 dBc/Hz.

Close-in floor of an LO: the lowest phase noise any LO derived from this reference can have at small offsets: Lref(f) + 20·log N. No later filtering removes it, because it is in-band.

Dual-oscillator reference: a 100 MHz OCXO phase-locked to a 10 MHz OCXO with a narrow loop (about 100 Hz), so that the 10 MHz crystal sets the noise below 100 Hz and the 100 MHz crystal above.

Within the loop bandwidth, every LO is the reference multiplied: the transmit LO, the first and second receive LOs, the DDS clock and the ADC clock each carry Lref + 20·log Ni, with their own Ni. A 1 GHz ADC clock carries the reference + 20 dB; a 10 GHz LO carries it + 40 dB. Above a few hundred hertz the phase-detector floor usually takes over, and beyond the loop bandwidth the VCO. So the reference decides the region from about 1 Hz to a few hundred hertz offset at every LO: exactly where slow targets, ground clutter spreading and SAR azimuth sidelobes live.

LLO(f) ≈ [Lref(f) + 20·log N] ⊕ LPD inside BL,    LVCO(f) outside BL⊕ means adding the noise powers. Worked, 100 MHz OCXO to 10 GHz: at 10 Hz −119.6 + 40 = −79.6 dBc/Hz; at 100 Hz −141.2 + 40 = −101.2, against the phase-detector floor −110, total −100.6; at 1 kHz the floor dominates.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive approach chooses the reference by its price and its far-out floor, because "the PLL cleans it up". A TCXO-class 100 MHz reference has a good floor, but at 10 GHz its close-in noise is −64.3 dBc/Hz at 100 Hz and −88.6 dBc/Hz at 1 kHz, against −100.6 and −107.8 with an OCXO: 36 and 19 dB worse, and the integrated phase error from 10 Hz to 10 MHz rises from 0.12° to 3.5°. No PLL setting can fix this, because a wider loop passes more reference noise and a narrower one lets the VCO in. The opposite naive choice, a 10 MHz OCXO because it is "lower noise", raises N to 1 000 and the phase-detector floor by 10 dB.

Step 6

Clock distribution: buffers, fan-out, added jitter and skew

You already know

Module 07 shows that sampling-clock jitter tj limits an ADC's SNR to −20·log10(2π·fin·tj): 100 fs gives 64 dB at 1 GHz. Jitter is the rms timing error of the clock edges, the time-domain view of phase noise integrated over a band.

Definitions

Clock buffer (fan-out buffer): an IC that takes one clock input and drives several identical outputs, in a logic standard such as LVCMOS, LVDS, LVPECL or HCSL. Fan-out: the number of outputs per device (4, 8, 10…).

Additive (residual) jitter: the jitter a buffer adds to a perfect input, in fs rms over a stated band (often 12 kHz–20 MHz). It comes from the buffer's noise floor divided by the edge slew rate.

RSS: root-sum-square. Independent jitters add as σtotal = √(σ12 + σ22 + …).

Skew: the difference in arrival time of the same edge at two destinations. Output-to-output skew is between outputs of one device; part-to-part skew is between two devices of the same type, and is much larger. Cable or trace mismatch adds 4.8 ps per mm of coax (velocity factor 0.7).

Jitter cleaner: a PLL with a narrow loop and a low-noise VCXO that replaces the incoming jitter above its loop bandwidth with its own lower jitter.

A system with 16 ADCs needs 16 copies of the sampling clock. They come from a tree: one buffer drives several, each of those drives several more. Each level the clock passes through adds its own jitter (RSS) and its own delay, which differs from device to device. Jitter limits the SNR of every channel; skew makes the channels sample at different instants, which at the signal frequency is a phase error between channels.

Skew is mostly static: it can be measured and calibrated out (Step 9). Jitter is random: it cannot. And skew that changes with temperature (delay tempco of buffers and cables) is a slow random error that must be re-calibrated.

The naive approach routes the ADC clocks through an FPGA, which has plenty of outputs. The fabric's part-to-part skew (800 ps here) is 288° at 1 GHz, almost a full cycle, and it moves with every recompilation of the FPGA. The other naive mistake is to equalise the clock chips but not the cables: 20 mm of extra coax is 96 ps.

Step 7

Coherence over the coherent processing interval: Doppler, MTI and SAR

You already know

From Step 1, a coherent radar measures the phase step of each echo from pulse to pulse. From Step 3, an oscillator's phase wanders by about 2π·fc·τ·σy(τ) over an interval τ. Module 02 (Step 9) explains the range-correlation effect for a single radar.

Definitions

Coherent processing interval (CPI), T: the length of time over which echoes are added in phase: one Doppler FFT (ms to tens of ms), or one SAR synthetic aperture (0.1 s to several s). Doppler resolution is 1/T; one Doppler bin is 1/T wide.

Monostatic: the transmitter and receiver share one site and one reference. Bistatic: they are separate, each with its own reference.

Range correlation: in a monostatic radar the echo is compared with the same oscillator τd = 2R/c later. Only the phase change during τd counts; slower wander cancels. Phase noise at offset f is suppressed by 4·sin²(π·f·τd).

Quadratic phase error (QPE): a frequency that changes linearly across the CPI makes a phase that grows as t²; its peak value at the edges, in degrees, sets how much the Doppler peak or SAR point response broadens. Below about 45° the loss is small.

MTI improvement limit: random pulse-to-pulse phase jitter σφ (radians) leaves a clutter residue; the best clutter cancellation is about 1/σφ2, written in dB as −20·log10σφ.

Three things can spoil a CPI. (1) A constant frequency offset between transmitter and receiver puts the target in the wrong Doppler bin (Step 1). (2) A frequency change during the CPI makes a quadratic phase that broadens the peak and raises sidelobes. (3) Random phase noise across the Doppler band adds a noise floor around every strong echo, which limits MTI and SAR contrast.

A monostatic radar with a common reference is immune to (1) and (2): both the transmitter and the LO move together, and only the change within τd (200 µs at 30 km) matters. A bistatic radar, or a multistatic SAR, sees the full wander of two independent oscillators over the whole CPI: it is exactly the case where Step 2 and Step 3 numbers apply directly.

Doppler error = fc·Δy0 (bins: × T)    QPE ≈ π·fc·Δy·T/4    σφ2 = 2∫ L(f)·H(f) df, f = 1/T … PRF/2Δy0: offset between the two references; Δy ≈ √2·σy(T): change across the CPI for two independent oscillators. What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive bistatic design gives the remote receiver a TCXO and treats it like a monostatic radar. With a 1 ppm offset at 10 GHz the Doppler error is 10 kHz, 10 000 bins of a 1 s CPI, folded to a random place; the drift across the second adds a QPE of 66.6°, and the phase noise integrated over the Doppler band is 49° rms. The Doppler spectrum is not a peak any more. The same TCXO in a monostatic radar at 30 km range, over a 10 ms CPI, gives σφ = 0.24° and an MTI limit of 47.7 dB: usable, but 23 dB below an OCXO.

Step 8

Timing accuracy for TOA, TDOA and pulse-descriptor time stamps

You already know

A counter clocked at fclk measures time in steps of 1/fclk: at 100 MHz, 10 ns. From Step 2, a clock whose frequency is high by y gains y seconds every second. Radio waves travel 30 cm in 1 ns.

Definitions

Time of arrival (TOA): the time stamp an ESM or radar receiver attaches to the leading edge of a pulse, read from a counter driven by the reference.

Pulse descriptor word (PDW): the record an ESM receiver makes for each pulse: TOA, pulse width, frequency, amplitude and angle of arrival. Deinterleaving sorts the pulses into emitters mainly by the differences between successive TOAs (the PRI).

Quantisation of a time stamp: a counter LSB of 1/fclk gives an rms error of 1/(√12·fclk): 2.9 ns at 100 MHz, 0.29 ns at 1 GHz.

TDOA (time difference of arrival): two or more receivers at known places measure the TOA of the same pulse; the difference fixes the emitter on a hyperbola (line of position). A TDOA error δt moves the range difference by c·δt: 1 ns → 0.30 m.

Synchronise / syntonise: to synchronise is to align the clocks' time; to syntonise is to align their frequency. Synchronised clocks drift apart again at the rate of their frequency difference.

1 PPS: one pulse per second from a GPS receiver, aligned to GPS time to about 20 ns rms (illustrative). Two-way fibre time transfer (White Rabbit class): time sent both ways over a fibre so the delay cancels; sub-nanosecond.

A time stamp's error has four parts: the error at the last synchronisation x0; a frequency offset y0 that makes it grow linearly; ageing that makes it grow as t²; and the random wander from Step 3, τ·σy(τ). On top sits the counter quantisation. For a single receiver doing deinterleaving, only differences over a pulse train (microseconds to milliseconds) matter, and the counter quantisation and the pulse-edge detection dominate: the reference is not the problem. For TDOA, two sites must agree on absolute time, and the reference and its synchronisation dominate everything.

x(t) = √(x02 + (y0·t)2 + (½·D·t2)2 + (t·σy(t))2 + q2)    δtTDOA = √2·x    δR = c·δtTDOAThe terms are combined as if independent (RSS), which is pessimistic for some and optimistic for others; illustrative. q = 1/(√12·fclk). Two sites with equal, independent errors give √2·x for the difference.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive TDOA design synchronises two sites once and lets them run on TCXOs. Even with perfect initial time, the factory accuracy of 1 ppm makes each clock gain up to 1 µs per second. After 10 s each site is 10 µs out, the time difference is 14 µs, and the range difference is wrong by 4.2 km: the line of position is useless. Syntonising them once (so that only a 0.1 ppm residual is left) only buys a factor of ten.

(2) If sites can be continuously disciplined (GPS, fibre), choose the method whose error fits, then a counter clock with q well below it. (3) If they cannot (GPS denied, no link), choose the oscillator and the re-synchronisation interval so that yres·t and t·σy(t) stay inside the budget: rubidium holds 71 ns for about 2 hours here. (4) For PDW deinterleaving alone, choose the counter clock so q is a small fraction of the PRI jitter you must resolve; the reference type barely matters.

Check yourself

Two sites with OCXOs are syntonised to 1 ppb and synchronised. How long before the time difference reaches 100 ns?

Each site drifts y·t; two independent sites √2·y·t = 100 ns → t = 100×10−9/(1.41 × 10−9) ≈ 71 s. Without continuous discipline, re-synchronise at least once a minute.

Step 9

Synchronising multiple channels or sites

You already know

From Step 6, clock and LO paths to different channels have skew. From Step 8, sites need time and frequency transfer. An interferometer measures the angle of arrival from the phase difference between two antennas a distance d apart.

Definitions

Channel: one antenna, receiver and ADC path. Arrays, monopulse and direction-finding receivers have several, which must agree in phase and time.

Interferometer phase: φ = 360°·d·sinθ/λ for a wave arriving at angle θ from boresight. A phase error δφ gives an angle error δθ = (δφ/360°)·λ/(d·cosθ) (in radians). If d > λ/2 the phase wraps and the angle is ambiguous; real interferometers add shorter baselines to resolve it.

Path delay mismatch: the difference in delay between two channels' LO and clock paths, in ps. At the signal frequency it is a phase error 360°·f·Δτ.

Calibration injection: a known signal injected at the front of every channel so the processor can measure and remove the phase and delay differences. Once at switch-on, or continuously.

Phase synchronisation between sites: sharing not only time and frequency but the carrier phase, for coherent multistatic radar, by distributing the RF reference over phase-stabilised fibre.

Within one equipment the rule from Step 0 applies: one reference, one distributed LO, one distributed clock per channel group, length-matched. What is left is a static skew, which calibration removes, and a slow drift with temperature (about 0.2 ps/°C of differential drift here), which only repeated calibration removes. Between sites the same three levels apply: syntonise (frequency, Steps 2, 7), synchronise (time, Step 8), and, for coherent multistatic work, phase-synchronise.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive design matches the cables "by eye" and skips calibration. 20 ps of residual path mismatch is only 4 mm of coax, yet at 10 GHz it is 72° of phase and a 2.3° bearing error on a 15 cm baseline, the same at every angle near boresight and growing towards the edges. Calibrating once at switch-on removes the 20 ps, but a 20 °C warm-up leaves 4 ps (14.4°, 0.46°). Continuous injection holds it at about 0.06°.

How to choose the synchronisation

(1) From the angle accuracy, get the phase budget: δφ ≤ 360°·δθ·d·cosθ/λ; for 0.5° at boresight, 10 GHz, 0.15 m: 15.7°, which is 4.4 ps. (2) If the static mismatch is larger, calibrate; if the drift between calibrations (drift rate × temperature change) is larger, calibrate continuously or more often. (3) A longer baseline lowers δθ in proportion to d but makes the phase wrap more often (more ambiguity). (4) A higher carrier raises the phase error for the same picoseconds (at 18 GHz the same 4 ps is 26°), but the bearing error from a pure delay mismatch is c·Δτ/d at any frequency. Phase errors that are not delays (mixer and filter phase differences) do not scale this way and must be calibrated at each frequency.

Glossary of terms introduced on this page

1 PPS
One pulse per second from a GPS receiver, aligned to GPS time; used to discipline oscillators and to synchronise sites.
Accuracy
Offset of the frequency from nominal at delivery or after adjustment, in ppm or ppb.
Additive jitter
Jitter a buffer or device adds to a perfect input clock, fs rms over a stated band.
Ageing
Slow drift of an oscillator's frequency with time, ppb/day or ppm/year.
Allan deviation σy(τ)
Time-domain measure of frequency stability: typical change between successive frequency averages over τ.
Bistatic
Transmitter and receiver at different sites, with separate references.
Calibration injection
A known signal fed into every channel so phase and delay differences can be measured and removed.
Coherent
Transmit and receive phases related through one reference, so echo phase can be compared from pulse to pulse.
CPI
Coherent processing interval: the time over which echoes are added in phase; Doppler resolution is 1/CPI.
Dual-oscillator reference
A high-frequency OCXO locked to a low-frequency OCXO with a narrow loop, taking the better close-in and far-out noise of each.
Fan-out
Number of outputs driven by one clock buffer.
Flicker floor
The flat part of the Allan deviation, set by flicker FM; the best stability an oscillator reaches.
g-sensitivity Γ
Fractional frequency change per g of acceleration, ppb/g.
GPSDO
GPS-disciplined oscillator: an OCXO steered to GPS with a slow loop.
Holdover
Operation of a disciplined oscillator after losing its steering signal; it drifts at its own ageing and temperature rates.
Isolator (vibration)
Spring-and-damper mount that attenuates vibration above about 1.4 × its natural frequency and amplifies it near resonance.
MTI improvement limit
Best clutter cancellation allowed by pulse-to-pulse phase jitter, about −20·log σφ dB.
Monostatic
Transmitter and receiver at one site, sharing one reference.
OCXO
Oven-controlled crystal oscillator: crystal held at its turnover temperature.
PDW
Pulse descriptor word: TOA, pulse width, frequency, amplitude and angle for one intercepted pulse.
Phase-detector floor
A PLL's own in-band noise: FOM + 10·log fPFD + 20·log N.
ppm, ppb
Parts per million (10−6) and per billion (10−9).
Quadratic phase error
Phase error growing as t² across the CPI, from a frequency change; broadens Doppler peaks and SAR responses.
Range correlation
Cancellation of slow phase noise in a monostatic radar because the echo is compared with the same oscillator τd later.
Rubidium standard
Atomic frequency standard locking a crystal to a rubidium-87 transition near 6.8 GHz.
RSS
Root-sum-square addition of independent errors.
Skew
Difference in arrival time of the same clock edge at two destinations.
Synchronise / syntonise
Align time / align frequency.
TCXO
Temperature-compensated crystal oscillator: a thermistor network pulls the crystal to cancel its temperature curve.
TDOA
Time difference of arrival of one signal at two sites; locates the emitter on a hyperbola.
TOA
Time of arrival: the time stamp of a pulse's leading edge.
XO
Plain crystal oscillator without compensation.
