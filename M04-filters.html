Filters and Preselection · RF Systems for Component Engineers

Filters and preselection, from one resonator to probability of intercept

A filter is the part of a chain that decides which frequencies are allowed to exist. In a receiver it keeps out everything that could become noise, an image, a spur or a blocker; in a transmitter it keeps your own harmonics and splatter out of everyone else's band. It does this with a trade-off: loss, which in front of the LNA is noise; group delay, which smears pulses and chirps; size, power limits and temperature drift. This page builds those trade-offs from a single resonator and its Q, through filter families and group delay, to the frequency plan, blocking, transmit masks, technology choice and the ESM trade between preselector bandwidth and probability of intercept. Every term is defined before it is used, every step has worked numbers, and every datasheet line is traced from your design choice to the system effect.

Step 0

What a filter does in each chain

You already know

A filter passes a band of frequencies and attenuates the rest. You know how to design resonators, couple them and measure S21 and S11 on a network analyser. What this page adds is why the system engineer writes each line of your filter specification, and what happens to the radar or ESM receiver when a line is missed.

Definitions

Preselector: the first filter in a receiver, right after the antenna (and limiter). It "selects in advance" which band the rest of the receiver is allowed to see.

Pass band / stop band: the frequencies the filter passes with little loss, and the frequencies it must attenuate by at least a stated amount. Between them is the transition band.

Insertion loss (IL): how much weaker a pass-band signal is at the output than it would be with a perfect through line, in dB. Rejection is the same quantity at a stop-band frequency, usually quoted relative to the pass-band loss.

Group delay: how long the envelope of a signal takes to pass through the filter, in ns; defined in Step 4.

Every RF chain on this page contains three to six filters, and each one is there for a different reason. Before any formula, here is where they sit and what each one protects.

What goes wrong with no filters at all

Take an X-band receiver and a 100 W transmitter with every filter removed. The image noise doubles the IF noise: +3 dB noise figure (Step 5). Two −20 dBm out-of-band signals create an intermod product at −70 dBm, 32.5 dB above the noise, a false target (Step 6). The PA's 2nd harmonic at −20 dBc is 1 W radiated at 2f0 (Step 7). Each filter in the table below exists to stop one of these.

Check yourself

Why can the same band-pass filter be called a preselector in one chain and an image filter in another?

The name describes the job, not the construction. A filter before the LNA that sets which band is seen is a preselector; a filter before the mixer that removes the image is an image filter. In many receivers one filter does both jobs, and its specification is the stricter of the two.

Step 1

Filter response basics: one resonator, loaded Q and unloaded Q

You already know

A series or parallel LC tank resonates at f0 = 1/(2π√(LC)). Its Q tells you how sharply it resonates. You have measured Q on a network analyser from the 3 dB bandwidth.

Definitions

Qu (unloaded Q): set by your construction only: conductor loss (surface resistance, plating, skin depth), dielectric loss tangent, radiation and housing loss. It is a property of the technology: about 50–150 for chip inductors, 2 000–8 000 for an air cavity.

QL (loaded Q): set mainly by how strongly you couple the resonator to the ports (tap point, gap, iris). It is chosen to give the bandwidth.

f0, centre frequency: the frequency of peak transmission. Fractional bandwidth (FBW): BW/f0, written in percent.

|S21| in dB: the transmission through the filter. Its negative is the loss.

Every band-pass filter is built from resonators. Before coupling several of them, look at the simplest possible filter: one resonator placed between a 50 Ω source and a 50 Ω load. Two different Q values describe it, and the difference between them is the most important idea on this page.

The resonator stores energy and loses a little of it each cycle in its own conductor and dielectric. That internal loss defines the unloaded Q, Qu. Once the resonator is coupled to the source and load, energy also leaves through the ports, which is how the signal gets through. Counting both losses gives the loaded Q, QL, which sets the bandwidth. The external Q, Qe, counts only the loss to the ports.

1/QL = 1/Qu + 1/Qe    BW3dB = f0/QL    IL(f0) = −20·log10(1 − QL/Qu) dBQ = 2π × (energy stored) / (energy lost per cycle). QL/Qu is the fraction of the energy that is lost inside the resonator instead of reaching the load. The narrower you make the filter (higher QL) with the same resonator (same Qu), the more of the signal is burned as heat.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive approach picks the coupling for the bandwidth and ignores the resonator's own Q. A 10 MHz filter at 1 GHz needs QL = 100. Built from chip LC with Qu = 200, QL/Qu = 0.5: the loss is 6.0 dB, only 25 % of the power reaches the load, 25 % is reflected and 50 % becomes heat. The bandwidth is right; the filter is useless in front of an LNA.

How to choose QL and Qu

(1) The bandwidth fixes QL = f0/BW. (2) The loss budget fixes the largest allowed ratio: QL/Qu ≤ 1 − 10−IL/20. (3) So Qu ≥ QL / (1 − 10−IL/20). Example: 10 MHz at 1 GHz with at most 1 dB loss: QL = 100, ratio ≤ 0.109, Qu ≥ 920. (4) Qu then picks the technology (Step 8). Raising QL narrows the filter and raises the loss; raising Qu lowers the loss at the same bandwidth but means a bigger or better resonator.

Step 2

Insertion loss of a multi-resonator filter, and its noise cost before the LNA

You already know

From the LNA and receiver noise module: a passive loss L at temperature 290 K has noise figure L, and in front of the LNA it adds to the system noise figure dB for dB (Friis). Sensitivity is −174 dBm/Hz + NF + 10·log B + SNRmin.

Definitions

Order N: the number of resonators (poles). More poles give a steeper skirt and more loss.

g-values: the normalised element values of the low-pass prototype. For a 5-pole 0.1 dB Chebyshev they are 1.147, 1.371, 1.975, 1.371, 1.147 (sum 7.01).

System noise figure: the noise figure of the whole receive chain from the antenna port, which sets sensitivity.

A practical filter couples N resonators. The response is defined by a low-pass prototype: a ladder of N normalised elements g1 … gN (from tables or formulas for each family) that is scaled to the centre frequency and bandwidth. Each resonator then stores energy, and each one has the same Qu. Cohn's classic result adds up their losses:

IL(f0) ≈ 4.343 · (g1 + g2 + … + gN) / (FBW · Qu) dB    IL(f) ≈ 4.343 · ω0 · τg(f) / Qu dBFBW is the fractional bandwidth as a ratio (0.05 for 5 %). The second form says the same thing in physical terms: loss is proportional to group delay τg, the time the signal spends stored in the resonators. Where the delay is largest (the band edges) the loss is largest too, so a lossy filter has "rounded shoulders".

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive approach puts the sharpest filter it can find in front of the LNA. A 7-pole 0.1 dB Chebyshev (Σg = 10.97) on microstrip (Qu 150), 500 MHz wide at 9.5 GHz: IL = 4.343 × 10.97/(0.0526 × 150) = 6.04 dB. System NF goes from 1.59 dB to 7.63 dB, and radar detection range falls to 10−6.04/40 = 0.71, a 29 % loss, before any interferer is present.

That is the central preselector trade: loss in front of the LNA costs sensitivity; no filter in front of the LNA costs dynamic range.

How to choose order, Qu and position

(1) The rejection you need (Steps 3, 5, 6) sets the lowest order N and the family. (2) The noise-figure cost you can accept sets the largest IL before the LNA. (3) Rearranging Cohn: Qu ≥ 4.343·Σg/(FBW·IL). Example: 5-pole 0.1 dB Chebyshev, FBW 5.26 %, IL ≤ 0.5 dB → Qu ≥ 4.343 × 7.01/(0.0526 × 0.5) ≈ 1 160: ceramic or cavity. (4) If no technology meets it, split the job: a wide, low-order, low-loss filter before the LNA for survival and gross rejection, and the sharp filter after the LNA where its loss is hidden. Each extra pole adds about Σg/N to the loss; each halving of the bandwidth doubles it.

  • Filter designers: 0.5 dB saved in a preselector is worth exactly as much as 0.5 dB off the LNA noise figure, and is often easier. Use the lowest order that meets rejection, and silver-plate cavities (surface resistance sets Qu).

Check yourself

A 7-pole filter replaces a 5-pole one of the same bandwidth and Qu. Roughly how much more loss?

For 0.1 dB Chebyshev, Σg rises from 7.01 to 10.97 (7-pole), so the loss grows by about 1.56 times: 1.93 dB becomes about 3.0 dB in the X-band example.

Why is a preselector specified at its worst band edge, not at the centre?

The system specification is a minimum sensitivity over the whole band. The band-edge channels have the most filter loss (largest group delay), so they set the guaranteed number.

Step 3

Bandwidth, ripple, rejection and shape factor; Butterworth, Chebyshev and elliptic

You already know

Butterworth is "maximally flat", Chebyshev ripples in the pass band, elliptic has notches in the stop band. You pick g-values from a table.

Definitions

Bandwidth: for Butterworth the 3 dB bandwidth; for Chebyshev and elliptic the ripple bandwidth, the band over which loss stays within the ripple. Always ask which one a datasheet quotes.

Ripple: the peak-to-peak variation of loss across the pass band, in dB.

Rejection: attenuation at a stated stop-band frequency, relative to the pass-band loss.

Shape factor: ratio of a wide-attenuation bandwidth (40 or 60 dB) to the 3 dB bandwidth. 1 would be a perfect "brick wall".

Transmission zero: a frequency where the response is exactly zero (a notch). Elliptic and cross-coupled filters create them by coupling non-adjacent resonators.

Return loss: −|S11| in dB. For a lossless filter it follows from the ripple: 0.1 dB ripple ↔ 16.4 dB return loss; 0.5 dB ↔ 9.6 dB.

Ωs (elliptic stop-band edge): the normalised frequency where the elliptic's stop band starts. Below it the response falls; above it the rejection stays at or above a fixed minimum (the floor). A smaller Ωs gives a steeper skirt but a lower floor.

The system engineer specifies a filter by its mask: the pass band where the loss must stay below a limit, and the stop bands where the rejection must exceed a limit. How fast the response goes from one to the other is the shape factor. To compare families fairly they are designed from a normalised low-pass prototype with frequency variable Ω, then mapped to a band-pass filter:

Ω = (f/f0 − f0/f) · f0/BW    |H|² = 1 / (1 + ε²·RN(Ω)²)    SF60/3 = BW60dB / BW3dBRN is the family's characteristic function: ΩN (Butterworth), the Chebyshev polynomial TN(Ω) (Chebyshev), or an elliptic rational function with zeros in the pass band and poles (transmission zeros) in the stop band (elliptic). ε sets the ripple: ripple = 10·log(1 + ε²) dB. Ω = ±1 is the band edge.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive approach meets a stop-band point by adding Butterworth poles. 60 dB at twice the band edge (Ω = 2) needs a 10-pole Butterworth (each pole gives about 6 dB per octave): Σg = 12.8, so 1.8 × the loss of the 5-pole Chebyshev from Step 2, and ten resonators to tune. An 8-pole 0.1 dB Chebyshev or a 7-pole 0.5 dB Chebyshev reaches the same point with the same loss (Σg ≈ 12.6); a 6-pole elliptic with Ωs = 2 reaches it with Σg = 7.9, only 1.1 × the 5-pole Chebyshev's loss.

(2) The elliptic is steepest near the band but its stop band has a floor: rejection never exceeds the chosen minimum (43.4 dB here), so it cannot meet a 60 dB specification however far away the interferer is. How to choose family, order, ripple and Ωs

(1) Write the mask: ripple bandwidth and every stop-band point (frequency, rejection). (2) Choose the ripple from the return loss and flatness the system needs: 0.1 dB ↔ 16.4 dB RL is the usual choice; 0.5 dB only if VSWR and gain flatness allow it. (3) Find the lowest order that meets the nearest stop-band point. (4) If one point close to the band dominates and the far rejection need is moderate, use an elliptic (or cross-coupled zeros) with Ωs at that point, and check that the floor meets every farther point. (5) Check group delay (Step 4) and loss (Step 2) before fixing the design: each step up in steepness costs both.

Check yourself

A specification asks for 60 dB at 1.5 × the band edge. Can a 5-pole elliptic with Ωs = 1.5 meet it?

No. Its stop-band minimum is 43.4 dB. Either increase the order (7-pole gives about 72 dB minimum at Ωs 1.5) or accept a larger Ωs.

Why do Chebyshev filters list a "ripple bandwidth" that is narrower than the 3 dB bandwidth?

The ripple band ends where the loss first exceeds the ripple value (0.1 dB). The response keeps falling after that and crosses 3 dB a little later: for a 5-pole 0.1 dB Chebyshev the 3 dB bandwidth is 1.13 × the ripple bandwidth.

Step 4

Group delay and its variation: pulse rise time, chirp fidelity and compression sidelobes

You already know

S21 has a phase. A network analyser can display group delay, the slope of that phase against frequency. A radar pulse occupies a bandwidth of roughly 1/(pulse width); a chirp occupies its sweep bandwidth B.

Definitions

Group delay variation (flatness): the peak-to-peak change in τg across a stated band, in ns. Datasheets give it over the pass band or over the central 80 %.

Dispersion: distortion caused by group delay that changes with frequency.

Rise time: time for the pulse envelope to go from 10 % to 90 % of its final value. Overshoot: how far it rises above the final value, in %. Ringing: the decaying oscillation that follows.

Pulse compression: transmitting a long chirp (frequency swept over B during T) and correlating the echo with a copy of the chirp (the matched filter), which squeezes it into a pulse about 1/B wide. PSLR, peak sidelobe ratio, is the highest sidelobe relative to the peak.

Weighting (window): tapering the reference chirp's amplitude (for example Hamming) to lower the range sidelobes from −13.3 dB to about −40 dB, at the cost of a wider main lobe.

Filter BW / signal BW: how much wider the filter is than the signal it carries (for a 100 ns pulse the signal bandwidth is taken as 10 MHz; for the chirp, its 100 MHz sweep). BW is the 3 dB bandwidth for Butterworth and the ripple bandwidth for the others.

Delay ripple and ripple cycles: a sinusoidal wobble of group delay on top of the filter's own delay, given as ns peak-to-peak and as the number of wobble periods across the filter bandwidth. Its period in frequency is Fr = filter BW / cycles.

A signal is a sum of frequencies. If every frequency in it is delayed by the same time, the signal comes out identical, only later. If different frequencies are delayed by different times, the parts of the signal arrive out of step and the shape is distorted. Group delay is the delay at each frequency:

τg(f) = −(1/2π) · dφ/df    τg,bp(f0) ≈ Σg / (2π·BW)    trise ≈ 0.7 / BW3dB    paired echo = 20·log(a/2), a = Δτpk·Frφ is the phase of S21 in radians. A sinusoidal delay ripple of peak Δτpk and period Fr (Hz) across the band is a phase ripple of peak a radians; after pulse compression it creates two false "echo" sidelobes at ± 1/Fr from the target, each 20·log(a/2) dB below it. Amplitude ripple works the same way: ripple p-p (dB)/17.4 = a.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive approach makes the filter exactly as wide as the signal (ratio 1). A 100 ns pulse through a 10 MHz 5-pole 0.1 dB Chebyshev: rise time 80 ns, so the pulse never reaches a flat top; the delay across the signal band varies by 110 ns because the band edges sit on the delay peaks. A 100 MHz chirp through a 100 MHz filter: compressed sidelobes rise from −42.7 dB (Hamming, ideal) to −32.7 dB.

How to choose the bandwidth margin, family and delay-ripple limit

(1) Bandwidth: make the filter 1.5–2 × the signal bandwidth; below that, rise time and band-edge delay dominate. (2) Family: for chirps and short pulses prefer low ripple (Butterworth, 0.1 dB Chebyshev) or add a delay equaliser; avoid 0.5 dB ripple. (3) Delay-ripple limit from the sidelobe budget: Δτpk ≤ 2·10SL/20/Fr; for −45 dB at 25 MHz that is 0.45 ns peak. Why does a sharper filter ring more?

Steep skirts need high-Q poles close to the jω axis. Those poles store energy and release it slowly, which is the ringing; in the frequency domain they show up as a tall delay peak at the band edge.

Step 5

Image and half-IF rejection in a frequency plan

You already know

A mixer multiplies RF by LO and produces sum and difference frequencies. The IF filter selects the difference |fRF − fLO| = fIF.

Definitions

IF (intermediate frequency): the fixed frequency the receiver converts every RF signal to, where most gain and filtering happen.

Low-side / high-side LO: LO below or above the wanted RF. It decides which side the image is on.

Image rejection: how much weaker the IF output is for a signal at the image than for the same signal at the wanted frequency, in dB.

Frequency plan: the choice of IF(s), LO(s) and filters so that images and strong spurs fall where filters can remove them.

Image rejection required: the rejection the system specification asks for, set by the strongest signal expected at the image minus the weakest wanted signal, plus margin. For example a −30 dBm emitter and a −90 dBm target need 60 dB.

A mixer cannot tell which side of the LO a signal is on. With the LO below the wanted signal (low-side LO, fLO = fRF − fIF), a signal at fLO − fIF also produces exactly fIF. That second frequency is the image. Once it is in the IF it cannot be removed: it looks exactly like a real signal. Only a filter before the mixer can stop it. The mixer also responds, more weakly, to many other frequencies m·fRF ± n·fLO = fIF (spurious responses). The most troublesome is the half-IF spur, from the second harmonics of RF and LO: 2fRF − 2fLO = fIF.

fimage = fRF ∓ 2·fIF    f½IF = fRF ∓ fIF/2    (upper sign: low-side LO)    rej½IF,total = S22 + (Pref − Pin) + 2·RfilterThe image is 2·fIF from the wanted signal; the half-IF spur is only fIF/2 away, so it is much closer and much harder to filter. The 2×2 spur is a second-order product: each dB the filter removes at the input lowers the spur by 2 dB. S22 is the mixer's 2×2 suppression (dBc) quoted at input level Pref.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive approach picks the lowest convenient IF, say 160 MHz, behind the same 500 MHz preselector. The image at 9.68 GHz is then inside the filter and only 10.9 dB down, and the half-IF spur at 9.92 GHz is 0.1 dB down. Every emitter at 9.68 GHz appears at 10 GHz as a false signal only 11 dB weaker, and the image noise adds up to 3 dB to the noise figure.

(5) If a low final IF is needed, convert twice: high first IF for image rejection, then low second IF.

Noise at the image frequency: why can a missing image filter cost 3 dB of noise figure even with no interferer present?

Step 6

Out-of-band blocking and intermodulation protection

You already know

From the LNA module: P1dB is where gain has dropped 1 dB; IIP3 is the input level where the extrapolated third-order product equals the fundamental; third-order products sit at 2f1 − f2 and 2f2 − f1.

Definitions

Blocker: a strong unwanted signal, usually out of band, that degrades reception of a weak wanted one.

Desensitisation: the rise in the minimum detectable signal caused by a blocker.

Spurious-free dynamic range (SFDR): the range of input levels from the noise floor up to where IM3 products reach the noise floor.

The antenna collects everything: your own transmitter's leakage, communications links, other radars, jammers. Most of it is outside your band, so it never reaches the detector, but it does reach the LNA and mixer if nothing stops it. There it does two kinds of damage. Blocking (desensitisation): a strong signal compresses the amplifier, so a weak in-band signal loses gain and noise rises. Intermodulation: two strong out-of-band signals at f1 and f2 create a product 2f1 − f2 that can land in your band, where no later filter can remove it. The preselector's rejection at f1 and f2 lowers both inputs before the LNA sees them.

PIM3,in = 2·P1 + P2 − 2·IIP3    with filter: P1 → P1 − R(f1), P2 → P2 − R(f2)    noise floor = −174 + NF + 10·log BAll in dBm referred to the LNA input. The IM3 product falls by 2R(f1) + R(f2): every dB of rejection at the nearer tone is worth 2 dB of intermod. P1 is the tone nearer the band.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

With no preselector the two −20 dBm tones put IM3 at 3 × (−20) − 2 × 5 = −70 dBm, 32.5 dB above the −102.5 dBm noise floor. The naive fix is a more linear LNA: IM3 falls 2 dB per dB of IIP3, so reaching the noise floor needs IIP3 +21.3 dBm instead of +5 dBm, 16 dB more, which costs a much larger device and several times the DC power. The 3-pole filter in the table does the same job for about 0.9 dB of loss (Qu 300).

Step 7

Transmit filtering: harmonics, spectral mask and power handling

You already know

From the power-amplifier module: a PA driven into saturation produces harmonics at 2f0, 3f0 … typically only 15–30 dB below the carrier. A rectangular pulse has a sin(x)/x spectrum whose sidelobes fall slowly.

Definitions

Spectral mask: a limit line of allowed emission level against frequency offset from the carrier.

Power handling: average power (set by heating) and peak power (set by voltage breakdown in gaps, and in vacuum by multipaction, a resonant electron avalanche) the filter can carry.

Spurious domain: frequencies far from the carrier (harmonics, mixing products). Out-of-band domain: frequencies just outside the necessary bandwidth.

Cut-off ratio fc/f0: where the low-pass filter's pass band ends, relative to the carrier. 1.3 means the ripple band ends 30 % above f0. A lower ratio moves the skirt closer to 2f0 (more rejection) but also brings the band-edge loss and delay peak closer to the carrier.

On the transmit side the filter protects other users from you. Two separate problems: harmonics, far from the carrier, usually removed by a low-pass filter after the PA; and out-of-band emission close to the carrier (spectral splatter from fast pulse edges and PA nonlinearity), which must stay under a regulatory or platform spectral mask. Because the filter sits after the PA, its loss is not noise but lost power, turned into heat inside the filter.

Pharm,out = P0 + Hn(dBc) − R(n·f0)    Pdiss = Pin·(1 − 10−IL/10)    Wstored ≈ Pin·τgHn is the PA's n-th harmonic level relative to the carrier. The energy stored in a filter is the power flowing through it times its group delay. At the band edges, where delay peaks, the stored energy, the resonator voltages and the risk of breakdown or multipaction all peak too.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive approach uses a simple maximally flat low-pass: a 5-pole Butterworth with cut-off 1.3 f0 leaves the 2nd harmonic at −38.8 dBc, 21 dB above the −60 dBc limit; at 100 W that is 13 mW at 2f0. Going to 9 poles only reaches 33.7 dB of rejection (−53.7 dBc) and still fails, with almost twice the loss.

(3) An elliptic with a transmission zero near 2f0 gives the rejection with fewer poles: 5-pole elliptic, 54.8 dB. (4) Check heat and peak field at the highest power and temperature.

Close to the carrier: the spectral mask

What the pulse spectrum is. A rectangular pulse of width τ has a sin(x)/x spectrum: nulls every 1/τ, and sidelobes whose envelope falls at 20 dB per decade of offset. Real pulses have rise and fall times tr; that multiplies the spectrum by a second sin(x)/x, and beyond about 1/(π·tr) the envelope falls at 40 dB per decade. For τ = 1 µs and tr = 20 ns the corners are 1 MHz and 16 MHz.

What the mask is and the output filter does. The illustrative mask below allows 0 dB out to ±1/τ, then falls at 40 dB per decade to a −60 dB floor. An output band-pass filter of bandwidth BW (Butterworth, order N) attenuates the far sidelobes, but every filter lengthens the rise time, roughly tout ≈ √(tr² + (0.7/BW)²).

What goes wrong with the naive approach

Fast 20 ns edges and no output filter: the sidelobes between 1 and 16 MHz fall at only 20 dB per decade while the mask falls at 40, and the spectrum breaks the mask by 14.1 dB.

How to build at least one of these technologies. Every material expands with temperature and every dielectric constant changes with temperature.

How to choose the technology

(1) Qu needed from the bandwidth and loss (Steps 1, 2) narrows the list. (2) Frequency range rules out some (SAW above about 2.5 GHz, lumped LC above about 3 GHz). (3) Drift budget: the shift over the temperature range should be a small part of the guard band. With a channel 80 % of the filter bandwidth the guard is 10 % on each side, so keep |TCF|·f0·ΔT well inside it, or widen the filter, or compensate (Invar, ceramic τf ≈ 0, heater). (4) Power handling (Step 7), size and weight, and for tunable filters the tuning or switching speed (Step 9).

Check yourself

Why are acoustic (SAW/BAW) filters so small for their Q?

The acoustic wave travels about 105 times slower than light, so a resonator a fraction of an acoustic wavelength long is tiny. The trade-off: low power handling, limited top frequency, and higher TCF unless temperature-compensated.

Step 9

ESM: preselector bandwidth versus probability of intercept

You already know

An ESM (electronic support measures) receiver listens for radar signals over a very wide band, for example 2–18 GHz, to detect, measure and identify them. Sensitivity improves as bandwidth falls (10·log B).

Definitions

Instantaneous bandwidth (IBW): the frequency range processed at one time.

Dwell time: how long a scanning receiver stays at one frequency step, including tuning or switching and settling time.

Probability of intercept (POI): probability that a given emitter is detected within a given time.

Pulse density: radar pulses per second arriving in the receiver's band. Overlapping pulses corrupt the measurements.

A radar receiver knows its own frequency. An ESM receiver does not: the emitter can be anywhere in 16 GHz, and its main beam may sweep past the ESM antenna for only a few milliseconds per antenna rotation. The ESM designer must choose an instantaneous bandwidth (IBW), the band the receiver sees at one moment, set by the preselector. Wide open, it sees every emitter all the time but with poor sensitivity and a crowded, intermod-prone band. Narrow, it is sensitive and clean, but must step or sweep the narrow window across the band, and may be looking elsewhere when the emitter's beam passes. The chance of being in the right place at the right time is the probability of intercept (POI).

M = ⌈span / IBW⌉ steps    Trevisit = M · Tdwell    POI ≈ min(1, (Ton + Tdwell) / Trevisit)    S = −174 + NF + 10·log B + SNRTon is how long the emitter's beam illuminates the ESM per scan. Tdwell includes tuning or switching time. A simple single-look model: the receiver catches the emitter if one of its dwells overlaps the illumination. A channelised receiver (all filters in parallel) has POI = 1 with narrow channels, at the cost of one receiver per channel.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive approach opens the receiver to the whole 2–18 GHz band. POI is 100 %, but the sensitivity is −47 dBm, and at 200 000 pulses per second in the band each 1 µs pulse has a 1 − e−0.4 = 33 % chance of overlapping another, corrupting its measurements. The opposite naive choice, a 50 MHz YIG, is 25 dB more sensitive but catches a 20 ms illumination only 3.2 % of the time.

How to choose the instantaneous bandwidth and preselector type

(1) The weakest emitter at the longest range sets the sensitivity, and so the largest IBW: each halving of IBW gains 3 dB. (2) The shortest illumination Ton sets the largest number of steps: POI ≈ (Ton + Tdwell)/(M·Tdwell) ≥ target. With 20 ms, 2 ms dwell and a 100 % target, M ≤ 11. (3) Pulse density sets an upper limit on IBW for clean measurements. (4) If no single IBW meets (1)–(3), go parallel: a channelised receiver, at the cost of one channel per sub-band. (5) The switching or tuning time adds to each dwell.

Check yourself

With the YIG at 50 MHz (320 steps) and 0.2 ms tuning per step, how short must the dwell become to reach 50 % POI against a 20 ms illumination?

With x = dwell + tuning: (20 + x)/(320·x) = 0.5 → x ≈ 0.126 ms. That is less than the 0.2 ms tuning time alone, so 50 % is impossible: even zero dwell gives (20.2)/(64) ≈ 32 %. A dwell shorter than the radar PRI would miss pulses anyway. The practical answers are a wider IBW, a faster tuner, or parallel channels.

Glossary of terms introduced on this page

Band-edge loss
Insertion loss at the edge of the pass band; larger than mid-band because group delay peaks there.
Blocking / desensitisation
Loss of sensitivity caused by a strong unwanted signal compressing an amplifier or mixer.
Butterworth
Filter family with maximally flat pass band and moderate skirt; the flattest group delay of the three classic families.
Channelised receiver
A receiver that splits the band into many filters processed in parallel, giving 100 % intercept with narrow channels.
Chebyshev
All-pole family with equal ripple in the pass band, steeper skirts than Butterworth for the same order.
Cohn's formula
IL ≈ 4.343·Σg/(FBW·Qu) dB: mid-band dissipation loss of an N-resonator filter.
Dispersion
Signal distortion from group delay that varies with frequency.
Elliptic (Cauer)
Family with ripple in both pass and stop band and transmission zeros; steepest skirt, but a fixed stop-band floor.
External Q (Qe)
Q counting only the energy that leaves through the ports; set by input/output coupling.
Fractional bandwidth (FBW)
Bandwidth divided by centre frequency.
Frequency plan
Choice of IFs, LOs and filters so that images and spurs fall where they can be filtered.
g-values
Normalised element values of a low-pass prototype filter.
Group delay (τg)
−(1/2π)·dφ/df; the time the envelope of a narrowband signal takes to pass through.
Group delay variation
Peak-to-peak change of group delay over a stated band.
Guard band
Extra filter bandwidth beyond the wanted channel, to absorb drift and tolerance.
Half-IF spur
Mixer 2×2 response at fRF ∓ fIF/2, from second harmonics of RF and LO.
Image
The second RF frequency that converts to the same IF: fRF ∓ 2fIF.
Instantaneous bandwidth (IBW)
The frequency range a receiver processes at one time.
Insertion loss (IL)
Pass-band loss of the filter compared with a through line, in dB.
Loaded Q (QL)
Q including both internal loss and loss to the ports; sets the bandwidth: BW = f0/QL.
Matched filter
Correlation of the received signal with a copy of the transmitted waveform; gives maximum SNR and compresses a chirp.
Multipaction
Resonant electron avalanche between metal surfaces in vacuum at high RF field; a peak-power limit for space filters.
Paired echoes
Two false sidelobes either side of a compressed pulse, caused by sinusoidal amplitude or phase (delay) ripple.
Preselector
The first filter in a receiver, deciding which band the rest of the receiver sees.
Probability of intercept (POI)
Probability that an ESM receiver detects a given emitter within a given time.
PSLR
Peak sidelobe ratio: highest sidelobe relative to the main peak, in dB.
Rejection
Attenuation at a stop-band frequency, relative to the pass-band loss.
Ripple
Peak-to-peak loss variation across the pass band.
Ripple bandwidth
The bandwidth over which a Chebyshev or elliptic filter stays within its ripple.
SAW / BAW
Surface / bulk acoustic wave resonators: very small, steep filters, limited in frequency and power.
Shape factor
Ratio of the 40 dB or 60 dB bandwidth to the 3 dB bandwidth.
Spectral mask
Limit on emitted power against frequency offset from the carrier.
Switched filter bank
A set of sub-band filters selected one at a time by switches.
TCF (τf)
Temperature coefficient of frequency, in ppm/°C.
Transmission zero
A frequency where the filter's transmission is zero (a notch).
Unloaded Q (Qu)
Q of the resonator from its internal losses only; a property of the technology.
YIG
Yttrium iron garnet: a magnetically tuned resonator used in tunable preselectors.
