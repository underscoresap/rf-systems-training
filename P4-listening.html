Listening: how a receiver watches the spectrum

Every radar in R1–R6 and Part 3 announces itself: it transmits strong pulses in a known band. A listening receiver (in defence terms an ESM or RWR receiver, in civil terms a spectrum monitor) tries to notice those pulses, measure each one, and work out where they came from. This page follows a listener on a hilltop as a search radar sweeps past, and builds up every technical idea needed: the one-way advantage, sensitivity against bandwidth, the search problem (probability of intercept), measuring each pulse, and direction finding. The receiver hardware itself is covered in Module 11.

Step 0

What the listener actually hears

You already know

From R2 Step 8: a scanning radar's main beam sweeps past any point once per scan, so a listener sees a burst of strong pulses, then weak sidelobe pulses for the rest of the scan. From R1: a listener needs only the one-way path.

Picture the scene. A surveillance radar rotates once every 6 s with a 2° beam, sending 1 µs pulses at 1 kHz. A small receiver sits on a hilltop 20 km away. What arrives there is not a steady signal:

  • For about 33 ms in every 6 s the main beam points at the hill: 33 strong pulses.
  • For the rest of the scan only the sidelobes point there: the same pulses, 25–40 dB weaker.
  • Each pulse is only 1 µs long: the radar is silent 99.9 % of the time even when it points at the listener.

So the listener's task has three parts: be sensitive enough to hear the pulses (Steps 1–2), be listening at the right frequency at the right moment (Step 3), and measure each pulse well enough to identify and locate the radar (Steps 5–6).

Bottom, the peak pulse power at the listener over two scans (12 s), with the receiver's sensitivity as a dashed line; the marker is the current time. Radar: 1 kW peak, 30 dBi, 10 GHz, 2° beam, 6 s scan; listener antenna 0 dBi. Things to check: (1) at 20 km with −54 dBm sensitivity, only the main-beam bursts are heard; (2) improve the sensitivity to −80 dBm: the sidelobes are heard all the time; (3) move to 150 km: even the main beam drops below −54 dBm.

Step 1

The one-way advantage

The radar's own echo travels out and back, falling as 1/R⁴, and is reduced again by the target's small RCS. The listener only needs the outward trip, falling as 1/R². That is why a listener can usually hear a radar long before the radar can see the listener.

Plistener = ERP · Gr · (λ / 4πR)²    in dB: Pr = ERP + Gr − FSPL, FSPL = 20·log10(4πR/λ)    ERP = Pt·Gt

The listener 100 km away receives 41 dB more than the radar does from a target ten times closer. Real limits come from the horizon (both must see each other; at 10 GHz radio waves travel only slightly beyond line of sight), from terrain, and from the listener's own sensitivity, which Step 2 shows is the real catch.

Step 2

Sensitivity against bandwidth

The problem. The radar knows its own frequency and uses a matched filter about 1/τ wide (R1). The listener does not know the frequency in advance. To be sure of catching any radar from 2 to 18 GHz at once, it would need a 16 GHz-wide receiver, and thermal noise grows with bandwidth: kTB.

noise floor = −174 dBm/Hz + 10·log10(B) + NF    sensitivity = noise floor + SNR needed (about 15 dB for reliable single-pulse detection at a low false-alarm rate)
Receiver bandwidth (NF 12 dB)Noise floorSensitivityMain-beam detection range of R1's radar (0 dBi)
2 GHz (wideband)−69 dBm−54 dBm38 km
10 MHz (narrow)−92 dBm−77 dBm530 km (horizon-limited in practice)

The trade. Every factor of 10 in bandwidth costs 10 dB of sensitivity, which costs a factor of 3.2 in detection range (one-way, R²). But a narrow receiver hears only a narrow slice of the spectrum at a time and must search for the radar, which Step 3 shows can take minutes. So every listening receiver chooses a point on a triangle: bandwidth (how much spectrum at once), sensitivity, and probability of intercept. You cannot have all three.

Other things that set sensitivity. The front-end noise figure (Module 01), including any limiter and filter loss in front of the LNA; the video bandwidth after detection (a crystal-video receiver's sensitivity is set by its video bandwidth, not its RF bandwidth); and the required false-alarm rate (R3: a 1 GHz receiver makes a billion decisions per second, so it needs a high threshold).

Step 3

The search problem: probability of intercept (POI)

The problem. An intercept needs three things at the same moment: the radar's main beam on the listener (33 ms every 6 s in our example), the radar transmitting (it is), and the receiver tuned to the radar's frequency. A wideband receiver is always tuned. A narrow swept receiver is tuned to any one frequency only briefly.

Worked. A swept receiver with a 20 MHz window covers 2–18 GHz in 800 steps. To be sure of catching at least one pulse at each step it must stay at least one PRI (1 ms), so one sweep takes 0.8 s and it sits on the radar's frequency for 1 ms of every 0.8 s. The two "windows" (33 ms of main beam every 6 s, 1 ms of tuning every 0.8 s) coincide on a given scan with probability about (33 + 1)/800 ≈ 4 %. On average it takes about 6 s / 0.043 ≈ 140 s to intercept. A wideband receiver catches the radar on the very first scan: 6 s.

POI per scan ≈ (dwellradar + dwellreceiver) / sweep periodreceiver (when the receiver sweeps faster than the radar scans)    mean time to intercept ≈ scan period / POI per scan

That is the reason modern listening receivers are wideband (channelised or digital, Step 4, Module 11), and the reason the sensitivity loss of Step 2 is accepted: catching a radar in seconds matters more than hearing it at the horizon.

Try it: 60 s of time across, 2–18 GHz up the side. The radar transmits at 9.4 GHz; its main-beam bursts are the purple bars. The receiver's tuned window is the teal sawtooth. An intercept (orange) happens when both overlap. The window slider is logarithmic (1.3 = 20 MHz, 4.2 = 16 GHz, i.e. the whole band). Things to check: (1) default 20 MHz: only a few intercepts in 60 s, or none; (2) window 16 GHz: every main-beam burst is caught; (3) a shorter radar dwell (5 ms, a fast-scanning radar) makes the swept receiver almost blind.

Step 5

Measuring each pulse: the pulse descriptor word

For every pulse it detects, the receiver writes a short record, the pulse descriptor word (PDW). Part 5 uses streams of these to sort and identify radars, so their accuracy matters.

FieldHow it is measuredWhat limits it
TOA (time of arrival)Time the detected envelope crosses a threshold, counted by a fast clockRise time (set by video/IF bandwidth), noise, clock resolution
PW (pulse width)Time between the leading and trailing threshold crossingsSame as TOA, on two edges; multipath stretches it
RF (frequency)IFM discriminator, channel number, or FFT of the samplesSNR, pulse length (FFT resolution ≈ 1/PW), channel width
PA (pulse amplitude)Peak of the envelopeCalibration, the radar's scan modulation, multipath
AOA (angle of arrival)Direction finding, Step 6Antenna and channel matching
Intrapulse modulationFrequency or phase change inside the pulse (chirp, code)SNR, sample rate

Edges and noise. A receiver with video bandwidth Bv turns a sharp pulse edge into a slope with rise time tr ≈ 0.35/Bv. Noise moves the threshold crossing up and down that slope: the timing error is roughly tr divided by the voltage SNR. A 20 MHz video bandwidth gives 17.5 ns rise time; at 20 dB SNR (10× in voltage) the TOA scatters by about 2 ns, but at 10 dB it is about 8 ns. Narrowing the bandwidth to improve sensitivity (Step 2) slows the edges and degrades TOA and PW: another trade.

Threshold placement. A fixed threshold just above the noise catches weak pulses, but strong pulses cross it earlier on their slope, so TOA depends on amplitude. Measuring at 50 % of each pulse's own peak removes that, at the cost of needing to know the peak first.

Try it: the detected envelope of one pulse (teal) with noise, the threshold (dashed) and the measured leading and trailing edges (orange). The readout also gives the rms error over 300 noisy pulses. Things to check: (1) default: TOA scatters by about 2 ns; (2) SNR 10 dB: about 8 ns; (3) video bandwidth 5 MHz: the edges slow to 70 ns and the error grows; (4) choose the fixed threshold, then raise the SNR from 15 to 35 dB: the leading edge is crossed earlier and the measured PW grows, because a stronger pulse reaches a fixed level sooner on its slope: the amplitude dependence.

Step 6

Direction finding

Amplitude comparison (the cheap, wide-coverage method). Four or six broad antennas point in different directions around the platform. A pulse arrives stronger in the antenna facing it; the ratio of the two strongest gives the angle, like monopulse (R6 Step 4) but with very broad beams. Typical accuracy: 5–15° rms, limited by antenna pattern differences and channel gain matching (1 dB of mismatch moves the answer by several degrees).

Phase interferometer (the accurate method). Two antennas a distance d apart receive the same wavefront. Unless it comes from straight ahead, it reaches one antenna slightly later, giving a phase difference:

Δφ = 360° · d · sin θ / λ    σθ ≈ σφ · λ / (2π · d · cos θ) (radians)    unambiguous over ±90° only if d ≤ λ/2
Worked: 10 GHz (λ = 3 cm), channel phase error 10° rms, θ near 0°AccuracyAmbiguity
d = λ/2 = 1.5 cm≈ 3.2°none
d = 4λ = 12 cm≈ 0.4°8 possible directions: needs extra baselines

The trade. A longer baseline turns the same phase error into a smaller angle error, but the phase wraps round more than once across the field of view and several angles fit the same measurement. Practical interferometers use several baselines: a short one to pick the right "wrap", a long one for accuracy, exactly like the multiple PRFs of R6 Step 2. The receiver channels must track each other in phase across frequency and temperature; every degree of mismatch is angle error.

Right, all the angles that would give the measured phase difference (teal ticks) and the true angle (orange). Things to check: (1) d = λ/2: one candidate, about 3° rms noise; (2) d = 4λ: eight candidates, each much sharper; (3) add a 20° channel phase error: the answer shifts by several degrees at d = λ/2 but much less at long baselines.

Step 7

Strong and weak together: dynamic range

A wideband receiver hears everything at once: a friendly radar 1 km away at −10 dBm and a distant one at −70 dBm, in the same instant. Two problems follow (Modules 01, 05, 07):

  • Intermodulation. Two strong signals at f1 and f2 in a nonlinear LNA or mixer create third-order products at 2f1 − f2 and 2f2 − f1, which look like real radars that do not exist. The spur-free dynamic range (SFDR = ⅔ × (IIP3 − noise floor)) says how strong two signals can be before their products rise above the noise.
  • Masking. The strong pulse saturates the ADC or a channel; a weaker pulse arriving at the same moment is lost. Digital receivers specify instantaneous dynamic range: how weak a second signal can be and still be measured next to a strong one.

Worked: noise floor −69 dBm (2 GHz, Step 2), IIP3 = +20 dBm: SFDR = ⅔ × (20 + 69) = 59 dB. Two signals each 59 dB above the noise (−10 dBm) produce products just at the noise floor. Stronger than that and false signals appear.

Step 8

Where your components show up in a listening receiver

Where your component shows up

  • Antennas: wideband (spirals, horns), with matched patterns for amplitude DF and stable phase centres for interferometry.
  • Limiter and front-end filters: survive nearby transmitters; their loss adds to the noise figure (Step 2).
  • LNA: noise figure sets sensitivity; IP3 sets the spur-free dynamic range (Step 7).
  • Mixers and LOs (superhet, channelised): spurs, image rejection, LO switching speed for swept receivers (Step 3).
  • IFM discriminator / delay lines: frequency accuracy per pulse.
  • Detectors and video amplifiers: video bandwidth sets rise time and TOA/PW accuracy (Step 5).
  • ADC and FPGA: instantaneous bandwidth, dynamic range for simultaneous signals, PDW generation in real time.
  • Channel matching: gain and phase tracking between DF channels sets angle accuracy (Step 6).
  • Clock and timestamping: TOA resolution, needed for Part 5's PRI analysis.
