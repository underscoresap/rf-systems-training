ADC, DAC and sampling, from one sample to radar, ESM and jammer performance

The analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) is where your analogue chain ends and the digital receiver begins; the digital-to-analogue converter (DAC) is where a transmitter's waveform is born. Everything the analogue chain did right can be lost at this boundary: a tone can turn up at the wrong frequency, the noise floor can rise above your LNA's, a spur can become a false target, and a jammer can radiate its own images. This page builds the converter from first principles: what a sample is, why the sample rate limits the bandwidth, how the number of bits sets the noise, where spurs come from, how the clock's jitter caps the signal-to-noise ratio, how much gain must sit in front of the ADC, and what a DAC's hold does to the output spectrum. Then it traces each converter number to radar dynamic range, ESM sensitivity, SAR data and jammer purity. Every term is defined before it is used, every step has worked numbers, and every datasheet line is traced from a design choice to the system effect.

Step 0

Where the converter sits in each chain, and what it is asked to do

You already know

A receiver chain amplifies, filters and frequency-converts a signal until it is at a convenient intermediate frequency (IF) or at baseband (centred on 0 Hz). You know noise figure, gain, compression and spurs from the LNA, filter and mixer modules. What this page adds is the last block of that chain: the box that turns a voltage into a stream of numbers, and its mirror image in the transmitter that turns numbers back into a voltage.

Definitions used on this page

ADC (analogue-to-digital converter): a circuit that, at regular instants, measures its input voltage and outputs that value as a binary number. Each measurement is a sample.

DAC (digital-to-analogue converter): the reverse: it receives a number at regular instants and outputs the matching voltage, holding it until the next number arrives.

Sample rate fs (samples per second, written MSPS or GSPS for millions or billions per second): how many samples are taken each second. The time between samples is the sample period Ts = 1/fs. Example: 100 MSPS means one sample every 10 ns.

Resolution N (bits): how many binary digits each sample has. N bits can express 2N different values. Example: 12 bits gives 4 096 values.

Digital receiver: the processing after the ADC (filtering, frequency shifting, FFTs, pulse compression, detection) done as arithmetic on the samples instead of with analogue parts.

In every chain the converter is asked the same three questions. Bandwidth: how wide a slice of spectrum can it see at once? That is set by the sample rate (Steps 1 and 2). Noise: how small a signal can it see? That is set by the number of bits, its own noise and the clock (Steps 3, 5, 6 and 7). Spurs: how strong a signal can it see without inventing signals that are not there? That is set by its linearity and, in fast converters, by mismatch between interleaved cores (Step 4). For a DAC the questions are mirrored: how clean is the waveform it produces, and where do its copies (images) land (Step 8)?

What goes wrong with the simplest view

The simplest view is "the ADC is a fast voltmeter: pick the most bits and the fastest rate you can buy". Three numbers show why that fails. (1) A 16-bit ADC promises 98 dB of signal-to-noise ratio on paper, but sampled with a clock that wanders by 500 fs (femtoseconds, 10−15 s) it delivers only 50 dB on a 1 GHz input: the extra bits are wasted (Step 6). (2) A 100 MSPS ADC fed with an unfiltered 70 MHz interferer shows it at 30 MHz, right on top of the wanted signal, with no way to tell them apart afterwards (Step 1). (3) A radar ADC with 66 dB SNR, used with 40 dB of processing gain, sees a spur only 75 dB below the clutter as a target 15 dB above its noise floor: a false alarm on every scan (Step 9).

Step 1

Sampling from first principles: the Nyquist rate, aliasing and the anti-alias filter

You already know

From the mixer module: multiplying a signal by a tone of frequency fLO shifts it to f ± fLO. Sampling turns out to be the same thing done with a train of very short pulses: it multiplies the input by every multiple of fs at once.

Definitions

Nyquist frequency fs/2: half the sample rate. The highest frequency that can be represented without ambiguity. At 100 MSPS it is 50 MHz.

Nyquist rate 2·fmax: the lowest sample rate that can represent a signal whose highest frequency is fmax. A 0 to 30 MHz signal needs at least 60 MSPS.

Alias: a tone at f, sampled at fs, gives exactly the same samples as a tone at |f − k·fs| for any whole number k. The lowest of these, between 0 and fs/2, is where it appears in the digital data. That apparent frequency is the alias.

Anti-alias filter: a low-pass (or band-pass) filter in front of the ADC that attenuates everything that would alias onto the wanted band. Its passband edge fmax is the highest wanted frequency; its cut-off fc is where it is 3 dB down; its order (number of poles) sets how fast it rolls off.

dBFS (decibels relative to full scale): a level measured against the largest sine wave the ADC can take without clipping. 0 dBFS is that full-scale sine; −10 dBFS is 10 dB below it.

Why the limit exists: a sample is only a value at one instant. Draw a 30 MHz sine and a 70 MHz sine and sample both every 10 ns (100 MSPS). At every sample instant the 70 MHz sine has advanced 0.7 of a cycle, which is the same as going back 0.3 of a cycle: the samples are the samples of a 30 MHz sine, with the phase reversed. Nothing in the data can tell them apart. In general the apparent frequency is

falias = | f − k·fs |, with k the whole number that brings the result into 0 … fs/2Example at fs = 100 MSPS: 30 MHz → 30 MHz; 70 MHz → |70 − 100| = 30 MHz; 130 MHz → |130 − 100| = 30 MHz; 170 MHz → |170 − 200| = 30 MHz. Four different inputs, one output.

So the sample rate must be at least twice the highest frequency present (the Nyquist criterion), and "present" means everything that reaches the ADC pins, not just what you want. An interferer at 70 MHz, or the third harmonic of your own IF amplifier, aliases into the wanted band and is then indistinguishable from a real signal. Only an analogue filter before the ADC can remove it; once sampled, it is too late.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

Naive approach 1: sample at exactly the Nyquist rate with no filter. A 0 to 30 MHz radar IF sampled at 60 MSPS has no room for any filter: the filter would have to pass 30 MHz and block 30.001 MHz. Naive approach 2: sample at 100 MSPS and fit a modest filter. Suppose an interferer at 70 MHz arrives at −10 dBFS, and the system needs every alias below −80 dBFS (that is, 70 dB of rejection at 70 MHz) so that it stays under the noise of a 12-bit converter in a narrow channel. A 5-pole Butterworth filter with fc = 35 MHz gives only 30.1 dB at 70 MHz, so the alias appears at 30 MHz at −40.1 dBFS: 40 dB too strong. The interferer is now a convincing signal.

One more definition

Oversampling ratio: fs / (2·fmax). At 100 MSPS for a 30 MHz signal it is 1.67; at 200 MSPS it is 3.33. The transition band of the anti-alias filter runs from fmax to fs − fmax: 30 to 70 MHz at 100 MSPS, 30 to 170 MHz at 200 MSPS.

How to choose the sample rate and the anti-alias filter

(1) Write down the wanted band (0 to fmax) and the strongest signal that can arrive outside it, with its level in dBFS. (2) Write down how far below full scale an alias must be (often "below the noise in the narrowest channel"). The difference is the rejection needed at fs − fmax. (3) Pick fs so that the transition band, fmax to fs − fmax, is wide enough for a filter you can build with acceptable loss, droop and group delay. Raising fs widens the transition band and simplifies the filter, but costs power, data rate and, as Step 6 shows, makes clock jitter matter more for nothing. (4) Keep the filter's passband droop at fmax small (well under 1 dB) or correct it digitally.

Step 2

Nyquist zones and bandpass (under)sampling

You already know

From Step 1: a tone at f appears at |f − k·fs|. From the mixer module: a low-side LO keeps the spectrum the right way round and a high-side LO turns it over. Sampling does both, depending on where the signal sits.

Definitions

Nyquist zone n: the frequency strip from (n − 1)·fs/2 to n·fs/2. At 100 MSPS zone 1 is 0 to 50 MHz, zone 2 is 50 to 100 MHz, zone 3 is 100 to 150 MHz, and so on. Every zone folds onto zone 1.

Spectral inversion: signals in even zones (2, 4, …) appear mirrored in zone 1: the upper edge of the band becomes the lower edge. Odd zones keep their orientation.

Bandpass sampling (undersampling): deliberately sampling a band-limited signal in zone 2 or higher, at a rate far below twice its highest frequency, so that the ADC does the job of the last mixer. It works only if the whole band sits inside one zone.

Analogue input bandwidth: the frequency at which the ADC's own sampling circuit (track-and-hold) has lost 3 dB. An ADC can only undersample a zone that lies inside this bandwidth.

Why it works: Step 1 showed that the samples cannot tell 30 MHz from 130 MHz. Usually that is a problem; here it is a tool. If a band-pass filter guarantees that only 120 to 140 MHz reaches the ADC, then everything in the samples between 20 and 40 MHz must have come from 120 to 140 MHz. The ADC has shifted the band down by fs, exactly as a mixer with a 100 MHz LO would, and the IF stage and mixer can be removed.

valid fs for a band fL … fH (width B = fH − fL): 2·fH/n ≤ fs ≤ 2·fL/(n − 1), for whole n from 1 to ⌊fH/B⌋Example: a 70 MHz IF, 20 MHz wide (60 to 80 MHz). n = 2: 80 ≤ fs ≤ 120 MSPS. n = 3: 53.3 ≤ fs ≤ 60 MSPS. n = 4: 40 ≤ fs ≤ 40 MSPS (one value, no margin at all). n = 1 is ordinary sampling: fs ≥ 160 MSPS.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

Naive approach 1: "a 60 to 80 MHz band needs 160 MSPS". It works, but it buys twice the sample rate needed, and twice the data. Naive approach 2: pick a rate halfway between, say 70 MSPS. The zone edge is then at 70 MHz, right in the middle of the band: 60 to 70 MHz (zone 2) appears inverted at 0 to 10 MHz, and 70 to 80 MHz (zone 3) appears upright also at 0 to 10 MHz. The two halves of the band land on top of each other and cannot be separated. Naive approach 3: pick 40 MSPS, the lowest valid rate. The band now fills zone 4 exactly, edge to edge, so the band-pass filter must cut from full pass to full stop in zero hertz: impossible to build.

How to choose the sample rate for bandpass sampling

(1) Fix the band fL to fH including the band-pass filter's transition bands, not just the signal. (2) List the valid windows from the equation for n = 1 up to ⌊fH/B⌋. (3) Pick a rate inside a window, preferably in its middle, so the band has guard bands on both sides for the filter skirts and for drift. (4) Check that the ADC's analogue input bandwidth exceeds fH and that the jitter budget at fH is met (Step 6). (5) Note whether the band is inverted; the digital receiver simply flips it back.

Step 3

Quantisation and resolution: the ideal SNR = 6.02·N + 1.76 dB, and ENOB

You already know

Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is signal power divided by noise power, in dB. You know thermal noise from the LNA module. This step adds a second kind of noise that exists even in a perfect, noiseless converter: the rounding error.

Definitions

Full-scale range (FSR): the peak-to-peak input voltage the ADC can code. Example: 2 Vpp, from −1 V to +1 V.

LSB (least significant bit): the voltage step between two adjacent codes, q = FSR/2N. 12 bits over 2 Vpp: q = 2/4 096 = 488 µV.

Quantisation error: the difference between the true input and the code's voltage. With a busy input it is spread evenly between −q/2 and +q/2, so its rms (root-mean-square) value is q/√12. For 12 bits: 488/3.46 = 141 µV rms.

Quantisation noise: that error treated as a noise. It is noise-like only if the input is busy; if the input repeats exactly, the error repeats too and becomes spurs (harmonics) instead.

SINAD (signal to noise and distortion): signal power over everything else (noise plus harmonics and spurs). ENOB (effective number of bits): the N of an ideal converter with the same SINAD, ENOB = (SINAD − 1.76)/6.02.

Dither: a small random noise added before the ADC on purpose, to keep the quantisation error random when the input itself is not busy.

Deriving the famous formula needs only two numbers. A full-scale sine has a peak of FSR/2, so its rms value is FSR/(2√2). The quantisation noise is q/√12 = FSR/(2N·√12). Divide and convert to dB:

SNRideal = 20·log10[ (FSR/2√2) / (FSR/(2N√12)) ] = 20·log10(2N) + 20·log10(√1.5) = 6.02·N + 1.76 dBEach extra bit halves q and buys 6.02 dB. For 12 bits: 6.02 × 12 + 1.76 = 74.0 dB; check: 20·log(0.7071 V / 141 µV) = 74.0 dB. The noise is spread from 0 to fs/2, which matters in Step 5. A signal at −A dBFS has an SNR A dB lower: 12 bits at −20 dBFS gives 54.0 dB.

A real ADC adds its own thermal noise, its nonlinearity and its clock jitter, so the datasheet SNR is lower than 6.02·N + 1.76. ENOB turns the measured SINAD back into "bits that are really working". A 12-bit ADC with SINAD 68.5 dB has ENOB = (68.5 − 1.76)/6.02 = 11.1 bits.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

Naive approach 1: "16 bits means 98 dB". The 16-bit converters on the market at 100 MSPS or more give about 76 to 80 dB SNR: the last three bits are buried in the converter's own noise. Their value is that the quantisation step is far below that noise, so the noise is smooth and well behaved, not that the noise is 98 dB down. Naive approach 2: a small, clean tone with no noise. Feed an 8-bit ADC a sine at exactly fs/32. Every 32 samples the same codes repeat, so the quantisation error repeats too: instead of a flat floor at −49.9 dBc spread over 2 048 FFT bins, the error collects in a few harmonics only about 50 to 60 dB below the signal. In a radar or ESM receiver those are false signals. Naive approach 3: a signal far below full scale. At −60 dBFS an 8-bit converter uses barely one code step: the output is a crude square wave, rich in harmonics.

Dither is the fix for the second and third naive cases: added noise breaks up the repeating pattern and turns the harmonics back into flat noise. It is not free. How to choose the resolution

(1) Find the instantaneous dynamic range the system needs: the strongest signal that must not clip, minus the weakest signal that must be seen, per sample bandwidth (Steps 5, 9). (2) Look up the datasheet SNR and SFDR, not the bit count: they are what is delivered. (3) Make sure the ADC's input sees some noise (at least about one LSB rms, Step 7) so the quantisation error stays random. (4) Do not pay for bits below the noise: going from 14 to 16 bits typically buys 6 dB of SNR, not 12.

Step 4

SFDR, harmonics and interleaving spurs

You already know

From the LNA and mixer modules: a nonlinear stage creates harmonics (2f, 3f, …) and intermodulation products, and spurious-free dynamic range compares the signal with the strongest of them. An ADC is a nonlinear stage too; Step 1 adds that its harmonics then alias.

Definitions

HD2, HD3 (second and third harmonic distortion): the level of the converter's own 2f and 3f products, in dBc (dB relative to the carrier, the wanted tone). Typical: −75 to −90 dBc.

SFDR (spurious-free dynamic range): the ratio between the wanted tone and the largest other single tone in the output spectrum, whatever its cause. Quoted in dBc (relative to the tone) or dBFS (relative to full scale; the two differ by the tone's back-off).

Spur: an unwanted single-frequency line in the output. Unlike noise it does not spread out: it sits in one FFT bin.

Interleaving: building a fast ADC from M slower cores, each running at fs/M and taking every M-th sample in turn. Four 250 MSPS cores make a 1 GSPS converter.

Core mismatch: the cores are never identical. They differ in offset (a constant voltage added, in mV), gain (in %) and timing skew (each core samples slightly early or late, in ps).

Harmonics alias like any other tone. At fs = 100 MSPS a 30 MHz input has HD2 at 60 MHz, which appears at 40 MHz, and HD3 at 90 MHz, which appears at 10 MHz. The 4th (120 MHz) appears at 20 MHz and the 5th (150 MHz) at 50 MHz. In a wideband ESM receiver every one of those is a candidate false emitter.

Interleaving adds a new family. If the cores' offsets differ, the output carries a pattern that repeats every M samples whatever the input: a fixed spur at fs/M (and its multiples). If gains or timing differ, the wanted tone is modulated by that repeating pattern, and copies (images) of the input appear at k·fs/M ± fin. For two cores:

gain spur ≈ 20·log10(ΔG/2) dBc; skew spur ≈ 20·log10(π·fin·Δt) dBc; both at fs/2 − fin; offset spur at fs/2, ≈ 20·log10(ΔVos/Vpeak,FS) − 3 dBFSExample, 2 cores, fs 1 GSPS, fin 203 MHz: ΔG = 0.1 % → 20·log(0.0005) = −66.0 dBc; Δt = 1 ps → 20·log(π × 203×106 × 10−12) = −63.9 dBc. The two add in power (they are 90° apart) to −61.8 dBc at 500 − 203 = 297 MHz. An offset difference of 0.5 mV on a 1 V peak full scale gives −66.0 − 3.0 = −69.0 dBFS at 500 MHz (the 3 dB is because a tone exactly at fs/2 has no partner at −fs/2 to share its power with).

The skew term grows by 6 dB for every doubling of input frequency: a skew that is harmless at 100 MHz may dominate at 1 GHz. This is why interleaved converters carry background calibration that trims offset, gain and timing while running.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

Naive approach 1: "choose the ADC on SNR". SNR deliberately excludes harmonics and spurs. A converter with 64 dB SNR and 50 dB SFDR has a spur 14 dB above its total noise; after the processing gain of Step 5 the noise in each bin falls by 30 dB or more, but the spur does not, so it stands out like a real signal. Naive approach 2: an uncalibrated interleaved ADC. With 1 % gain mismatch and 5 ps skew at a 2 GHz input, the image spur is at −46.0 dBc from gain and −30.1 dBc from skew: a 10 GSPS converter used this way has the spurious performance of a 5-bit one.

Step 5

Full scale, noise spectral density in dBFS/Hz, and processing gain

You already know

From the LNA module: thermal noise is −174 dBm/Hz at 290 K, and noise in a bandwidth B is that plus 10·log10(B). Step 3: the converter's SNR is measured over its whole Nyquist band, 0 to fs/2. This step turns that SNR into a density, so it can be compared with your analogue noise.

Definitions

Full-scale power PFS (dBm): the power of a 0 dBFS sine at the ADC input, set by the input range and impedance. Example: 2 Vpp differential into 100 Ω is (0.707 V)²/100 Ω = 5 mW = +7 dBm; this page uses +4 dBm as an illustrative value.

Noise spectral density (NSD): the converter's noise per hertz. Because the SNR's noise is spread over fs/2: NSD = −SNR − 10·log10(fs/2) in dBFS/Hz, and NSD + PFS in dBm/Hz.

ADC noise figure: how many dB the ADC's NSD (in dBm/Hz) lies above −174 dBm/Hz: NFADC = NSDdBm/Hz + 174.

Processing gain: the SNR improvement from keeping only the bandwidth the signal needs. Filtering digitally to a bandwidth B gives 10·log10(fs/(2B)). An FFT of K points splits 0 to fs/2 into K/2 bins, so the noise per bin is 10·log10(K/2) below the total.

Decimation: filtering digitally to a narrower band and then keeping fewer samples (a digital down-converter, DDC, does this with a frequency shift first).

NSD = −SNR − 10·log10(fs/2) dBFS/Hz; NFADC = PFS + NSD + 174 dB; SNR in B = SNR + 10·log10(fs/2B)Example: SNR 70 dB at 500 MSPS: NSD = −70 − 84.0 = −154.0 dBFS/Hz; with PFS +4 dBm that is −150.0 dBm/Hz, so NFADC = 24.0 dB. In a 5 MHz channel: SNR = 70 + 17.0 = 87.0 dB. A 4 096-point FFT puts the noise per bin at −70 − 33.1 = −103.1 dBFS.

NFADC of 24 dB looks terrible next to an LNA's 2 dB, and it is: an ADC is a noisy stage. That is why Step 7 puts gain in front of it, exactly as an LNA is put in front of a mixer. Processing gain is not free extra sensitivity for the system: it only reveals that the noise per hertz was low all along. Two converters with the same NSD give the same SNR in the same channel, whatever their quoted SNR and rate.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

Naive approach 1: "the higher-SNR converter is the quieter one". Compare three illustrative parts. A 14-bit, 250 MSPS ADC with SNR 72 dB has NSD −153.0 dBFS/Hz. A 12-bit, 1 GSPS ADC with SNR 65 dB has −152.0 dBFS/Hz, only 1 dB worse in any given channel despite a 7 dB lower SNR and two fewer bits, while seeing four times the bandwidth. Naive approach 2: "processing gain also lowers spurs". It does not: a spur is a single frequency and stays in one bin. With a 4 096-point FFT the noise per bin falls to −103.1 dBFS but a −90 dBFS spur stays at −90 dBFS, now 13 dB above the floor.

Step 6

Aperture jitter and the SNR limit it sets

You already know

From the synthesiser and phase-noise module: phase noise integrated over a band gives rms phase error, and rms phase error divided by 2π·f is rms timing jitter. Here the clock's jitter becomes noise on the samples.

Definitions

Sampling instant: the moment the ADC's track-and-hold stops following the input and holds a value. It is triggered by an edge of the sample clock.

Clock jitter tc (rms, in fs or ps): the random variation of the clock edges from their ideal times, inherited from the clock source and its distribution.

Aperture jitter ta: the random variation added inside the ADC itself between the clock edge and the actual hold. Typical datasheet values: 50 to 150 fs rms.

Total jitter tj = √(ta² + tc²): independent random timings add in power.

Slew rate: how fast the input voltage changes. A sine of amplitude A at frequency f changes at up to 2π·f·A volts per second, at its zero crossings.

Why it matters: if the sample is taken t seconds late, the value is wrong by (slope × t). For a sine the rms slope is 2π·f·A/√2, so the rms error is 2π·f·tj times the signal's rms value. Divide and convert to dB:

SNRjitter = −20·log10(2π·fin·tj); SNRtotal = −10·log10(10−SNR0/10 + 10−SNRjitter/10)fin is the input (analogue) frequency, not the sample rate. Example: 100 fs at 100 MHz gives 84.0 dB; at 1 GHz, 64.0 dB. Combined with a converter whose low-frequency SNR (SNR0) is 70 dB: 69.8 dB at 100 MHz but 63.0 dB at 1 GHz. From phase noise: tj = √(2·10A/10)/(2π·fclk), with A the integrated single-sideband phase noise in dBc: −70 dBc on a 500 MHz clock gives 142 fs.

This is the reason the input frequency, not the sample rate, sets the clock requirement. It also shows the hidden cost of undersampling (Step 2): sampling a 1 GHz IF at 200 MSPS needs the clock jitter of a 1 GHz input, ten times stricter than sampling 100 MHz.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

Naive approach: take the sample clock from the FPGA (field-programmable gate array) that processes the data, because it is already there. An FPGA-generated clock typically has around 5 ps of jitter. At a 70 MHz IF that limits the SNR to 53.2 dB, whatever the converter: a 16-bit ADC now performs like an 8.5-bit one. At a 1 GHz input the limit is 30.1 dB.

Step 7

Matching the ADC to the analogue chain: gain, noise above the LSB, and the ADC in the NF cascade

You already know

From the LNA module: Friis's formula, Ftotal = F1 + (F2 − 1)/G1, where F is noise factor (the linear form of noise figure) and G the linear gain before the stage. Step 5 gave the ADC a noise figure. Now the ADC simply joins the cascade as the last stage.

Definitions

Analogue chain (front end): everything between the antenna and the ADC: LNA, filters, mixers, IF amplifiers. Here summarised by its noise figure NF1 and its total gain G.

Noise margin: how far the analogue noise density at the ADC input (−174 + NF1 + G dBm/Hz) lies above the ADC's own NSD in dBm/Hz.

Clip level at the antenna: the input power that drives the ADC to full scale: PFS − G dBm. Any stronger signal clips: the ADC's output is stuck at its top or bottom code.

Headroom: clip level minus the largest signal expected (clutter, a nearby emitter, own-transmitter leakage, a jammer).

Noise in LSB rms: the analogue noise at the ADC input, over 0 to fs/2, expressed in LSBs (Step 3). About 1 LSB rms or more keeps the quantisation error random.

Instantaneous dynamic range: the clip level minus the noise floor in a stated bandwidth, here 1 MHz, both referred to the antenna.

Ftotal = F1 + (FADC − 1)/G; clip at antenna = PFS − G; noise margin = (−174 + NF1 + G) − NSDdBm/HzExample: NF1 3 dB, ADC from Step 5 (NFADC 24.0 dB, PFS +4 dBm, 500 MSPS, 12 bits), G = 30 dB: F = 2.00 + 250/1 000 = 2.25, NF = 3.52 dB; analogue noise −141 dBm/Hz against the ADC's −150.0, a 9.0 dB margin; clip at −26 dBm; noise 1.29 LSB rms; dynamic range in 1 MHz 84.5 dB.

The trade is now visible: gain buries the ADC's noise under the front end's (good for sensitivity) but brings the clip level down by the same dB (bad for strong signals). Every dB of gain above "just enough" is a dB of dynamic range given away.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

Naive approach 1: "the ADC is the last stage, so its noise doesn't matter". With only G = 10 dB in front, the system NF is 14.3 dB instead of 3 dB: the LNA's careful 3 dB is lost and the radar's range falls by a factor 1011.3/40 ≈ 1.9. Naive approach 2: "more gain is always better". With G = 60 dB the NF is 3.00 dB, but the ADC clips at −56 dBm at the antenna. Ground clutter or a nearby emitter at −40 dBm saturates the converter, and a clipped ADC spreads the strong signal's energy across the whole spectrum, hiding everything.

How to choose the gain in front of the ADC

(1) Start at the top: the largest signal that must not clip, Pmax, plus a few dB of back-off (crest factor, fading). That fixes the maximum gain: Gmax = PFS − back-off − Pmax. (2) Start at the bottom: the gain that puts the analogue noise 6 to 10 dB above NSD: Gmin ≈ NSDdBm/Hz + 174 − NF1 + 6…10. (3) If Gmin ≤ Gmax, choose near Gmin to keep headroom. (4) If not, the converter cannot cover the dynamic range at once: use a lower-NSD ADC, sensitivity time control (radar, gain that rises with range), automatic gain control, or split the band into channels.

Step 8

DAC sinc roll-off, images and reconstruction filters

You already know

From Step 1: a sampled signal has copies at every k·fs ± f. In an ADC those copies are folded in before sampling, so the anti-alias filter must remove them first. In a DAC they come out afterwards, as real analogue tones at the output, so a filter must remove them after the converter. This is the reconstruction filter, the anti-alias filter's mirror image.

Definitions

DAC clock fclk: the rate at which the DAC changes its output (its sample rate).

Zero-order hold, NRZ (non-return-to-zero): the normal DAC output. Each value is held for the full clock period, giving a staircase.

Sinc roll-off: holding each value for one period acts as a filter with response |sin(π·x)/(π·x)|, x = f/fclk. It is 0 dB at 0 Hz, −3.9 dB at fclk/2 and zero at fclk, 2·fclk, …

Image: a copy of the wanted output tone at k·fclk ± f, weighted by the sinc. The first image is at fclk − f.

RZ (return-to-zero): the output is held for half the period and returns to zero for the other half. Response 0.5·|sinc(x/2)|: 6 dB lower, but flatter, with its first null at 2·fclk.

Mix-mode (also called RF mode): the second half of each period outputs the inverted value. Response |sinc(x/2)·sin(π·x/2)|: weak near 0 Hz, strongest around 0.7 to 1.5·fclk, so an image in the second or third zone can be used as the output.

Interpolation: computing extra samples digitally (×2, ×4) so the DAC runs at I times the data rate fd. A digital filter removes the images that would have been at fd − f, so the first remaining image moves to I·fd − f.

Reconstruction filter: the analogue low-pass (or band-pass) filter after the DAC that removes the images. Inverse-sinc filter: a digital pre-emphasis that cancels the sinc droop across the band.

HNRZ(f) = |sin(π·f/fclk)/(π·f/fclk)|; first image at fclk − f, level 20·log[H(fclk − f)/H(f)] dBc before filteringExample, fclk 1 GSPS, f = 400 MHz: droop at 400 MHz = 20·log(sinc 0.4) = −2.42 dB; image at 600 MHz = 20·log(sinc 0.6) = −5.94 dB, so the image is only 3.52 dB below the wanted tone. In mix-mode the same tone is at −5.19 dB and the 600 MHz image at −3.17 dB: the image is now the stronger one.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

Naive approach: an NRZ DAC at 1 GSPS producing 400 MHz, with a 3-pole Butterworth low-pass at 450 MHz. The image at 600 MHz starts 3.52 dB below the wanted tone; the filter takes 1.74 dB off the wanted tone and 8.21 dB off the image. The image leaves at only −10.0 dBc. In a jammer or exciter that is a second transmitter on a frequency nobody asked for, at a tenth of the power of the first. A second, quieter fault: the 2.42 dB droop at 400 MHz makes a wideband waveform (a chirp from 0 to 400 MHz) lose amplitude towards its top edge, which raises the range sidelobes after pulse compression (module 04, Step 4).

Interpolation is the DAC's version of oversampling (Step 1): it widens the gap between the wanted tone and its first image, so a simple filter suffices and the droop shrinks. The cost is a faster DAC, more power and a faster clock with the jitter requirements of Step 6 (a DAC's clock jitter also becomes noise on the output, with the same −20·log(2π·f·tj) law). Mix-mode trades the first zone for the second and third, which lets a 1 GSPS DAC produce 600 to 1 400 MHz without an upconverter, as long as a band-pass filter removes the other zones.

How to choose the DAC rate, mode and reconstruction filter

(1) Decide the output band and which zone to use: zone 1 with NRZ for baseband or low IF, zone 2 or 3 with mix-mode for direct IF or RF output. (2) Pick fclk (data rate × interpolation) so the nearest image lies far enough away for a filter you can build: the same octave counting as Step 1. (3) Size the filter for the image rejection the spectrum mask needs (often −60 to −80 dBc for a transmitter), adding the sinc's own help. (4) Correct the droop across the band with an inverse-sinc filter if the waveform needs flat amplitude (chirps, multi-tone jamming). (5) Check the clock jitter at the highest output frequency.

Step 9

System effect 1: radar dynamic range and clutter

You already know

From the radar pages R3 and R4: a target is declared when its echo exceeds a threshold set a fixed number of dB above the noise (about 13 dB for a high probability of detection with few false alarms). From Step 5: processing gain lowers the noise in each output cell, while a coherent signal keeps its level.

Definitions

Clutter: echoes from things the radar does not want (ground, sea, rain). In the same range cell as a target, clutter can be 60 to 90 dB stronger than the target.

Pulse-Doppler processing: pulse compression followed by an FFT across many pulses. Stationary clutter collects at zero Doppler (zero frequency shift); a moving target collects in its own Doppler bin, where only noise competes with it, provided the ADC has not mixed the clutter into that bin.

Radar processing gain PG: the total coherent gain of pulse compression and Doppler FFT, for the target relative to the noise. Example: time-bandwidth product 100 (20 dB) and a 128-pulse FFT (21.1 dB): PG ≈ 41 dB.

Thermal noise at the ADC: the analogue chain's noise power over 0 to fs/2, in dBFS. It is fixed by the gain chosen in Step 7, so it does not change when only the ADC is swapped.

Spur not at the clutter's Doppler: harmonics of a stationary clutter echo stay at zero Doppler and are removed with the clutter. But interleaving images, clock spurs that modulate the clutter, and intermodulation between clutter and strong moving echoes (rain, large aircraft) land in Doppler bins where targets are sought. Their level is set by the ADC's SFDR below the clutter.

Subclutter visibility (SCV): how much weaker than the clutter in its cell a target may be and still be detected.

noise after processing = 10·log(10−SNRADC/10 + 10Nth/10) − PG; target SNR = Ptarget − noise after processing; spur-to-noise = (Pclutter − SFDR) − noise after processingExample (all dBFS): clutter −6, target −80, thermal −57, ADC SNR 66 (noise −66), SFDR 75, PG 40. Noise per sample −56.5; after PG −96.5; target SNR 16.5 dB: detected. Spur at −6 − 75 = −81 dBFS: 15.5 dB above the noise after processing, over the 13 dB threshold: a false target in every scan.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

Naive approach 1: set the gain so the clutter sits at −1 dBFS "to use all the bits". Clutter fluctuates by several dB from scan to scan; peaks clip, and a clipped clutter echo spreads energy across every Doppler bin, burying targets. Naive approach 2: judge the ADC by its SNR. In the example, raising the SNR from 66 to 72 dB improves the target's SNR by only 0.4 dB (thermal noise dominates), while the false target remains. Only raising the SFDR from 75 to 90 dBc removes it.

(4) If the requirement cannot be met, use sensitivity time control (less gain at short range where clutter is strongest), a cleaner clock, or a calibrated converter.

Step 10

System effect 2: ESM bandwidth against sensitivity, SAR data, and jammer waveform purity

You already know

ESM (electronic support measures) receivers listen for other people's emitters across a wide band, for example 2 to 18 GHz. From Step 5: a wider sample rate spreads the converter's noise thinner per hertz only if the SNR holds up; from Step 7: the gain in front of the ADC is limited by the strongest signal.

Definitions

Instantaneous bandwidth (IBW): the width of spectrum the receiver sees at one moment. For a real-sampling ADC, about 0.4·fs once the anti-alias filter's transition band is allowed for.

Probability of intercept (POI), simplified: for a short emission at an unknown frequency in a 16 GHz band, the chance that it falls inside the IBW: IBW/16 GHz. A wider IBW sees more of the band at once.

Detection bandwidth Bdet: the bandwidth the digital receiver uses to detect a pulse, matched to the pulse (about 1/pulse width: a 0.1 µs pulse needs about 10 MHz).

Required SNR: the SNR in Bdet needed to declare a pulse (here 15 dB).

Sensitivity: the weakest pulse detected: noise density at the antenna + 10·log(Bdet) + required SNR, where the noise density is −174 + system NF.

Converter classes (illustrative, full scale +4 dBm): A 16-bit 250 MSPS SNR 76 dB; B 14-bit 1 GSPS SNR 68 dB; C 12-bit 4 GSPS SNR 58 dB; D 8-bit 20 GSPS SNR 42 dB.

G = PFS − 3 dB − Pmax; NFsys = 10·log[F1 + (FADC − 1)/G]; S = −174 + NFsys + 10·log(Bdet) + SNRreqExample: NF1 6 dB, Pmax −20 dBm (so G = 21 dB), Bdet 10 MHz, SNRreq 15 dB. Class A: NFADC 21.0, NFsys 6.97, S = −82.0 dBm, IBW 100 MHz (0.6 % of 2 to 18 GHz). Class B: −81.5 dBm, 400 MHz (2.5 %). Class C: −80.0 dBm, 1.6 GHz (10 %). Class D: NFADC 36.0, NFsys 15.5, −73.5 dBm, 8 GHz (50 %). A thermal-only receiver (perfect ADC) would reach −83.0 dBm.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

Naive approach 1: "take the widest IBW, then nothing is missed". Class D sees half the band at once but is 9.5 dB less sensitive than a thermal-limited receiver; an ESM receiver's detection range on a radar falls as 10−ΔS/20, so 9.5 dB costs two thirds of the range. Naive approach 2: "add gain to recover the sensitivity". Every dB of gain lowers the strongest signal the receiver can take without clipping, and in a wide IBW the chance that a strong nearby emitter is in view at the same time is high: class D with G = 41 dB clips at −37 dBm.

SAR: bandwidth, data rate and bright targets

A SAR (synthetic aperture radar) forms an image by pulse-compressing each echo in range and then combining thousands of echoes in azimuth (along the flight path). Range resolution is c/(2B): a 300 MHz chirp gives 0.5 m. The converter must capture that bandwidth: complex (I/Q) sampling at about 1.2·B, 360 MSPS per channel, or real sampling above 2·B. With 12 bits per sample on I and Q the raw rate is 2 × 12 × 360×106 = 8.64 Gbit/s, which is why SAR systems compress the raw data, for example block-adaptive quantisation (BAQ), which rescales each block of samples to its own level and keeps only 3 to 4 bits. Coarse quantisation works because the raw echo is noise-like (thousands of scatterers add up) and the processing gain is large: a 20 µs, 300 MHz chirp has a time-bandwidth product of 6 000, 37.8 dB, before azimuth compression adds roughly another 30 dB. The ADC's danger in SAR is the bright point target (a ship, a building corner): if it clips, its compressed response gains sidelobes and ghosts that smear across darker neighbours. The I/Q pair's gain and phase match (module 05) sets the image's ghost level just as the IRR does in a receiver.

Jammer and exciter waveform purity

A DAC-based jammer synthesises its waveform digitally, so every DAC impurity is amplified and radiated. Suppose the jammer radiates 1 kW ERP (effective radiated power, +60 dBm). (1) A DAC spur at −70 dBc is radiated at +60 − 70 = −10 dBm ERP, a clean signal on a frequency the jammer never meant to use, possibly inside a friendly band. (2) The DAC's noise floor, say −160 dBc/Hz, gives +60 − 160 + 70 = −30 dBm ERP in any 10 MHz channel, which can desensitise the platform's own ESM receiver a few metres away. (3) An unfiltered NRZ image (Step 8) at −3.5 dBc is a second jammer at +56.5 dBm. (4) Sinc droop and filter ripple across a wideband noise or chirp waveform shape the jamming spectrum, wasting power where the victim is not listening. The jammer's purity budget therefore flows straight back to the DAC's SFDR, NSD, clock jitter and the reconstruction filter.

Glossary of terms introduced on this page

ADC
Analogue-to-digital converter: measures the input voltage at regular instants and outputs each value as a number.
Alias
The apparent frequency |f − k·fs| at which a tone at f appears after sampling.
Analogue input bandwidth
The input frequency at which the ADC's track-and-hold has lost 3 dB; limits the highest Nyquist zone usable.
Anti-alias filter
Analogue filter before the ADC that removes what would alias onto the wanted band; its stop band starts at fs − fmax.
Aperture jitter ta
Random timing uncertainty of the ADC's own sampling instant, in fs rms.
BAQ
Block-adaptive quantisation: SAR raw-data compression that rescales each block and keeps a few bits per sample.
Bandpass sampling
Sampling a band-limited signal in zone 2 or higher so that the ADC also frequency-converts it; also called undersampling.
Clipping
An input beyond full scale; the output sticks at the top or bottom code and the signal's energy spreads across the spectrum.
Clock jitter tc
Random variation of the sample clock's edges from their ideal times, in fs rms.
Clutter
Radar echoes from ground, sea or rain that compete with the target.
DAC
Digital-to-analogue converter: outputs a voltage for each number and holds it until the next.
dBc
Decibels relative to the carrier (the wanted tone).
dBFS
Decibels relative to full scale (a full-scale sine is 0 dBFS).
Decimation
Digital filtering to a narrower band, then keeping fewer samples.
Dither
Small noise added before the ADC so the quantisation error stays random.
ENOB
Effective number of bits: (SINAD − 1.76)/6.02.
FFT, bin
Fast Fourier transform of K samples; it splits 0 to fs/2 into K/2 bins of width fs/K.
Full-scale range (FSR)
Peak-to-peak input voltage the ADC can code.
Full-scale power PFS
Power of a 0 dBFS sine at the ADC input, in dBm.
HD2, HD3
Second and third harmonic distortion, in dBc.
Headroom
Clip level minus the largest expected signal.
IBW
Instantaneous bandwidth: the spectrum width a receiver sees at one moment; about 0.4·fs for real sampling.
Image (DAC)
Copy of the output tone at k·fclk ± f, weighted by the hold response.
Interleaving
Building a fast ADC from M slower cores taking samples in turn.
Interpolation
Computing extra samples so the DAC runs faster than the data, moving the first image away.
Inverse-sinc filter
Digital pre-emphasis that cancels the DAC's sinc droop.
LSB
Least significant bit: the voltage step between adjacent codes, FSR/2N.
Mix-mode
DAC mode that outputs the inverted value in the second half of each period, favouring zones 2 and 3.
MSPS, GSPS
Millions or billions of samples per second.
NFADC
ADC noise figure: PFS + NSD + 174 dB.
Noise margin
Analogue noise density at the ADC input minus the ADC's NSD, in dB.
NRZ
Non-return-to-zero (zero-order hold): each DAC value held for a full period.
NSD
Noise spectral density: −SNR − 10·log(fs/2) in dBFS/Hz.
Nyquist frequency
fs/2, the highest frequency represented without ambiguity.
Nyquist rate
2·fmax, the lowest sample rate for a signal up to fmax.
Nyquist zone
The strip (n − 1)·fs/2 to n·fs/2; even zones appear inverted.
Oversampling ratio
fs/(2·fmax).
POI (simplified)
Share of the band seen at once, IBW divided by the total band.
Processing gain
SNR improvement from keeping only the needed bandwidth: 10·log(fs/2B), or 10·log(K/2) per FFT bin.
Quantisation noise
The rounding error treated as noise, q/√12 rms when the input is busy.
Reconstruction filter
Analogue filter after a DAC that removes the images.
RZ
Return-to-zero: DAC value held for half a period; 6 dB lower, flatter response.
Sample rate fs
Samples per second; Ts = 1/fs between samples.
SFDR
Spurious-free dynamic range: wanted tone over the largest spur, in dBc or dBFS.
SINAD
Signal to noise and distortion: signal over everything else.
Sinc roll-off
The |sin(πx)/(πx)| response of a hold, x = f/fclk.
Skew
Timing difference between interleaved cores, in ps.
SNR
Signal-to-noise ratio; for an ADC measured over 0 to fs/2, excluding harmonics.
Spectral inversion
A band appearing mirrored after sampling from an even zone.
Subclutter visibility
How much weaker than the clutter in its cell a target may be and still be detected.

For the anti-alias filter, fmax to fs − fmax.
