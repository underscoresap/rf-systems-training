How SAR makes a picture of the ground

This page follows one job from start to finish: take a picture of one patch of land, detailed enough to see a car, at any hour and in any weather. It first shows why a camera cannot always do it and why an ordinary radar's picture is a blur. Then it shows how flying a small antenna past the land builds a "synthetic" antenna 300 m long. Every equation is solved with the same numbers, and the last steps show how the recorded amplitudes and phases are turned into the image, pixel by pixel. It builds on R1–R6: range resolution from bandwidth (R6), phase that turns with distance (R4 Step 6) and the matched filter (R6 Step 1).

Step 0

The job: a picture of one patch of land

In plain words

We want a picture of the patch of land below, taken from an aircraft 10 km away. It has to be sharp enough to see the shape of each house, and to show the two parked cars as two cars, not one blob. We also want it at night, through monsoon cloud and through smoke. Every step on this page uses this same patch and the same numbers.

The patch: 400 m along the flight track × 240 m across it. The aircraft flies left to right along the top edge, 10 km away, looking sideways at the patch. Its distance to each point is the "range". The two cars are parked 8 m apart (centre to centre); each is 4.6 m × 1.9 m.

Resolution

The resolution of an image is the smallest distance at which two points still appear as two separate points. Closer than that, they merge into one blob. An image has a resolution in each direction. Here the two directions are along the flight track (called azimuth) and across it, away from the aircraft (called range).

Step 1

First try: why not just use a camera?

In plain words

A camera from 10 km away would show the cars beautifully on a clear day. But it needs sunlight, and it is blinded by cloud, smoke and haze. A radar brings its own illumination, and its 3 cm waves go straight through cloud. The catch, which the rest of the page solves, is that a radar antenna of a sensible size gives a picture hundreds of metres blurry.

How a camera makes its picture. The lens collects light from every point of the ground and focuses each point to a small spot on the sensor. The spot cannot be smaller than diffraction allows. The finest angle a lens of diameter D can separate is

θmin = 1.22 · λ / D     detail on the ground = R · θmin

Radar, used the same way3 cm1 m antenna36.6 mrad366 m

Where the camera fails.

1. It is passive. It only sees light that the sun (or a lamp) puts on the ground. At night a visible camera sees nothing. A thermal camera sees the heat that objects give off themselves, so it works at night, but its longer wavelength makes it 18 times coarser (1.2 m instead of 6.7 cm).

2. Cloud. Cloud is made of water droplets about 5–20 µm across. That is the same size as, or larger than, the wavelength of light (0.5 µm) and heat (10 µm). Droplets that size scatter light very strongly, so a 1 km thick cloud blocks both cameras completely. A 3 cm radar wave is about 3 000 times larger than a droplet. Scattering by small particles falls as (size/λ)4, so the radar wave barely notices: a whole cloud costs about 0.1 dB at 10 GHz.

3. Smoke, haze and dust. These particles are 0.1–1 µm, so they scatter visible light strongly, heat less, and radar not at all.

4. Rain. Raindrops (1–5 mm) are closer to the radar wavelength. At 10 GHz, rain of 10 mm/h costs about 0.3 dB/km, so 5 km of rain costs about 3 dB there and back. The image gets dimmer but is still there. Lower-frequency radars (C band, L band) lose less.

5. No distance. A camera measures only direction. A radar measures distance directly, and very precisely, from the echo's delay.

So a radar has the right properties: it brings its own illumination, ignores weather and measures distance. Used the way a camera is used, though, the same diffraction formula with λ = 3 cm and a 1 m antenna gives 366 m. That would put the whole village in one pixel. That is the problem the rest of this page solves.

Step 2

What an aperture is, and why its size sets the detail

In plain words

The aperture is the opening that collects or sends the wave: the lens of a camera, the dish or the antenna of a radar. A bigger opening makes a narrower beam, and a narrower beam sees finer detail. What matters is not its size in metres but its size in wavelengths. A 10 cm lens is 182 000 wavelengths of light across. A 1 m radar antenna is only 33 wavelengths of 3 cm.

You already know

From R2: a beam's width is about λ/D radians, and its footprint at range R is R·λ/D wide. This step shows where that comes from, using the camera and the radar side by side.

Aperture

The aperture is the area of a sensor that actually collects (or radiates) the wave: the clear diameter of a lens or mirror, the diameter of a dish, or the length of an antenna or array. Its size D is measured in the direction in which you want detail. For detail along the flight track, D is the antenna's length along the track.

Why a bigger aperture gives a narrower beam. Think of the aperture as many small sources side by side (the dots). Straight ahead (θ = 0) every path to the far point is the same length, so all contributions add. At an angle θ, the far edge's path is longer than the near edge's by D·sin θ. When that extra path is exactly one wavelength, each point in the upper half has a partner in the lower half whose path is half a wavelength longer. The partners cancel in pairs and nothing arrives: the first null of the beam. So the beam's half-width to the first null is sin θ = λ/D. The −3 dB width of a uniformly lit aperture is 0.886 λ/D, about λ/D.

A circular lens gives 1.22 λ/D instead of λ/D (the Bessel-function pattern of the Antenna fundamentals page). A radar's beam footprint at range R is therefore about R·λ/D wide. A radar also uses its antenna twice, once to transmit and once to receive, which narrows the effective beam a little more; Step 4 uses that.

Step 3

A real-aperture radar: sharp in range, a blur along the track

In plain words

A radar on the aircraft looks sideways at the patch. It can tell distance very precisely from the echo delay, so the picture is sharp across the track: 1 m. Along the track it can only say "somewhere in my beam", and the beam is 300 m wide on the ground. Every house, and both cars, becomes a 300 m long smear. This is a "real-aperture" radar: its detail comes only from its real, physical antenna.

Why sideways. The radar looks to one side, not down. Looking straight down, a point to the left and a point to the right at the same distance would return at the same time and could not be told apart. Looking to one side, every distance corresponds to one strip of ground parallel to the track.

Definitions

Azimuth: the direction along the flight track. Range: the direction across it, away from the track.
Slant range R: the straight-line distance from radar to ground point, which is what the radar measures. Ground range: the distance along the ground.
Look angle: the angle of the beam from vertical at the radar. Incidence angle θi: the angle between the beam and the vertical at the ground. For an aircraft it is about the same as the look angle; for a satellite it is larger, because the Earth curves.
Swath: the width of ground imaged, between near range and far range.
Pixel: one range cell × one azimuth cell of the final image.

Try it: side view (flat Earth), radar on the left, looking to the right. The orange strip on the ground is the swath. Things to check: (1) default: incidence from 35° to 55°, and with 150 MHz the ground resolution is 1.7 m at near range and 1.2 m at far range; (2) look more steeply (smaller look angle): the swath moves in and the near-range ground resolution gets much coarser; (3) raise the height: the same angles cover a wider swath.

3a. Across the track (range): sharp, from timing

The radar sends a pulse and times the echoes. An echo from a point 1 m further away arrives 6.7 ns later (2 × 1 m / c). With pulse compression (R6 Step 1) or a dechirped FMCW signal (R5), two echoes can be separated when they are c/2B apart in slant range. The image is wanted in ground range, though. On flat ground one slant step δr covers a longer stretch of ground, δr / sin θi, and the steeper the beam looks down, the longer that stretch.

δrslant = c / 2B    δrground = δrslant / sin θi
B = 150 MHz (δrslant = 3×10⁸ / (2 × 150×10⁶) = 1 m)θi = 30°θi = 45° (our patch)θi = 60°

That is why SAR never looks straight down: as θi → 0 the ground resolution becomes infinitely coarse. Across the track, the patch is already fine: 1.41 m, enough to see a car's length.

3b. Along the track (azimuth): a blur as wide as the beam

Along the track there is no timing to help. Two points at the same range, one at the front of the beam and one at the back, send their echoes back at the same moment. The radar can only say that a point is "somewhere inside my beam right now". As the aircraft flies, it paints each echo at the position of the beam's centre, so a single point shows up in every pulse while the beam passes over it: a streak as long as the footprint.

The obvious fixes fail.

• A bigger antenna: 600 m cannot be carried by an aircraft, let alone a satellite.

• Fly closer: at 1 km range the blur is still 30 m, and the aircraft is now within reach of the ground and sees only a narrow strip.

• A higher frequency: at 100 GHz (3 mm) the blur at 10 km is still 30 m, and the air and rain absorb several dB per km.

A real aperture alone cannot do the job.

Step 4

The synthetic aperture: building a 300 m antenna by flying a 1 m one

In plain words

An antenna does not have to exist all at once. A 300 m array would be 300 m of small antennas, each catching the echo, with their signals added together with the right phases. SAR uses one small antenna and moves it along those 300 m, catching the echo at each position in turn. It records every echo's strength and phase, and adds them together afterwards in a computer, as if all the positions had been there at once.

Imagine walking past a lamp post, clapping at every step and recording the echo. As you approach, the echo comes back slightly sooner each clap; after you pass, slightly later. That pattern is unique to that post's position. SAR finds each point's pattern in the recording.

How long the synthetic antenna can be. A point of the patch can only contribute while it is inside the real beam. The beam's footprint is R·λ/D = 300 m long (Step 3), so the aircraft flies 300 m while any one point is lit. That flown distance is the longest synthetic aperture available for that point: L = R·λ/D = 300 m, flown in 3 s at 100 m/s.

Why the resolution comes out as D/2. A real array of length L gives a beam λ/L wide when it transmits from one place and receives on all elements: the path differences across it are one-way. In SAR, each position transmits and receives its own pulse, so each position's path difference counts twice, there and back. The synthetic beam is therefore half as wide, λ/(2L), and the azimuth resolution is

δa = R · λ / (2L) = R · λ / (2 · R·λ/D) = D / 2

For our patch that is 10 000 × 0.03 / 600 = 0.5 m. R and λ have cancelled. A point further away stays in the beam longer, so its synthetic aperture is longer by the same factor. A shorter real antenna gives a finer picture, because its wider beam lights each point for longer (Step 8 shows the price: a higher pulse rate).

Three conditions for it to work. (1) The ground stays still while the aircraft passes. (2) The radar's oscillator keeps its phase steady for the whole 3 s, so that phases recorded at the start and at the end can be compared (R4 Step 6). (3) The position of the antenna at every pulse is known to about 2 mm, a small fraction of the 3 cm wavelength (Step 6).

Lines 8–11 are the heart of SAR. Follow one point of the patch as the aircraft flies past, in four steps:

5a. How long the point is seen (lines 4–6). The point stays in the 300 m footprint while the aircraft flies 300 m, which takes 3 s.

5b. How the range to the point changes (line 8). At closest approach the range is R0 = 10 km. When the aircraft is a distance x along the track from that point, the range is √(R0² + x²) ≈ R0 + x²/(2R0). It shrinks while approaching and grows after passing: a parabola. At the edge of the 300 m aperture (x = 150 m) the extra range is 150² / 20 000 = 1.125 m.

5c. What that does to the phase (lines 9–11). From R4 Step 6: every λ/2 change of range turns the echo phase by 360°. 1.125 m of extra range is 2 × 1.125 / 0.03 = 75 cycles. So the echo phase follows the same parabola: it turns fast at the start, slows to zero at closest approach, then turns fast the other way. The rate of turning is the Doppler shift, so the Doppler frequency sweeps linearly from +100 Hz to −100 Hz as the point passes. That is a chirp, like the range chirp of R6 but in azimuth, with bandwidth Bd = 2V/D = 200 Hz.

5d. Compress it (line 7). A matched filter multiplies the recording by the conjugate of the expected phase history and sums. That collapses the azimuth chirp, just as it did in range: the point's echoes across all 300 m add in phase only at the point's own position. The time resolution is 1/Bd = 5 ms, and the aircraft moves V/Bd = 100 × 0.005 = 0.5 m in that time: δa = V / Bd = V / (2V/D) = D/2, the same answer as Step 4.

Why the phase correction matters: "unfocused" SAR (line 16). If the recorded echoes are simply added without removing the parabolic phase, they only add in phase over the short stretch where the parabola stays within about a quarter wavelength of round trip: L ≈ √(λR) = √300 = 17 m. The resolution is then about √(λR)/2 = 8.7 m instead of 0.5 m. Focusing is what makes SAR work. It requires the phase of every echo to be right to a small fraction of a cycle over the whole aperture: the coherence requirement of R4 Step 6, held for seconds instead of milliseconds.

Step 6

Recording amplitude and phase at every position: the raw data

In plain words

At every pulse, the radar writes down one line of numbers: for every distance, how strong the echo was (amplitude) and where in its cycle the wave was (phase). After 3 s there are 1 200 lines, stacked into a table. That table does not look like the patch at all. Each point of the ground has been spread into a long curved stripe across hundreds of lines, with its phase winding along it. The picture is hidden in those phases, and Step 7 gets it back out.

What happens at each pulse (the chain of R1 and R4):

1. The exciter makes a 150 MHz chirp; the PA sends it out through the antenna.

2. The echo comes back and the LNA amplifies it. It is mixed down with the same oscillator that made the transmitted pulse. This is what makes the radar coherent: the phase of the echo is measured against the phase of what was sent.

3. Two mixers, 90° apart, give I and Q (in-phase and quadrature). The ADC samples both. Each sample pair I + jQ is one complex number: amplitude √(I² + Q²) and phase atan2(Q, I).

4. Range compression (the matched filter of R6) turns the chirp into a sharp 1 m peak at each echo's range. The result is one line of complex numbers, one per range cell.

What each recorded number means

Amplitude: how strongly the ground in that range cell reflected the pulse. A house corner is strong; smooth water is weak.
Phase: φ = −4π·R/λ + ψ, wrapped to 0–360°. R is the exact distance to the reflector, and ψ is the reflector's own phase shift, which stays the same from pulse to pulse. Because 4π/λ is so large, the phase records the distance to a tiny fraction of the wavelength. One full turn of phase is λ/2 = 1.5 cm of distance.
Raw data matrix: one row per pulse (position along the track), one column per range cell. Each entry holds both numbers.

Step 7

Forming the image: adding 1 200 echoes for every pixel

In plain words

To find out how bright one pixel of the image should be, the computer asks: "If there were a reflector exactly here, where in the table would its echoes be, and what phase would each one have?" It reads those 1 200 entries, turns each one back by the phase it should have, and adds them up. If there really is a reflector there, all 1 200 arrows point the same way and add up to a big number. If there is nothing there, the arrows point every which way and nearly cancel. Doing this for every pixel draws the picture.

The method (time-domain back-projection), for one pixel at along-track position x0 and range r0:

1. For every pulse p, with the antenna at xp, compute the distance the pixel would have: Rp = √(r0² + (xp − x0)²).

2. In row p of the table, read the complex sample at range Rp, interpolating between cells.

3. Rotate that sample by +4π·Rp/λ. That removes the phase a reflector at this pixel would have had. A real reflector at this pixel now points the same way in every row.

4. Add the rotated samples from all pulses that lit the pixel. The magnitude of the sum is the pixel's brightness.

Step 8

The PRF window: why SAR cannot have everything

In plain words

Two rules squeeze the pulse rate from both sides. Fast enough: the aircraft must not move more than half the antenna length between pulses, or the fingerprints are sampled too coarsely. Slow enough: all echoes from the near edge to the far edge of the strip must come back before the next pulse goes out. For an aircraft there is plenty of room between the two. For a fast satellite the gap is narrow.

The lower limit: sample the azimuth chirp. The pulses are the samples of the azimuth chirp. Its bandwidth is 2V/D, so (sampling I and Q) the PRF must exceed 2V/D, usually by 10–30 %. Otherwise the chirp aliases and ghost copies of bright targets appear along the track (azimuth ambiguities). In distance terms: the platform must move less than D/2 between pulses.

The upper limit: the whole swath must fit in one PRI. Echoes from near range to far range must all come back before the next pulse, or far-range echoes are mistaken for near-range ones (R4 Step 5, range ambiguity). So PRI > 2·(far − near slant range)/c.

The trade this creates. For a fast satellite, finer azimuth resolution (smaller D) raises the minimum PRF, and a wider swath lowers the maximum. Push either too far and the window closes. This is the reason spaceborne SAR antennas are large (10–15 m long) and why the modes of Step 10 exist: there is no free lunch between resolution and swath. (A further constraint, not drawn: the radar cannot receive while transmitting, nor during the strong echo from directly below, the nadir return; real PRF choice avoids those times too.)

Step 9

Range migration: when the parabola crosses range cells

In plain words

While the aircraft flies past a point, its distance to that point changes a little. If it changes by more than one range cell, the point's echo slides from one cell to the next during the recording, and the processor must follow it. It is like tracking a car that changes lanes: you must look in the new lane, not the old one.

Step 5b showed the range to a point changes by x²/(2R) along the aperture. At the aperture edges, x = L/2, so the total change is L²/(8R). If that is more than about one range cell, the point's echo walks across several range cells during the aperture, and the azimuth compression must follow it from cell to cell. This is range cell migration, and the processing step that straightens it is range cell migration correction (RCMC).

For component engineers the message is simple: the processor needs the platform's exact position along the whole aperture, to a fraction of a wavelength. Motion sensors (GPS and an inertial unit) and "autofocus" algorithms supply it. A platform that wobbles by a centimetre at 10 GHz has shifted the phase by 240°, and the image blurs exactly as if the antenna were defocused.

Step 10

Modes: trading resolution against swath

In plain words

The longer you watch a point, the sharper it gets. Watch one patch for a long time (spotlight) and it becomes very sharp. Hop between several strips (ScanSAR) and you cover more ground, but each strip is watched for less time and comes out less sharp.

All three imaging modes follow the same rule: the azimuth resolution is set by how long, in angle, each point is watched. Watching longer (spotlight) gives finer detail; sharing the time among several swaths (ScanSAR) gives coarser detail but more area. Electronically steered arrays (R2 Step 5) make all three possible with one antenna.

Step 11

What a SAR image looks like, and why

In plain words

Speckle: even a smooth field looks grainy, because each pixel holds many tiny reflectors whose echoes add up randomly, like the shimmering spots you see when a laser shines on a wall. Averaging several images (looks) smooths it, at the cost of sharpness. Layover and shadow: the radar sorts everything by distance, so the top of a tall building, being nearer to the radar, is drawn before its base, and the ground behind it gets no signal at all.

11a. Speckle

Each pixel of grass, soil or water contains many small scatterers. Their echoes add with random phases, exactly as noise does in R3 Step 1, so even a perfectly uniform field gives pixels whose brightness varies randomly from pixel to pixel: an exponential distribution of power with a standard deviation as large as its mean. This grainy pattern is speckle. It is not noise from the receiver; it is a real property of coherent imaging, and the same field imaged again from the same place gives the same speckle.

Reducing it: multilooking. Split the synthetic aperture (or the range bandwidth) into N parts, form N lower-resolution images ("looks") and average them. The scatter shrinks by √N, and the resolution gets N times coarser in that direction. 4 looks halve the scatter at 4 times coarser resolution.

Try it: a simple scene (a dark field, a brighter field, a bright road and a few strong point targets), imaged with speckle. The slider sets the looks: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64. Things to check: (1) 1 look: the two fields are hard to tell apart; (2) 16 looks: the fields separate clearly, but the road and points are smeared, because each look has coarser resolution.

11b. Layover, foreshortening and shadow

  • Foreshortening: a slope facing the radar is compressed in the image, because its far end is only slightly further away in slant range than its near end. It looks short and bright.
  • Layover: if the slope is steeper than the beam, the top is actually closer in slant range than the foot. The image then shows the top before the foot: tall buildings and mountains appear to lean towards the radar.
  • Shadow: the ground behind a tall object receives no illumination and returns nothing: a black area in the image, longer at shallow look angles.

None of these is a fault. They come from measuring slant range, and a SAR image must be read with the radar's position in mind, unlike a photograph.

Step 12

Sensitivity: noise-equivalent sigma zero (NESZ)

In plain words

NESZ answers one question: how dark a surface can the radar still see? Calm water reflects very little (about −25 dB), towns reflect a lot (0 dB). If the NESZ is −37 dB, everything brighter than −37 dB shows up and anything darker is lost in noise. More power, a bigger antenna, a lower noise figure, a shorter range or a slower aircraft all push the NESZ lower (better).

What is measured. SAR images ground, not point targets, so its sensitivity is quoted for distributed scatterers: σ⁰ ("sigma nought"), the radar cross section per square metre of ground (dimensionless, in dB). Typical values at 10 GHz: calm water −25 dB or lower, grass and fields −15 dB, forest −10 dB, towns 0 dB and above.

NESZ is the σ⁰ that gives a pixel SNR of 1 (0 dB). Anything darker than the NESZ is lost in the receiver's noise, so a SAR that should image calm water must have an NESZ below about −25 dB.

The SAR radar equation, step by step. Start from R1's energy form for a point target, SNR ∝ Pavg·T·G²λ²σ / R⁴. Three SAR-specific substitutions:

  1. The integration time T is the time in the beam: λR / (2V·δa), from Step 5 (for a resolution δa).
  2. The target is a pixel of ground: σ = σ⁰ · δa · δrground.
  3. Multiply out: the δa cancels.
SNR = Pavg · G² · λ³ · σ⁰ · δrground / (2 · (4π)³ · R³ · V · kT0 · F · L)    NESZ = σ⁰ at SNR = 1

Reading the equation. R³, not R⁴: the longer aperture at long range recovers one factor of R. V in the denominator: flying faster means less time on each pixel. Azimuth resolution does not appear: finer azimuth detail is paid for with exactly the longer integration it needs. Average power, gain and noise figure appear just as in R1.

Step 13

Where your components show up in SAR

Where your component shows up

  • Exciter / chirp generator: bandwidth sets range resolution; linearity and flatness set range sidelobes (R6).
  • Master oscillator and STALO: phase must stay coherent over the whole synthetic aperture, seconds long. Low-frequency phase noise and drift defocus the azimuth compression and raise azimuth sidelobes.
  • PA and LNA: average power and noise figure set NESZ directly; PA phase drift with temperature over the aperture time blurs the image.
  • T/R switch and limiter recovery: set the shortest usable range and the nadir-return blanking; they constrain the PRF window.
  • ADC and digital receiver: sample rate follows the chirp bandwidth (hundreds of MHz for sub-metre SAR); data rate and storage become large.
  • Antenna: its length D sets the azimuth resolution and the minimum PRF; its sidelobes cause range and azimuth ambiguities.
  • Timing and navigation interfaces: pulse timestamps and the platform motion data must line up to a fraction of a wavelength of position.
