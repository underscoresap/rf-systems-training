Recognising: from a stream of pulses to a list of radars

Part 4's listener measured every pulse and wrote one record per pulse, the pulse descriptor word (PDW). On a real hilltop, pulses from many radars arrive mixed together, about 10 000 of them every second. This page is about processing, not hardware. It first takes the measurable parameters one at a time (frequency, direction, pulse width, amplitude, timing, the inside of the pulse and the scan) and, for six example radars, shows why each radar chose its value and what that value tells us. Then it builds on those parameters: sorting the mixed stream into radars, finding each one's rhythm, and matching a library to say what each radar is. Every step uses the same six radars.

Step 0

What arrives: one mixed stream of pulse records

In plain words

Imagine six drummers in a dark hall, each beating their own rhythm, and you hear every beat but cannot see who played it. For each beat you can write down when it came, how high it sounded, how long it lasted and which direction it came from. Recognition is the job of sorting those notes back into six drummers, finding each one's rhythm, and then saying which kind of drummer each one is.

You already know

From Part 4 Step 5: for every pulse the receiver writes a PDW with time of arrival (TOA), frequency (RF), pulse width (PW), amplitude and angle of arrival (AOA). From Part 4 Steps 5–6: TOA is good to tens of ns, AOA to about 3° with an interferometer. From R4 Step 5: the pulse repetition interval (PRI) is the time from one pulse to the next of the same radar.

Step 2

RF: the carrier frequency

In plain words

RF is the "pitch" of the radar. It is fixed by the radar's hardware and by which band it is allowed to use. To us it is the first and quickest clue: the band says roughly what kind of radar it is. But radars of the same kind share a band, and some radars change frequency on every pulse precisely so that RF tells us less.
RadarRFWhy the radar uses it (its side)What it means to us (our side)
A surveillance10.00 GHzX band: a 1.5 m antenna gives a 2° beam (λ/D, R2)X band → short-to-medium-range radar; one fixed value, easy to sort
B1, B2 ship navigation9.41 GHz both9.3–9.5 GHz is the band set aside for ship radars worldwide; every ship uses ittells us "ship radar", but cannot tell one ship from another
C air traffic2.80 GHzS band: rain loss is about 1/10 of X band, so it sees aircraft 100+ km away in any weatherS band → long-range surveillance; alone in its band, sorted by RF in one step
D tracking9.60 GHzX band: narrow beam for precise angle trackingclose to A in RF and in direction; RF alone separates them (400 MHz apart)
E frequency-agile9.00–9.80 GHz, a new 20 MHz channel every pulsehopping avoids interference and makes it hard to listen to or jam on one frequencyRF useless for sorting (every pulse differs) but the hopping itself is a strong clue

How we measure it, and how well. An IFM (instantaneous frequency measurement) receiver compares the pulse with a delayed copy of itself. The phase difference across a delay τ is 2π·f·τ, so a 1 ns delay turns 360° per GHz. A digital receiver takes the FFT of the samples. Either way, about 1 MHz rms is typical for a single pulse. That is far finer than the gaps between most radars, but zero for B1 and B2, which share 9.41 GHz.

Worked: which radars can RF separate? A (10 000), D (9 600) and C (2 800) are hundreds of MHz from everyone. B1 and B2 are 0 MHz apart. E's 41 channels cover 9 000–9 800 MHz, the same span that contains B and D: one of its channels is exactly D's 9 600 MHz, and two (9 400 and 9 420 MHz) sit 10 MHz either side of B's 9 410 MHz.

Try it: 200 measured pulses from each radar, stacked as a histogram on a frequency axis, one colour per radar. Things to check: (1) the whole range: C sits alone in S band, and the other five crowd into X band; (2) X band: E is a comb of 41 channels spread over 800 MHz, through B's and D's frequencies; (3) zoom on 9.41 GHz: B1 and B2 are one single peak, whatever the error; (4) raise the error to 20 MHz: E's comb melts into one smear.

RF, in one line

To the radar: set by band rules, range and weather needs; can be made to hop. To us: measured to ≈ 1 MHz; the first clue to the radar's class; good for sorting fixed-frequency radars, useless for sorting agile ones, and blind to two radars of the same model.

Step 3

AOA: the direction the pulse came from

In plain words

A radar can change its frequency, its pulse length and its rhythm, but it cannot change where it is. So the direction its pulses come from is the parameter we trust most. It is also the only one that leads to where the radar is: two listeners some distance apart each draw a line, and the radar is where the lines cross.
RadarWhere it is (from our hill)AOAWhy there (its side)Width of our ±3° uncertainty at that distance (our side)
A20 km north-east40°on a hill for coverage± 1.0 km
B18 km, on the river118°wherever the ship is; moves slowly± 0.4 km
B29 km, on the river123°another ship, 5° further along± 0.5 km
C45 km, at the airport200°at the airport it serves± 2.4 km
D15 km north-east45°near what it protects or tracks± 0.8 km
E30 km west300°wherever its operator puts it± 1.6 km

How we measure it. By amplitude comparison between antennas pointing different ways (5–10° accuracy) or by the phase difference across an interferometer baseline (0.5–3°), as in Part 4 Step 6. The error grows as the signal gets weaker.

Worked: what 3° means. A sideways error of R·σ: at 20 km, 20 000 × 3 × π/180 = 1.05 km. So one listener knows A's direction well but not its distance at all. A second listener 10 km away sees A from a different angle. The two bearing lines cross at A, and the overlap of their error wedges is a patch about ±2 km across: a location. (The bearings cross at only 29°; a longer baseline between the listeners would make them cross more squarely and shrink the patch.)

Worked: which radars can AOA separate? With a 3° error, the sorting gate (Step 11) is ±9°. B1 and B2 are 5° apart, A and D are 5° apart: AOA alone merges both pairs. With a 0.5° error the gate is ±1.5°, and all six separate by direction alone.

Try it: a map, about 140 km across, with our listener at the centre and rings at 10, 25 and 50 km. Each radar gets the error wedge our DF gives it. Things to check: (1) default ±3σ at 3°: the A and D wedges overlap, and so do B1 and B2; (2) error 0.5°: every wedge is separate; (3) error 10°: wedges become wide fans, and the error at C's 45 km is ±23 km; (4) turn on the second listener: each pair of wedges crosses over the true radar: that is how direction becomes location.

AOA, in one line

To the radar: not a choice; it is wherever it is. To us: the most trusted sorting parameter, the only route to location, limited by our DF accuracy (0.5–10°). It cannot separate two radars in the same direction.

Step 4

PW: how long each pulse lasts

In plain words

A longer pulse carries more energy, so it reaches further, but it blurs range: two targets closer than c·PW/2 return overlapping echoes. A shorter pulse sees fine detail at short range. So the pulse width tells us what distance and what detail the radar was designed for. Pulse compression (Step 7) breaks this rule, and a long pulse with a chirp inside is itself a clue.
RadarPWRange resolution c·PW/2Duty cycle PW/PRIWhy (its side)To us
A1.0 µs150 m0.1 %general surveillance: aircraft-sized detailordinary
B1, B20.25 µs37 m0.02 %sees buoys and banks close by; minimum range c·PW/2 = 37 mshort pulse: short-range, fine-detail radar
C2.0 µs300 m0.2 %more energy for long range; aircraft are far apartlong pulse, plain: long-range surveillance
D10 µs (chirped)1 500 m plain → 30 m after compression2.5 %energy of a long pulse, detail of a 5 MHz chirplong pulse + high duty → pulse compression, a modern radar
E0.5 µs (phase-coded)75 m → 5.8 m after compression0.2 %13 chips of 38 ns give fine detailshort, coded: check inside the pulse (Step 7)

How we measure it. From the two edges of the detected pulse: where it crosses half its peak on the way up and on the way down (Part 4 Step 5). The rise time of our video path is about 0.35 / video bandwidth. With 50 MHz of video bandwidth, the rise time is 7 ns and every PW here is measured to about 50 ns.

Where it goes wrong. If our video bandwidth is too low, a short pulse never reaches its full height before it ends, and its measured width is wrong. 0.35 / 5 MHz = 70 ns of rise is already a quarter of B's 0.25 µs. Overlapping pulses from two radars, and echoes off hills (multipath), stretch the measured width too.

Try it: each radar's pulse as our detector outputs it, all on one time scale, with the half-height crossings marked and the measured PW written beside it. Things to check: (1) default, 50 MHz: every measured PW is within a few ns of the true value; (2) switch to 0–1.2 µs: B's 0.25 µs and E's 0.5 µs side by side; (3) drop the video bandwidth to 1 MHz (350 ns rise time): B's pulse reaches only 79 % of its height (−2 dB) and reads 0.28 µs, while D's 10 µs is unaffected. Measuring at half the peak hides most of the error in width; it shows up in amplitude instead.

PW, in one line

To the radar: a trade between energy (range) and detail, unless it uses pulse compression. To us: measured to ≈ 50 ns given enough video bandwidth; a good second sorting parameter after AOA, and a clue to range and to pulse compression. Distorted by low video bandwidth, overlaps and multipath.

Step 5

Amplitude: how strong each pulse arrives

In plain words

How strong a pulse is when it reaches us depends on three things: how powerful the radar is (its ERP), how far away it is, and where its beam is pointing at that moment. The radar chooses its power for its own job, not for us. To us amplitude is a poor sorting parameter, because it rises and falls by 30 dB or more as the beam sweeps past. But it says whether we are in the main beam, it roughly shows how far away the radar is, and over seconds it gives the scan (Step 8).

What the numbers say. The strongest signal at our hill, C at −20.5 dBm, comes from the radar furthest away, because it is the most powerful. So amplitude alone does not give distance. If the library (Step 13) tells us a radar's ERP, then the measured main-beam amplitude gives the distance: R = λ/(4π) · 10(ERP − Pr)/20. A 3 dB error in amplitude makes that distance wrong by a factor of 1.4.

Strong and weak together. C's main beam (−20.5 dBm) and A's sidelobes (−78.5 dBm) are 58 dB apart, almost exactly the 59 dB SFDR of Part 4 Step 7. A weaker receiver would lose A's sidelobe pulses whenever C's beam passes.

Things to check: (1) at −80 dBm sensitivity we hear every radar's sidelobes, so we hear all six all the time; (2) at −54 dBm (Part 4's wideband receiver) only C's sidelobes are still heard; the others are heard only while their main beams pass, and D, which never points at us, not at all; (3) move C out to 200 km: still −33.5 dBm in the main beam; (4) the readout's "distance estimated from amplitude" is right when the ERP is known.

Amplitude, in one line

To the radar: power is chosen for its own detection range; we only see it through distance and beam pointing. To us: poor for sorting (it swings 30 dB with the scan), useful for main-beam warning, rough range if ERP is known, and the raw material of scan analysis.

Step 6

TOA and PRI: when pulses arrive, and the rhythm

In plain words

Each pulse gets a time stamp (TOA). On its own a time stamp means nothing. The gaps between one radar's pulses, though, are its PRI: its rhythm. The radar chooses that rhythm to suit how far it needs to see and how it deals with moving targets, so the PRI and its pattern are among the strongest clues to what the radar is. They are also the key to separating two radars that agree in every other parameter, like B1 and B2.

Step 6b

PRI patterns: what the rhythm says about the radar, in detail

In plain words

Radars do not always beat a steady rhythm. Some alternate between a few intervals, some wobble randomly, some switch between rhythms. The pattern is a strong clue to what the radar is for, because each pattern solves a particular problem from R4 and R6.
PatternWhat it looks likeWhy a radar uses itOur scene

Staggera few PRIs in a repeating orderremoves MTI blind speeds (R4 Step 5)C: 900, 1 000, 1 100 µs, frame 3 000 µs

How each pattern is recognised. Plot each extracted pulse's PRI against pulse number (the "PRI sequence"), and make a histogram of those PRIs.

• Constant: one value; the histogram is one bin wide (only the TOA error, about 0.07 µs rms for the difference of two TOAs).

• Stagger: N levels, repeating in a fixed order. The sum of one cycle, the frame, is constant: 900 + 1 000 + 1 100 = 3 000 µs. In the difference histogram of Step 12, the frame is the strongest peak. A stagger radar is described by its levels and their order.

• Jitter: a flat spread. For D, 400 µs ± 10 % is a uniform spread from 360 to 440 µs: a standard deviation of 80/√12 = 23 µs. That is far wider than a 10 µs bin, which is why jitter spreads the Step 12 peak over 8 bins and makes it weak.

• Dwell and switch: steps between levels that each last many pulses.

• Sliding: a ramp.

Try it: top, the PRI of each of 120 pulses in order. Bottom, the histogram of those PRIs. The readout is what a pattern classifier would measure. Things to check: (1) default, stagger with 3 levels at 1 000 µs: levels 900 / 1 000 / 1 100 µs, frame 3 000 µs, as for radar C; (2) jitter at 10 %: a flat block from 900 to 1 100 µs, standard deviation ≈ 58 µs; (3) add missed pulses: occasional PRIs at twice the value, which a classifier must recognise as gaps, not as a new level; (4) dwell and switch: long flat steps.

Step 7

Inside the pulse: intrapulse modulation

In plain words

Looked at from outside, a pulse is just a burst of a certain length. Looked at from inside, sample by sample, it may sweep in frequency (a chirp) or flip its phase in a code. The radar does this to get the detail of a short pulse with the energy of a long one (R6 Step 1). To us it is a fingerprint, and even radars with no intended modulation leave small accidental ones that can tell two units of the same model apart.

Intrapulse modulation, in one line

To the radar: pulse compression, detail without giving up energy. To us: a strong identifier and, at its finest (accidental drift), a way to tell individual transmitters apart; needs wide bandwidth, flat phase and good SNR.

Step 7b

Measuring the modulation inside one pulse

In plain words

Most radars send a plain burst of one frequency. Pulse-compression radars (R6 Step 1) change the frequency or phase during the pulse, so that a long pulse can give the range detail of a short one. If the listener samples fast enough to see inside each pulse, that modulation is a fingerprint: radar D's 10 µs pulse sweeps 5 MHz.

You already know

From R6 Step 1: a chirp of bandwidth B compressed by a matched filter gives range resolution c/2B, whatever the pulse length. From Part 4 Step 4: an IFM or digital receiver measures frequency.

Instantaneous frequency

The rate at which the phase of the signal turns, at each moment: f(t) = (1/2π)·dφ/dt. A digital receiver gets φ(t) from its I and Q samples (as in the SAR raw data of Part 3 Step 6) and differentiates it. A plain pulse gives a flat line, a chirp a sloped line, a phase code a flat line with sudden phase jumps.

ModulationWhat is measured inside the pulseWorkedRange resolution after compression, c/2B
None (plain pulse)flat frequency; spectrum width ≈ 1/PW1 µs pulse → 1 MHz wide150 m
Linear FM (chirp)frequency sweeps linearly; slope = B / PWradar D: 5 MHz in 10 µs → 0.5 MHz/µs, time-bandwidth 5030 m
Phase code (Barker 13)13 chips; phase flips 0°/180° at the chip boundaries1 µs pulse, 77 ns chips → ≈ 13 MHz wide11.5 m

Why noise matters. The phase of a noisy sample is uncertain by about 1/√(2·SNR) radians, and differentiating it makes the frequency estimate noisy. Averaging over many samples helps, which is why a long pulse is easy to analyse and a short one is hard. At 20 dB SNR, a 10 µs chirp is obvious. At 0 dB it is buried sample by sample, but a straight-line fit across the whole pulse still recovers the slope.

What this needs from the hardware. The sample rate must cover the pulse's bandwidth (at least 13 MHz of complex bandwidth for the Barker code), the receiver's phase must be flat across that band, and the ADC must have enough bits to keep the SNR.

Try it: the pulse after mixing down to a low frequency (top), its instantaneous frequency measured sample by sample at 100 Msamples/s (middle, dots, with a straight-line fit) and its unwrapped phase (bottom). Things to check: (1) chirp at 20 dB: the fit finds about 5 MHz over 10 µs; (2) no modulation: a flat line, slope near 0; (3) Barker 13: flat frequency, but the phase plot shows steps of 180° at the chip edges; (4) lower the SNR to 0 dB: the dots scatter widely, but the fitted bandwidth stays within about 10 % of the true value.

Step 8

The scan: how each radar moves its beam

In plain words

Over several seconds, the amplitude of a radar's pulses rises and falls as its beam passes over us. The radar chooses how it moves its beam to suit its job: sweep all round, look at one target, or jump around electronically. To us, the timing of those bursts is a clue to the radar's job, and a beam that stops moving and stays on us is the most important observation of all.

Scan, in one line

To the radar: how often it revisits each direction, or whether it stays on one target. To us: scan type and period identify the job; a steady, strong signal is a warning; needs seconds of listening, far longer than any other parameter.

Step 8b

Measuring the scan period

In plain words

Over several seconds, the amplitude of a radar's pulses rises and falls as its beam sweeps past the listener (Part 4 Step 0). The shape and timing of that rise and fall show how the radar moves its beam, and that says a lot about its job. A beam that stops moving and stays on the listener is the most important case of all: the radar is probably tracking something near the listener.
Scan typeAmplitude seen over timeTypical periodTypical job
Circularone main-beam burst per period, sidelobes between2–12 ssurveillance (A: 6 s)
Sector (back and forth)bursts in pairs, uneven spacing1–5 ssector search, height finding
Conicalsteady amplitude with a small, fast ripple15–50 Hzolder tracking radars
Steady (no scan seen)constant, high—tracking, or an electronically steered beam dwelling on the listener

How the period is measured. Take the amplitude of the group's pulses over time and find where it repeats, by autocorrelation: shift the record against itself and see at which shift it matches best. For radar A, the bursts are 6 s apart and each lasts BW / (360°/period) = 2° / 60°/s = 33 ms. Measuring a 6 s period needs at least two bursts, so at least 6–12 s of listening: much longer than the 100 ms used for PRI analysis.

Watch out. A listener that is itself scanning or sweeping in frequency (Part 4 Step 3) only samples the radar now and then. Its own revisit time must be shorter than the burst length, or the bursts are missed, and the period it measures is wrong.

Try it: top, the amplitude of each pulse heard (dBm) over the listening time; bottom, its autocorrelation, with the period found marked. Conical scan is drawn at 25 Hz, so only its first 0.5 s is shown. Things to check: (1) circular, 6 s: bursts every 6 s, period found 6.0 s; (2) shorten the listening time to 5 s: one burst only, and no period can be found; (3) sector: bursts in pairs, and the period found is the full back-and-forth cycle; (4) steady: a flat line near the main-beam level and no period.

Step 10

The naive approach: take the gap to the next pulse

In plain words

The obvious idea is to measure the gap between each pulse and the next one, and look for gaps that repeat. With one radar that works perfectly. With six, the next pulse almost always belongs to a different radar, so the gaps are a meaningless jumble.

With one radar. Radar A alone: every gap is 1 000 µs, and a histogram of gaps has one sharp peak at 1 000 µs.

With all six. The chance that the next pulse after one of A's pulses is also A's is tiny: in 1 000 µs about 9 other pulses arrive. The gaps measured are spread from 0 to a few hundred µs, with an average of 98 µs. None of them is a real PRI.

With missing pulses. Even with one radar, if the receiver misses a pulse (weak signal, a collision with another pulse, a moment spent tuned elsewhere as in Part 4 Step 3), that gap becomes 2 000 µs. Any method has to survive missed pulses.

So recognition is done in two stages. First, sort the pulses into groups using the parameters that each pulse carries on its own (Step 11). Then, inside each group, find the rhythm using the time of arrival of every pair of pulses, not only neighbours (Step 12).

Step 11

Sorting by parameters: grouping pulses that look alike

In plain words

Before looking at timing, put pulses that came from the same direction, have the same length and the same frequency into the same pile. Direction is the most trusted: a radar can change its frequency every pulse, but it cannot change where it is. This first sort is called pre-sorting or clustering.

Which parameters can be trusted

AOA (angle of arrival): fixed by the radar's position, changes only slowly as it or the listener moves. The radar cannot hide it. Accuracy about 3° (Part 4 Step 6).
PW (pulse width): fixed by the radar's mode. Accuracy about 50 ns, but distorted when pulses overlap or by multipath.
RF: measured to about 1 MHz by an IFM or digital receiver. Reliable for fixed-frequency radars, useless for sorting a frequency-agile one.
Amplitude: changes all the time as the beam scans (Part 4 Step 0), so it is not used for sorting.
TOA: carries the rhythm, used in Step 12.

The method. Two pulses are put in the same group if they are close in every parameter used: here within 3σ of the measurement error (9° in AOA, 150 ns or 10 % in PW, 5 MHz in RF). Any pulse close to any member joins the group.

Choosing the gate width. Too narrow, and one radar's pulses are split into several groups by measurement noise. Too wide, and two radars are put in one group. 3σ keeps 99.7 % of one radar's pulses together, so gates follow the receiver's measurement accuracy. That is why AOA accuracy matters so much: with a 10° error the gate becomes 30° and radars A (40°) and D (45°) can only be split by their PW.

Step 12

Finding the pulse rhythm: PRI analysis

In plain words

The group at 118–123° holds two ships' radars that look identical. To split them, measure the time between every pair of pulses in the group, not only neighbours, and count how often each gap occurs. A radar with a 1 100 µs rhythm produces many pairs exactly 1 100 µs apart; random pairs spread evenly. The rhythms stand out as peaks.

Definitions

PRI: pulse repetition interval, the time between successive pulses of one radar. Difference histogram: a count of how many pulse pairs are separated by each time difference τ. Order: a first-order difference is between neighbours, a second-order difference skips one pulse, and so on. Harmonic: a peak at 2×, 3× … a true PRI, which every difference histogram also shows, because pulses two apart are 2 PRIs apart.

Three methods, from simplest to best.

1. Consecutive differences (first order only): fails as soon as radars are interleaved (Step 10).

2. All differences (cumulative difference histogram, CDIF/SDIF family): count the differences between every pulse and every later pulse up to a maximum τ. True PRIs give tall peaks. But so do their multiples (2 × 1 100 = 2 200 µs, 2 × 1 250 = 2 500 µs), and peaks can also appear at sums of two PRIs. A threshold is set above the level that random pulse pairs would give, and the lowest peak above it is tried first.

3. PRI transform: like method 2, but each pair adds a unit arrow with phase 2π·t/τ instead of adding 1. For a true PRI τ, every pair's later pulse is at t = t₀ + kτ, so all arrows point the same way and add. At 2τ, half the pairs start on odd pulses and half on even ones, so their arrows point opposite ways and cancel. Harmonics are suppressed.

Choosing the parameters. Bin width: wider than the TOA error plus any jitter, so that one radar's pairs stay in one bin; narrower than the gap between two PRIs to be separated. 10 µs here. Maximum τ: at least the longest expected PRI, ideally 2–3×, so that missed pulses and stagger frames still show. Observation time: the peak grows in proportion to time while the random background per bin grows too, so the contrast improves; 100 ms gives about 80 pulses per radar. Threshold: a few times the random level, or pulses from noise make false PRIs.

Then extract. Once a PRI is found, the sorter walks through the group. It starts at a pulse, looks for another pulse one PRI later (within a tolerance), and repeats. The chain of pulses found is one radar. Those pulses are removed, and the analysis is run again on what is left.

Step 13

Matching a library: what is this radar?

In plain words

After Steps 2–12, each group has a list of measured properties: frequency, pulse width, PRI pattern, modulation and scan. The listener compares that list with a library of known radar types, each described by the range of values it can use, and gives each type a score. One clear winner is an identification; two close scores are an ambiguity; no good score is an unknown radar, which is itself useful news.

Definitions

Emitter library: a table of radar types, each with the ranges of RF, PW and PRI, the PRI pattern, the modulation and the scan. Match score: how well a measurement fits a library entry, from 0 to 1. Ambiguity: two or more entries scoring nearly the same.

The scoring used here. Each parameter scores 1 if the measured value is inside the entry's range, falling linearly to 0 at one tolerance outside it. The tolerance is the measurement accuracy plus a margin (RF 20 MHz, PW 20 %, PRI 10 %, scan 20 %). Categories (PRI pattern, modulation) score 1 if they match and 0.3 if not, because a pattern can be mis-classified. The total is the product of the parameter scores, so one clearly wrong parameter rules an entry out.

Why ranges overlap. Many radar types share bands and pulse widths; that is why one parameter alone rarely identifies anything. B1 and B2 are both a perfect match to "Navigation, X band": the library can say what they are, but not tell the two ships apart. Only their PRIs and directions do that.

What the listener reports. One line per radar: its identity and score, bearing, RF, PRI and when it was last heard. That list is updated continuously as new PDWs arrive, and is what an operator or a jammer (Part 6) works from.

Step 14

Where your components show up

Component link

  • Clock and timestamping (FPGA): TOA resolution and stability. The PRI peaks of Step 12 are 10 µs bins, but stagger levels, jitter statistics and pulse extraction need TOA to tens of ns over seconds.
  • IFM or digital receiver: RF accuracy (1 MHz here) sets the RF gate of Step 11 and the RF score of Step 13.
  • Video bandwidth and detector: PW accuracy (Part 4 Step 5), which split A from D in Step 11.
  • DF antennas and channel phase match: AOA accuracy, the most trusted sorting parameter. A 10° error made the gates swallow neighbours.
  • ADC sample rate, bits and phase flatness: intrapulse analysis (Step 7b) needs the pulse's whole bandwidth sampled with a flat phase response.
  • Sensitivity and dynamic range (Part 4): every missed pulse weakens Step 12 and Step 8b. Strong pulses hiding weak ones (SFDR) cause missed pulses in exactly the busy environments where sorting is hardest.
  • Memory and processing: 10 000 PDWs per second here, a million in a dense environment. The pair counting of Step 12 grows with the number of pulses × pulses per maximum τ.
