Detection: deciding "target or noise"

R1 ended with a number: the SNR of an echo. But a radar never sees "SNR". It sees a stream of noisy samples and must decide, one sample at a time, whether a target is there. This page shows what those samples look like, how a threshold trades detections against false alarms, where the famous "13 dB" comes from, and how CFAR keeps the false-alarm rate steady when the noise, clutter or jamming changes.

Step 0

What the receiver actually hands to the detector

You already know

A receiver chain: LNA, mixer, IF filter, ADC, and digital I/Q (in-phase and quadrature) samples. You know thermal noise power kTB and noise figure. From R1: a matched filter's bandwidth is about 1/τ, and the radar collects one echo sample per range cell per pulse.

After each transmitted pulse the receiver samples continuously. Each sample corresponds to one distance from the radar (one range cell, R1 step 6). After the matched filter, every range cell holds one complex number I + jQ. The detector looks only at its size: the envelope √(I² + Q²), or more often the power I² + Q².

Where a "cell" comes from, from the start

1. The clock starts at the pulse. The radar transmits a short pulse at time t = 0 and then switches to receive. From then on, anything that comes back is an echo from somewhere along the beam.

2. Arrival time is distance. An echo from a target at range R has travelled out and back, 2R, at the speed of light, so it arrives at t = 2R/c. Every 1 µs after the pulse corresponds to 150 m of range (c × 1 µs / 2). Time after the pulse and distance are the same axis with different units.

3. The ADC chops that time into samples. The receiver output is sampled at a rate fs, one sample every 1/fs seconds. Sample number 0 is taken just after the pulse, sample 1 one period later, and so on, until the next pulse is sent (one PRI later).

4. Each sample is a range cell. Sample number n holds whatever arrived at time n/fs, which is the echo from range n·c/(2fs). So a range cell (or range bin, range gate) is simply one ADC sample slot after the pulse, labelled with the distance it corresponds to. A cell is not a physical thing; it is a slice of distance defined by the sampling clock.

5. How wide is a cell? Cell width = c/(2fs). The sample rate is normally matched to the pulse: a pulse of width τ (bandwidth about 1/τ) is sampled about once per τ, so a cell is about cτ/2 wide, which is also the range resolution from R1 step 6. Two targets closer than one cell fall into the same sample and cannot be separated.

6. Repeat every pulse. Every pulse produces a new row of samples, cell 0 to cell N−1. Stack the rows and you get a table: one row per pulse ("slow time"), one column per range cell ("fast time"). A column holds the history of one distance, pulse after pulse. Integration (R1 step 7) adds down a column, and the detector decides one column (one cell) at a time.

cell width = c / (2·fs)    cell number of a target = round(2R·fs/c)    cells per pulse = PRI × fs

Most range cells contain only noise. A few contain an echo plus noise. The detector has no way of knowing which is which in advance. It applies one simple rule: if the power in a cell is above a threshold, declare a detection. Everything on this page is about choosing that threshold well.

Definitions

Range cell (range bin): one sample time after the pulse, which corresponds to one distance.
Detection: a cell whose power crosses the threshold.
Probability of detection Pd: the chance that a real target's cell crosses the threshold.
Probability of false alarm Pfa: the chance that a noise-only cell crosses it.
False alarm: a detection with no target. It is noise that happened to be large.

The two probabilities always move together: lower the threshold and you catch weaker targets (Pd up) but you also let through more noise spikes (Pfa up). There is no setting that gives Pd = 1 and Pfa = 0. The only way to get both better at once is more SNR.

Step 1

What noise looks like at the detector

Thermal noise in I and in Q is Gaussian: each is a random voltage with zero mean, and the two are independent. When you take the size of a point whose x and y are independent Gaussians, the result follows a Rayleigh distribution. Its square, the power, follows an exponential distribution. The exponential is the key fact for detection, because it has a simple tail:

P(power > T) = exp(−T / Pn)Pn = mean noise power. A threshold T set at x times the mean noise power is crossed by noise with probability e−x.

Read that carefully: noise is not a flat line at Pn. Most samples are below the mean, but occasional samples are many times larger. The chance of a sample 10 times the mean (10 dB) is e−10 = 4.5 × 10−5. That sounds small, until you remember that a radar makes about a million such decisions every second.

Bottom: histogram of the noise powers (bars) and the exponential law (line), on a log scale so the rare large samples are visible. What fraction of noise samples are above the mean noise power?

e−1 = 37 %. Most samples (63 %) are below the mean, because a few large samples pull the mean up.

Step 2

Setting the threshold: how many false alarms can you live with?

Turn the tail formula around. For a wanted Pfa, the threshold must sit at T / Pn = ln(1/Pfa) above the mean noise power.

T / Pn = ln(1 / Pfa)    false alarms per second = Pfa × (range cells × PRF × Doppler cells …)

Why do radars use Pfa around 10−6? Count the decisions. A radar with 1 000 range cells and a PRF of 1 kHz makes 106 decisions every second. At Pfa = 10−6 that is one false alarm per second, which a tracker can tolerate. At 10−3 it would be 1 000 false targets every second, and the display would be useless.

That is the exponential tail at work: the threshold is a very sensitive knob for false alarms, which is why the threshold must sit at exactly the right height relative to the noise. False-alarm rate: false alarms per second, Pfa × decisions per second.
Mean time between false alarms: its inverse.

Step 3

Signal plus noise: the probability of detection

The idea in one line: an echo never arrives clean. Noise is always added to it, so the same echo measures a little bigger on one pulse and a little smaller on the next.

Think of it as a vector sum. The echo is a fixed arrow (a phasor) of length A. Noise is a small arrow of random length pointing in a random direction. The receiver measures the length of the two added together:

  • When the noise points the same way as the echo, the sum is longer than A.
  • When it points the opposite way, the sum is shorter than A.
  • Most of the time it lands somewhere in between.

Repeat this thousands of times and draw a histogram of the measured values. You get a hump centred near A, spread out by the noise. That hump is the teal curve in the demo. (Its textbook name is the Rician distribution; you do not need the formula.)

Now put both cases on one picture. The grey curve is what a cell holding only noise measures: mostly small values. The teal curve is what a cell holding echo + noise measures: values around A. The orange line is the threshold. Anything to its right is declared "target".

  • Grey curve to the right of the line = noise mistaken for a target = Pfa (false alarm). It is so small you can barely see it.
  • Teal curve to the right of the line = real target correctly caught = Pd (detection).
  • Teal curve to the left of the line = a real target missed.

Three things to try in the demo:

  1. Slide the threshold right. Fewer false alarms, but more of the teal hump falls left of the line, so more targets are missed.
  2. Slide the threshold left. More targets caught, but the grey curve crosses the line and false alarms shoot up (watch "false alarms per second").
  3. Raise the SNR. The teal hump moves right, away from the grey one. Now one threshold can catch almost every target and keep false alarms rare. More SNR is the only thing that improves both at once.

So the detector's job reduces to this: put the line where false alarms are acceptable (step 2), then ask how much SNR is needed to push the teal hump far enough past it. That question is answered in step 4.

Shaded areas right of the orange threshold: Pfa (grey, mostly invisible because it is so small) and Pd (teal). Decisions per second is 10 to the power shown.

Check yourself

With the threshold at 11.4 dB (Pfa = 10⁻⁶), what is Pd when the SNR equals the threshold, 11.4 dB?

About 50 %. When the echo sits exactly at the threshold, the noise pushes it above or below about equally often. To be confident of a detection, the echo must sit well above the threshold, a couple of dB more.

Step 4

Where the "13 dB" comes from

R1 used "about 13 dB SNR needed" without proof. Here it is. Ask for Pfa = 10−6 (threshold 11.4 dB) and Pd = 0.9. The Rician curve must be pushed far enough right that 90 % of it is above the threshold. That takes an SNR of 13.1 dB. Try it in the demo above: set SNR 13.1 dB and threshold 11.4 dB.

Computing the Rician area needs a numerical integral. Engineers use Albersheim's equation, a curve fit accurate to about 0.2 dB, for a steady (non-fluctuating) target:

SNRdB = −5·log10N + (6.2 + 4.54/√(N+0.44)) · log10(A + 0.12·A·B + 1.7·B)A = ln(0.62 / Pfa), B = ln(Pd / (1 − Pd)), N = number of pulses integrated non-coherently. The result is the SNR needed per pulse.

Fluctuating targets need much more. R1 step 2 showed that a real target's RCS changes with aspect. If the echo fades from look to look (the "Swerling 1" model), a single look sometimes catches a deep fade, and no threshold can save it. For one pulse, Pd = Pfa1/(1+SNR), which needs 21.1 dB for Pd = 0.9 at Pfa = 10⁻⁶: 8 dB more than a steady target. The extra is called the fluctuation loss. It is largest at high Pd, and it is one reason radars use several looks (frequency agility decorrelates the fades, so the looks fade independently).

Integration helps. With N = 10 pulses integrated, Albersheim gives 5.0 dB per pulse. With 100 pulses, −1.3 dB per pulse: each individual echo is below the noise, yet the sum is detectable. This connects back to R1 step 7.

Where your component shows up

  • Every 1 dB of noise figure you save is 1 dB less SNR needed from the transmitter or the antenna. In R1's equation it is the same as 26 % more power.
  • Frequency agility (a fast-settling synthesiser) decorrelates target fades between pulses, which cuts the fluctuation loss.
Step 5

When the noise is not steady

A threshold set at 11.4 dB above the noise works only if the noise stays where it was measured. In practice it does not:

  • Receiver gain drifts with temperature, and the noise floor moves with it.
  • Clutter: echoes from ground, sea, rain and chaff. They are noise-like, but much stronger than thermal noise and they vary from place to place.
  • Interference and jamming (Part 6) raise the noise in some directions and at some ranges.

Here is how sharp the effect is. If the noise power rises by 3 dB (doubles) but the threshold stays fixed, the threshold is now only 13.8/2 = 6.9 times the new mean. Pfa goes from e−13.8 = 10−6 to e−6.9 = 10−3: a thousand times more false alarms from a 3 dB change. In general a noise rise by a factor k turns Pfa into Pfa1/k. Try it with the "Noise rises by" slider in step 1.

Setting the threshold high enough for the worst case fixes the false alarms but throws away detection everywhere else. The answer is to measure the noise locally, all the time, and set the threshold from that measurement: CFAR.

Definitions

Clutter: unwanted echoes from the environment.
CFAR (constant false-alarm rate): a detector that estimates the local noise and clutter level around each cell and scales its threshold to it, so that Pfa stays constant.

6.1 What goes wrong with one single threshold

Our example row has three regions: quiet thermal noise (cells 0–27), a strong target T1 = 30 at cell 14 (some of its energy spills into cells 13 and 15, at 8 and 7.2), a weaker target T2 = 15 at cell 20, and clutter from cell 29 onwards, where the background is about 30 times stronger and varies from cell to cell (1.2 to 101).

The reason is simple: one number cannot describe two different backgrounds. A line that suits the quiet cells is far too low in clutter, and a line that suits the clutter is far too high in the quiet cells. The threshold has to be measured locally, cell by cell. That is CFAR.

6.2 The fix: compare each cell with its neighbours

CFAR asks, for every cell in turn: "is this cell much louder than the background around it?" To answer, it splits the cells around the one being decided into three groups:

The three groups, and why each exists

Cell under test (CUT): the one cell being decided right now. Its own power is never used to set its threshold; it is only compared with the threshold at the end. Every cell takes a turn as the CUT, and the whole window slides along one cell at a time.
Reference cells (also called training cells): cells a little way out on both sides. Their average is the estimate of the background "around here". They are the reason the threshold can follow the background: quiet neighbours give a low threshold, loud neighbours a high one.
Gap cells (also called guard cells): the cells right next to the CUT, which are skipped. They exist because a real target does not fit in exactly one cell: some of its energy spills into the cells beside it. If those cells were averaged, the target would make its own background look loud and raise its own threshold.

Every method uses the same window. They differ only in which single number they take from the reference cells as the "background". Let L = average of the left half, R = average of the right half.

6.4 Choosing the gap and the number of reference cells

The gap: just wide enough to hold the target's spill. Count how many cells one target's energy occupies on each side of its peak and use that many gap cells. The spill comes from:

  • The matched-filter response: a pulse of width τ sampled once per τ spreads over about one cell on each side.
  • Straddle: a target between two sample instants splits its energy between two cells.
  • Pulse-compression sidelobes (R6): small echoes several cells away.
  • Large targets: a 150 m ship with 15 m cells occupies 10 cells, so a 1-cell gap is useless for ships.

Typical choice: 1–3 cells on each side for aircraft-sized targets. Wider than necessary is not free: the reference cells move further away, where the background may already be different, and more cells at the ends of the range have no complete window.

The reference cells: as many as the background stays uniform over. More cells give a steadier average, so α can be smaller and the threshold sits closer to the ideal. But the whole window must still lie inside one kind of background.

α = N · (Pfa−1/N − 1)   (CA-CFAR, N reference cells in total)With perfect knowledge of the noise the threshold would be ln(1/Pfa) × noise. CFAR only has an estimate that wobbles by about ±1/√N (±25 % for N = 16). A low estimate lets noise through much more easily than a high one keeps it out, so α must be larger than ln(1/Pfa) to hold the same average Pfa. The difference is the CFAR loss.

Worked sizing. 1 µs pulse, 1 MHz sampling, so cells are 150 m. Choose a gap of 2 cells and 16 reference cells (8 each side): the window spans 8 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 8 = 21 cells = 3.15 km. That is fine over open sea or clear sky, where the background is steady over several km. Over a coastline or the edge of a rain cell, the background changes within a few hundred metres, so a shorter window (or GO or OS) is better.

Rule of thumb, putting it together:

  1. Gap = the target's spill on each side (1–3 cells; more for big targets or pulse compression).
  2. Reference = 8–16 cells each side, so the window is shorter than the typical patch of uniform background.
  3. Method: CA for uniform noise, GO where clutter edges dominate, SO where dense targets dominate, OS when both occur.
  4. α from the wanted Pfa and the number of reference cells (the formula above for CA).

In a real radar

  • Two dimensions. Pulse-Doppler and FMCW radars produce a range-Doppler map (R4–R6). CFAR then uses a window in both range and Doppler around each cell, with gap cells in both directions.
  • Where it runs. In an FPGA, CA is a running sum: add the cell entering the window, subtract the one leaving. OS needs sorting and costs more logic.
  • Clutter maps. For fixed ground clutter, some radars keep a map of the average power in each cell over many scans and use that as the background.
  • Jamming. A noise jammer raises the background in every cell in its direction. CFAR raises the threshold with it, so the display is not flooded, but every target in that direction now needs more SNR. CFAR protects the display, not the detection range (Part 6).

Check yourself

Why is the CUT's own value never included in its background estimate?

If it were, a target would raise its own threshold in proportion to its own strength, and strong targets would never cross it. The CUT is the thing being judged, so it cannot also be the judge.

Your radar uses 15 m range cells and must detect ships up to 120 m long. How many gap cells?

A 120 m ship covers 8 cells, so its echo spreads about 4 cells either side of its centre, plus about 1 cell of matched-filter spill: use about 5 gap cells each side. With 1 or 2 the ship masks itself.

Over a coastline, CA-CFAR gives false alarms just inside the land clutter. Name two fixes.

Switch to GO (take the louder half, which is the clutter side), or shorten the window so it does not straddle the edge. A clutter map is a third option for fixed coastlines.

Two aircraft fly in close formation, 3 cells apart, and the weaker one keeps disappearing. Why, and what helps?

Each sits in the other's reference cells and raises its threshold (masking). SO or OS-CFAR ignore the half or the values that hold the strong target. A narrower window also helps, at the cost of a larger α.

Step 7

From a detection to a plot and a track

  • Clustering. One target often crosses the threshold in several neighbouring cells (in range, in Doppler and in adjacent beams). These are merged into one plot, and the strongest or a weighted centre gives its position.
  • Confirmation. A single crossing could be a false alarm. Radars use rules such as M-of-N: declare a target only if it is detected in, say, 2 of 3 consecutive looks near the same place. A false alarm at Pfa = 10⁻⁶ almost never repeats in the same cell, but a real target does. Phased arrays send an immediate confirmation dwell (R2 step 7).
  • Tracking. Confirmed plots are joined from frame to frame into tracks with position and velocity, which smooth the noise and predict where to look next.

Confirmation is why the detector can run at a fairly high Pfa: the tracker filters most false alarms out. The system's true false-target rate is much lower than the detector's.

Step 8

Where your components show up in detection

Where your component shows up

  • LNA noise figure sets the thermal noise floor Pn. It is the reference for every threshold on this page.
  • Spurs from synthesisers, clocks, DC-DC converters or ADC interleaving appear in the same cells every pulse. They are not random, so they cross a threshold every time and look like real targets. A spur must sit well below the noise in the final processed cell, after integration gain, not just below it at the ADC.
  • LO phase noise spreads strong clutter into neighbouring Doppler cells, which raises the noise floor around it. CFAR then raises the threshold and small moving targets near clutter are lost (R4, R6).
  • ADC clipping and LNA compression on strong clutter change the noise statistics and create intermodulation products: false alarms and a wrong CFAR estimate. AGC that changes gain within one pulse would also confuse CFAR, which is why radars use STC instead (R4).
  • Limiter recovery after transmit leaves a decaying "noise" at short range, which is a clutter edge for CFAR.
  • I/Q imbalance and DC offset create image and zero-Doppler false targets.

Glossary of terms introduced on this page

Range cell
One sample time after the pulse, which corresponds to one distance.
Envelope / power
√(I² + Q²) / I² + Q² of a sample.
Rayleigh / exponential distribution
The distribution of the noise envelope / noise power.
Rician distribution
The distribution of the envelope of a steady echo plus noise.
Threshold
The level above which a cell is declared a detection.
Pd, Pfa
Probability of detecting a real target / of noise crossing the threshold.
False-alarm rate
Pfa × number of decisions per second.
Albersheim's equation
Curve fit giving the SNR needed for a Pd, Pfa and N, for a steady target.
Swerling 1, fluctuation loss
A model of a target whose RCS fades from look to look / the extra SNR this costs.
Clutter
Unwanted echoes from ground, sea, rain or chaff.
CFAR
Constant false-alarm rate detection: the threshold follows a local estimate of the noise.
Cell under test, gap (guard) cells, reference cells
The cell being decided / cells skipped next to it so a target cannot raise its own threshold / cells averaged to estimate the background.
Self-masking, masking
A target's own spill raises its threshold / a nearby target raises another target's threshold.
CA / GO / SO / OS-CFAR
Cell-averaging / greatest-of / smallest-of / ordered-statistic variants.
CFAR loss
The extra SNR needed because the noise is estimated, not known.
Clutter edge
A sudden change in clutter level along range, where CA-CFAR makes errors.
Plot, M-of-N, track
Merged detections of one target / confirmation over several looks / a target followed over time.
