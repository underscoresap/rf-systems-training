Front-end protection and duplexing: one antenna, kilowatts out, picowatts in

A radar sends kilowatts out of an antenna and, a few microseconds later, listens through the same antenna for echoes far below a picowatt. The low-noise amplifier that has to hear those echoes would be destroyed by a millionth of the transmit power. This page explains, from first principles, the parts that make this possible: the circulator, the duplexer, the T/R switch and the limiter. For each one it traces your design choice to the datasheet line and then to what the system loses: noise figure, detection range, blind range, dynamic range or time spent deaf. Every term is defined before it is used and every step has worked numbers.

Step 0

The problem: one antenna shared by a transmitter and a receiver

You already know

An LNA has a maximum input power (its damage level, often +20 dBm for a GaAs part) and a compression point well below that. A PA can deliver hundreds of watts to kilowatts. Both connect to the same antenna in almost every radar.

Using one antenna for both directions saves size, weight and cost, and it guarantees that the transmit beam and the receive beam point the same way. The price is that the transmitter and the receiver are now joined by a few centimetres of transmission line. Something has to steer the transmit power to the antenna and not to the receiver, steer the echo from the antenna to the receiver and not back into the transmitter, and catch whatever still leaks across before it reaches the LNA.

Four kinds of part do this. A circulator steers power by direction (port 1 → 2 → 3). A duplexer steers it by frequency (transmit band one way, receive band the other). A T/R switch (transmit/receive switch) steers it by time (connected to the transmitter while transmitting, to the receiver while listening). A limiter is the last line of defence: it passes weak signals with little loss and turns into a near short circuit when the power is high. Most pulsed radars use a circulator plus a limiter, sometimes with a switch; continuous-wave systems use a duplexer or two antennas.

Definitions used everywhere on this page

dB and dBm: dB is a ratio, 10·log10(P2/P1). dBm is power relative to 1 mW: 0 dBm = 1 mW, +30 dBm = 1 W, +60 dBm = 1 kW, −90 dBm = 1 pW.

Insertion loss (IL): how much weaker the wanted signal is after passing the part, in dB.

Isolation: how much weaker an unwanted path is than the input, in dB. 20 dB isolation means 1 % of the power gets through.

Peak and average power: a pulsed radar transmits a pulse of width τ every pulse repetition interval (PRI). Duty cycle = τ/PRI. Average power = peak × duty. Damage from heating follows average power; damage from voltage breakdown and diode burn-out follows peak power.

Check yourself

Why not simply use two antennas?

Many FMCW and some jammer systems do. For a large pulsed radar, a second aperture doubles size and weight, and the two beams must stay aligned. Even two antennas give only 40–80 dB of isolation, so a limiter is still needed.

Step 1

The power gap between transmit and receive

You already know

Thermal noise is kTB: −174 dBm/Hz at room temperature, so −114 dBm in 1 MHz. Noise figure (NF) adds to that. The LNA module derives this in full (LNA and receiver noise, step 1).

A radar echo falls with the fourth power of range, so the weakest echo the radar must detect sits just above the receiver noise floor. The transmitter, on the same antenna, is often 180 dB stronger. A ratio of 180 dB is 1018: the same ratio as one second to thirty billion years. No single component provides that much isolation. The design works because the radar does not need it all at once: it needs enough isolation that the LNA survives, and enough that the receiver has recovered by the time the first echo it cares about comes back.

Step 2

The circulator: a one-way roundabout made from a magnetised ferrite

You already know

Ordinary passive parts made of metal, dielectric and resistors are reciprocal: S21 = S12. Power goes from port 1 to port 2 exactly as easily as from 2 to 1. To steer power by direction you need a material that breaks this symmetry.

Ferrite is a magnetic ceramic (for example yttrium-iron garnet, YIG, or nickel or lithium ferrites). It is an electrical insulator, so microwaves pass into it, and it is full of electron spins that act like tiny gyroscopes. A permanent magnet applies a steady field that lines the spins up. If anything tips a spin, it does not just swing back; it precesses, wobbling around the field like a spinning top, always in the same sense (say clockwise seen along the field). The natural wobble rate is the Larmor frequency, about 28 GHz per tesla of internal field.

Now send in a microwave whose magnetic field rotates. If it rotates in the same sense as the precession, it keeps pushing the spins in step and interacts strongly with them: the ferrite looks like a material of one permeability, μ+. If it rotates the other way, it fights the precession and barely interacts: permeability μ−. Reverse the direction of travel and the rotation sense, seen from the field, flips. So the wave sees a different material going forward from going backward. That is non-reciprocity, and it comes from the fixed spin sense set by the magnet.

A junction circulator puts a ferrite disc at the centre of a Y-shaped junction of three lines. A signal entering port 1 sets up a standing-wave pattern in the disc, made of two counter-rotating waves. Without the magnet they are equal and the pattern is symmetric: power splits between ports 2 and 3. With the magnet, μ+ ≠ μ−, the two waves see different electrical lengths, and the pattern twists by 30°. The magnet strength is chosen so the twist puts a null exactly at port 3: all power leaves at port 2. By symmetry, port 2 feeds port 3 and port 3 feeds port 1. Put the transmitter on port 1, the antenna on port 2 and the receiver on port 3.

Ideal S-matrix: S21 = S32 = S13 = 1, all others 0.   Isolation = −20·log10|S31|   IL = −20·log10|S21|The null at port 3 is a cancellation of two waves, so it is deep only at the design frequency and degrades toward the band edges. Datasheets quote the worst isolation across the band.

Definitions

Isolator: a circulator with port 3 terminated in a matched load. Power flows 1 → 2; anything reflected back from port 2 is absorbed. Used to protect a PA from antenna mismatch.

Bias field: the steady magnetic field from the permanent magnet. Its value sets the operating point; temperature changes the ferrite's magnetisation and shifts the band.

What goes wrong with the simplest approach: a reciprocal splitter instead of a circulator

A lossless, matched, reciprocal three-port cannot exist, so a reciprocal part must use a resistor. Use a Wilkinson divider with the PA and receiver on the two isolated arms and the antenna on the common port. The PA and the receiver are isolated from each other by about 20 dB, but half of the transmit power is burned in the resistor (5 kW of a 10 kW pulse), and half of every echo is burned too: 3 dB of loss in front of the LNA, so +3 dB system NF and about −16 % radar range. A circulator gives the same 20 dB with 0.2 dB loss each way.

The spin precession sense flips, so the wave that was strongly interacting becomes the weak one, and the pattern twists −30° instead of +30°: 1 → 3 → 2.

Step 3

Antenna mismatch: reflected transmit power goes straight into the receiver

You already know

A mismatched load reflects part of the incident wave: reflection coefficient Γ, VSWR = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|), return loss = −20·log|Γ|.

The circulator routes port 2 to port 3. That is what you want for an echo coming in from the antenna. It is also exactly what happens to transmit power that the antenna reflects: it enters port 2 travelling back and is sent, with only the small circulator loss, to the receiver. So the receiver port sees two waves: the direct leakage through the circulator (set by isolation) and the antenna reflection (set by antenna VSWR). They add as voltages, with a phase that depends on line lengths and frequency. The worst case is in phase.

An antenna VSWR of 1.5 means |Γ| = 0.2, a return loss of 14 dB. That reflection is larger than the leakage through a 20 dB circulator. A damaged radome, ice, or a broken waveguide joint can push VSWR much higher, and an open circuit reflects everything: then the full transmit power reaches the receiver port. That is why the limiter's power handling is usually specified against total reflection, not against circulator isolation.

Step 4

The duplexer: separating transmit and receive by frequency

You already know

A band-pass filter passes its band with low loss and rejects frequencies outside it; more resonators (higher order) give steeper skirts but more loss. Resonator unloaded Q (Qu) sets that loss.

If the transmitter and receiver use different frequencies, two band-pass filters joined at the antenna do the job: the transmit filter passes the transmit band and blocks the receive band, and the receive filter does the opposite. This is a duplexer (frequency-division duplexing). It has no moving parts and no switching time, so it works for continuous-wave systems that transmit and receive at the same moment: FDD communications links, beacons, some data links, and FMCW radars with offset transmit and receive bands.

A duplexer must do two jobs. First, the receive filter must reject the transmit carrier so the LNA is not compressed. Second, the transmit filter must reject the PA's own noise and spurious output falling in the receive band: a PA with 40 dB gain and 10 dB NF puts out −174 + 40 + 10 = −124 dBm/Hz of broadband noise, 50 dB above the receiver's own floor. A pulsed radar transmits and receives on the same frequency, so a duplexer cannot separate them: pulsed radars use a circulator and a T/R switch (next step) instead. In radar texts the word "duplexer" is often used for the whole circulator, switch and limiter assembly; here it means the frequency-division filter pair.

Step 5

The T/R switch: separating transmit and receive by time

You already know

A PIN diode is a p-type and an n-type region with a wide undoped "intrinsic" (I) region between them. Forward bias floods the I region with stored charge (electrons and holes): its RF resistance falls to about 1 Ω. Zero or reverse bias empties it: it looks like a small capacitance Cj.

A pulsed radar transmits for τ (say 1 µs) and then listens for the rest of the pulse repetition interval. A T/R switch connects the receiver only while it is listening. The usual radar layout puts it after the circulator, in front of the receiver: during the pulse the switch is "off" (isolating), adding its isolation to the circulator's; after the pulse it turns "on" (low loss).

A common switch uses PIN diodes in shunt across the line. Forward-biased, each diode is a low resistance Rs that short-circuits the line and reflects the signal: isolation. Unbiased, each diode is a capacitance Cj that loads the line slightly: insertion loss. Two or more diodes spaced a quarter wavelength apart add their isolation, plus about 6 dB for each extra diode, because the quarter-wave line transforms each short into an open at the previous diode. Switching time is how fast the driver can pull the stored charge out of the I region; carrier lifetime τc sets how much charge there is. A long lifetime is needed for low distortion at low frequency and for high power, but it slows switching.

Choose the number of diodes from the isolation the protection budget needs (step 1), remembering each diode adds about 0.2 dB of loss. Step 6

The limiter: flat leakage, spike leakage, power handling and recovery

You already know

From step 3, the receiver port of a 10 kW radar can see close to 1 kW in the worst case, and the full transmit power if the antenna fails. The LNA survives +20 dBm. The part that closes this 40–50 dB gap, every pulse, without anyone switching it, is the limiter. The LNA module introduced the terms (LNA and receiver noise, step 7); this step builds them from the diode up.

A passive PIN limiter is a PIN diode in shunt across the line with a DC return (a choke) and no bias supply. At low power the diode is unbiased: a small capacitance, so weak echoes pass with about 0.1–0.5 dB of loss. At high power the RF voltage itself drives the diode into conduction on every positive half-cycle. Charge builds up in the I region faster than it recombines, the RF resistance falls to about an ohm, and the diode reflects most of the power back toward the circulator (where it is absorbed in the load or the transmitter). Because no bias is needed, it protects the receiver even when the radar is switched off, which matters on a ship or airfield full of other people's radars.

The I-region thickness W is the main design choice. A thin I region (1–2 µm) turns on at a low power, so its flat leakage is low, but it cannot stand much power and it holds little charge, so it recovers fast. A thick I region (7–15 µm) withstands hundreds of watts to kilowatts, but turns on only at a higher power, lets more through, turns on more slowly and holds more charge, so it recovers slowly. No single diode does everything, so high-power limiters use two stages a quarter wavelength apart: a thick coarse diode that takes the power, followed by a thin fine diode that sets the low flat leakage. A Schottky detector diode can be added to drive the PIN diode harder and faster (lower threshold, shorter spike). A driven (active) limiter, or switch, is forward-biased by the radar's transmit trigger just before each pulse: it has no spike, but it protects nothing when the radar is off.

The spike exists because the diode needs time to fill with charge. During the first nanoseconds of a fast-rising pulse it is still a capacitor, and almost the full pulse goes through. Limiter datasheets give spike leakage as an energy, in ergs (1 erg = 10−7 J = 100 mW for 1 µs), because a short spike damages a transistor by the heat it deposits in the gate, not by its peak power alone.

Threshold Pth ∝ W²   power handling ∝ W²   carrier lifetime τ ∝ W²   recovery trec ≈ τ · ln(x0/0.122)x0 = 10A/20 − 1, with A the attenuation (dB) while limiting; 0.122 is the value where the extra loss falls to 1 dB. A harder overload leaves more charge and recovers later. The W² scaling is the illustrative model used in the demo: threshold 7 + 20·log W dBm, handling 10·W² W peak, τ = 5·W² ns.

Definitions

Threshold: input power at which the loss has risen by 1 dB above its small-signal value.

Flat leakage: the output power once the limiter is fully limiting, during the flat top of the pulse.

Spike leakage: the extra energy passed during the first nanoseconds before the diode has turned on, in ergs.

Power handling: the largest input (peak, for a stated pulse width and duty, and CW) the limiter survives. The diode absorbs part of the power and must conduct the heat away.

Recovery time: time from the end of the overload until the small-signal loss is back within 1 dB (some datasheets use 3 dB) of normal.

The simplest limiter, one thin (3 µm) PIN diode, has a low threshold but in this model handles only 90 W: it burns out on the first pulse. One thick (10 µm) diode survives 1 kW but lets +33 dBm (2 W) through. Worked number for a single diode: W = 2 µm gives a threshold of 7 + 20·log 2 = 13 dBm, handles 10·2² = 40 W and has τ = 5·2² = 20 ns.

Step 7

The blind (minimum) range that protection creates

You already know

Radio waves travel 300 m per microsecond. An echo from range R arrives 2R/c after the pulse starts: 6.7 µs per kilometre.

The receiver cannot hear anything while its own transmitter is on, and it stays deaf until the T/R switch has switched and the limiter has recovered. Both start when the pulse ends, so the receiver is ready at τ + max(tsw, trec). Any echo that arrives before then is lost or partly lost (eclipsed). That sets the radar's minimum range, also called the blind range. Long pulses used with pulse compression make it worse: a 50 µs pulse alone blinds the first 7.5 km, which is why such radars add a short "fill" pulse to cover close range.

Rmin = c · (τ + max(tsw, trec)) / 2    (c/2 = 150 m per µs)τ: pulse width. tsw: T/R switch switching time. trec: limiter (and LNA) recovery time.

What goes wrong with the simplest approach: assume the receiver is ready when the pulse ends

The simplest estimate of minimum range is cτ/2: 150 m for a 1 µs pulse. It ignores the protection. With the two-stage limiter's 2.5 µs recovery, the receiver is actually ready at 3.5 µs, so the true minimum range is 525 m, 3.5 times larger. A target at 300 m that the simple estimate says is visible is completely lost.

Step 9

FMCW: the transmitter never stops, so leakage cannot be timed out

You already know

Phase noise L(f) is the noise power, relative to the carrier, in 1 Hz at offset f (dBc/Hz). The synthesiser module derives it and its effect on radar (Synthesiser and phase noise).

A frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) radar transmits a continuous chirp: a frequency that sweeps linearly, here 150 MHz in 1 ms. The receiver mixes the echo with a copy of the transmit signal. An echo delayed by td = 2R/c is at a slightly older frequency, so the mixer output is a tone, the beat frequency fb = slope × td: 1 MHz for a target at 1 km. An FFT of the mixer output is a range profile.

There is no listening gap, so a T/R switch is useless and a limiter would only distort. Leakage from the transmitter (through the circulator, or between two antennas, plus the antenna reflection) arrives continuously. It hurts twice. First, its power can compress the LNA and mixer, and it uses up the ADC's range, reducing dynamic range. Second, it carries the transmitter's phase noise. Because the leakage path is short, its phase noise is almost the same as that of the copy it is mixed with, and most of it cancels: this range correlation leaves a residue 4·sin²(π f τL) of the original, where τL is the leakage delay. Short internal leakage (1 ns) cancels very well; an antenna reflection at the end of 2 m of cable (20 ns) cancels much less and can raise the noise floor where real targets sit.

Use one antenna, a 25 dB circulator and a limiter, as a pulsed radar does. The receiver is compressed for the whole sweep and cannot see weak targets.

Step 10

You already know

Free-space path loss is 20·log10(4πd/λ). An ESM (electronic support measures) receiver listens across a wide band for other people's radars, so it needs high sensitivity and has no transmitter of its own to time against.

On a ship, aircraft or vehicle, the ESM antennas are metres away from the platform's own radar and jammer antennas. This is the co-site problem. The coupled power is the emitter power plus both antennas' gains toward each other, minus the path loss. Sidelobe-to-sidelobe coupling might total 0 dBi; when the radar's main beam sweeps past the ESM antenna it can be 30 dB more. An ESM receiver built for −65 dBm sensitivity then sees tens of dBm.

Four defences, often combined. A limiter, so the LNA survives; it leaves the receiver blind during and just after each pulse. Blanking: the radar sends its transmit trigger to the ESM, which gates its receiver off for the pulse plus a guard time, so the own radar is not reported as a threat and the LNA is not driven into compression. A band-stop (notch) filter at the own radar's frequency removes it continuously, at the cost of not seeing other emitters in that band. And physical separation and antenna placement, which is the only one that costs no sensitivity or time.

Step 11

The jammer look-through problem

You already know

A jammer transmits noise or false echoes at a threat radar. To aim it in frequency and time, it relies on its own ESM receiver, which must keep hearing the radar's pulses. The PA module covers how jammer power is generated (Power amplifier).

Look-through means the jammer's receiver can still see the threat while the jammer is transmitting. The threat pulse arrives at the jammer at, say, −60 dBm. To detect it, the jammer's own transmission leaking into its receiver must be well below that, say 10 dB below. A 1 kW jammer (+60 dBm) therefore needs 60 − (−70) = 130 dB of isolation between its own transmit and receive antennas. On an aircraft or pod, antenna-to-antenna isolation is typically 50–80 dB. The 50–80 dB shortfall cannot be closed by a limiter (it only stops damage) or by a filter (jammer and threat share the same frequency).

The usual answer is time-shared look-through: stop jamming for a short look window every look period, listen, then resume. That costs jamming duty (the radar gets brief unjammed looks) and the window may miss the threat's pulses if it is shorter than the threat's PRI. The design is a trade between how often and how long to look. Simultaneous look-through is possible only with very high isolation: separate, well-separated antennas, cross polarisation, and active cancellation of the jammer's own signal.

What goes wrong with the simplest approach: jam and listen at the same time

Transmit continuously and keep the ESM receiver on, with the 60 dB of antenna isolation a pod typically has. The jammer arrives at its own receiver at 0 dBm, 60 dB above the −60 dBm threat. The jammer never sees the threat again: it cannot tell if the radar has changed frequency, mode or switched off, so it may jam empty spectrum.

How to choose the design parameters

Choose the look window at least as long as the PRI of the threats that must be tracked, plus the receiver's recovery time. Choose the look period from how fast the jammer must react to a change. Every microsecond of look costs jamming duty, so improve isolation (antenna placement, polarisation, cancellation) and transmitter switch-off speed to shorten the window.

Glossary of terms introduced on this page

Blanking
Gating a receiver off during a known own transmission, using a trigger from the transmitter.
Blind (minimum) range
Range inside which echoes return before the receiver is ready: c·(τ + recovery)/2.
Beat frequency
In FMCW, the mixer output tone whose frequency is proportional to target range.
Carrier lifetime τ
Average time before an injected electron and hole recombine in a PIN diode; sets recovery and switching speed.
Chirp
A signal whose frequency sweeps linearly with time.
Circulator
Three-port ferrite device passing power 1 → 2 → 3 → 1 only.
Co-site
Interference between transmitters and receivers on the same platform.
Duplexer
Two filters joined at the antenna, separating transmit and receive by frequency. In radar texts sometimes the whole T/R protection assembly.
Duty cycle
Fraction of time a pulsed transmitter is on: τ/PRI.
Eclipsing
Loss of part of an echo because it arrives while the receiver is blind.
Erg
Unit of energy, 10−7 J; used for limiter spike leakage.
ESM
Electronic support measures: receivers that detect and identify other emitters.
Ferrite
Insulating magnetic ceramic whose electron spins precess about an applied field, making it non-reciprocal at microwave frequencies.
Flat leakage
Limiter output power during the steady part of a high-power pulse.
FMCW
Frequency-modulated continuous-wave radar.
I region
The undoped middle layer of a PIN diode; its thickness W sets threshold, power handling and lifetime.
Insertion loss
Loss of the wanted signal passing through a part.
Isolation
Attenuation of an unwanted path, in dB.
Isolator
Circulator with one port terminated: a one-way device.
Larmor frequency
Natural precession rate of electron spins in a magnetic field, about 28 GHz per tesla.
Limiter
Part that passes low power with small loss and reflects high power.
Look-through
A jammer's ability to keep receiving the threat while (or between) transmitting.
Non-reciprocal
Behaves differently in the two directions: S21 ≠ S12.
PIN diode
Diode with an intrinsic layer, used as a current-controlled RF resistor.
PRI
Pulse repetition interval: time between pulses.
Range correlation
Cancellation of transmit phase noise in a short-delay echo or leakage when it is mixed with the transmit signal.
Recovery time
Time after an overload until loss or gain is back within 1 dB of normal.
Return loss
−20·log|Γ|: how far below the incident power the reflection is.
Spike leakage
Energy a limiter passes before its diodes turn on.
T/R switch
Switch that connects the receiver only while the radar is listening.
Threshold (limiter)
Input power at which limiter loss has risen 1 dB.
VSWR
Voltage standing wave ratio, (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|).
