Synthesisers and phase noise, from the oscillator to the radar picture

Every radar, SAR and ESM receiver needs one or more local oscillators (LOs) and a reference. This page opens the box that makes them: the VCO, the crystal reference, the phase-locked loop, the fractional-N divider and the DDS. It starts from what phase noise actually is, then follows each design choice to a datasheet number and on to what the system loses: clutter that is not cancelled, a slow target that disappears, a smeared SAR image, a weak emitter hidden next to a strong one. Every term is explained before it is used. Every step has worked numbers and most have a demo you can change.

Step 1

What phase noise is: a sine wave whose timing wobbles

You already know

An ideal oscillator output is v(t) = A·cos(2πf₀t): one frequency f₀, a single line on a spectrum analyser. A real oscillator has noise inside it (thermal noise in resistors, shot noise and flicker noise in transistors). An amplifier adds noise power kTB·F to whatever passes through it. A mixer multiplies two signals and produces their sum and difference frequencies.

Noise disturbs either the size or the timing of the sine. Write the real output as v(t) = A·[1 + a(t)]·cos(2πf₀t + φ(t)). a(t) is amplitude noise: the peaks are not all the same height. φ(t) is phase noise: the zero crossings arrive a little early or a little late, at random. An oscillator runs in saturation (its gain compresses until the loop gain is exactly 1), so amplitude disturbances are pulled back every cycle; phase disturbances are not, because nothing in the oscillator knows what the "correct" phase is. Phase errors accumulate. That is why phase noise is much larger than amplitude noise in every oscillator, and why the datasheet talks about phase noise.

Phase wobble makes sidebands. Take the simplest case, a small sinusoidal phase wobble φ(t) = β·sin(2πfmt), with β the peak phase deviation in radians and fm the rate. For small β, cos(x + φ) ≈ cos x − φ·sin x. Put in φ and use the product rule for sines: the output is the carrier plus two small lines at f₀ + fm and f₀ − fm, each with amplitude β/2 of the carrier. Random phase noise is the sum of many such wobbles at all rates, so instead of two lines you see a continuous "skirt" of noise on both sides of the carrier. The distance from the carrier is called the offset frequency, f.

Terms

Carrier: the wanted sine at f₀. dBc: decibels relative to the carrier power.

Offset frequency f: distance from the carrier, for example "at 10 kHz offset".

Sideband: energy at f₀ ± f. Spur (spurious tone): a discrete line at a fixed offset, caused by something periodic (a reference leaking, a divider pattern). Phase noise is random and continuous; a spur is a single line.

L(f) ("script L"): the power of one sideband in a 1 Hz bandwidth at offset f, divided by the carrier power. Unit: dBc/Hz.

Check yourself

A spur sits at −60 dBc. What peak phase wobble causes it?

20·log(β/2) = −60 → β/2 = 0.001 → β = 2 mrad = 0.11°.

Why can an LO buffer amplifier make phase noise worse far from the carrier but not close to it?

The amplifier adds a flat (white) noise floor of about −174 + NF − P − 3 dBc/Hz. Close to the carrier the oscillator's own noise is far above that floor; far from the carrier the oscillator noise has fallen below it, so the floor sets L(f).

Step 2

Integrated phase noise and jitter: one number for the whole curve

You already know

Noise power in a band is the density times the bandwidth: kT·B. When the density changes across the band you add up (integrate) the density over the band. From step 1: L(f) is a density in dBc/Hz, and Sφ = 2·L in rad²/Hz.

The rms phase error is the area under the curve. Many systems do not care which offset the noise sits at, only how much phase error accumulates over the time they look at the signal. A system that looks for a time T is blind to wobbles slower than about 1/T (they look like a constant phase) and averages out wobbles faster than its bandwidth B. So the useful single number is the phase variance integrated from f₁ ≈ 1/T to f₂ ≈ B. Remember to count both sidebands: that is the factor 2.

Jitter is the same thing seen as time. A phase error of σφ radians on a carrier with period 1/f₀ is a timing error σt = σφ/(2πf₀). Digital designers specify clocks in femtoseconds of jitter; RF designers specify LOs in degrees rms or dBc. They are the same quantity.

Terms

Integrated phase noise: 10·log₁₀(σφ²), in dBc, over a stated band f₁ to f₂.

RMS phase error σφ: the square root of the integrated variance, in radians or degrees.

RMS jitter σt: rms timing error of the zero crossings, in seconds (fs, ps).

EVM (error vector magnitude): the error of a received constellation point relative to the ideal; for a phase-only error EVM ≈ σφ in radians.

Check yourself

A 2 GHz LO has 0.5° rms. It is multiplied by 8 to 16 GHz. What is its rms phase error and jitter now?

Phase scales with N: 4° rms. Jitter is unchanged: 0.5° = 8.7 mrad, σt = 8.7·10⁻³/(2π·2·10⁹) = 0.69 ps, and at 16 GHz 4° = 69.8 mrad gives the same 0.69 ps.

Why does the integration band have to be stated with every jitter number?

The integral depends on where you start and stop. Starting at 10 Hz instead of 1 kHz can double the number on a free-running source, and a wideband ADC sees noise up to half its sampling rate.

Step 3

The VCO: why resonator Q sets phase noise (Leeson's model)

You already know

An oscillator is an amplifier with a resonator in a feedback loop. It oscillates at the frequency where the loop phase shift is 360° and the loop gain is 1. A resonator of quality factor Q has a phase slope: its phase changes by about 2Q radians per unit of fractional frequency change near resonance. A varactor is a reverse-biased diode whose capacitance changes with voltage.

The resonator turns a phase disturbance into a small frequency change. Noise in the amplifier adds a small random phase δφ to the loop. The loop must still total 360°, so the oscillator moves its frequency until the resonator cancels δφ. A high-Q resonator needs only a tiny frequency shift to do that (steep phase slope); a low-Q one needs a large shift. A random frequency wobble Δf at rate f is a phase wobble Δf/f (phase is the integral of frequency), so inside the resonator half-bandwidth f₀/(2QL) the noise rises as 1/f²: 20 dB per decade towards the carrier. Outside it the resonator has no effect and you see the amplifier's own flat noise floor.

Flicker noise adds a steeper part close in. Transistors have flicker (1/f) noise below a corner frequency fc (about 1 to 50 kHz for silicon bipolar and SiGe, 1 MHz and more for GaAs FETs). It modulates the phase, so inside both fc and f₀/2QL the slope becomes 30 dB per decade. This model is Leeson's equation. It is approximate, but it shows every lever you have.

The VCO is also an FM modulator for noise on its tuning line. The tuning sensitivity KV (Hz per volt) turns any noise voltage on the varactor into frequency noise. A wideband VCO has a large KV, so its tuning line must be very quiet: the loop filter resistors, the op-amp and the supply all feed in here.

Terms

VCO: voltage-controlled oscillator, tuned by a varactor (or by current, for a YIG oscillator).

Loaded Q (QL): the Q of the resonator as it sits in the circuit, including the loading by the transistor and output coupling. Always lower than the unloaded Q of the resonator alone.

Leeson frequency f₀/(2QL): the offset below which the 1/f² region starts. Flicker corner fc: where 1/f noise equals white noise.

KV (tuning sensitivity): MHz/V. Pushing: frequency change per volt of supply. Pulling: frequency change with load mismatch.

Check yourself

A 5 GHz VCO measures −110 dBc/Hz at 100 kHz. What would the same topology give at 20 GHz with the same Q?

In the 1/f² region L ∝ (f₀/Q)². Four times f₀ at the same Q is 20·log 4 = 12 dB worse: −98 dBc/Hz.

Why does more oscillator signal power improve phase noise everywhere, not only on the floor?

Every term in Leeson's equation is multiplied by FkT/2Ps. The same transistor noise is a smaller fraction of a larger signal, so the phase disturbance it causes is smaller at every offset. The limit is device breakdown and the varactor's own nonlinearity.

Step 4

The reference, and why multiplying frequency by N costs 20·log N

You already know

A quartz crystal is a mechanical resonator with an unloaded Q of 10⁵ to 10⁶, far higher than any microwave resonator. It only works up to about 100 to 150 MHz (the crystal gets too thin). A frequency doubler (a diode or a saturated transistor) makes 2f₀ from f₀. A divider (a flip-flop) makes f₀/2.

Crystals are clean but low in frequency. Because Q is so high, the 1/f² region of a crystal oscillator starts only a few kHz from the carrier (Leeson frequency 100 MHz/(2 × 50 000) = 1 kHz), and the floor is reached by 10 kHz. To get a clean 10 GHz signal, every synthesiser starts from a crystal and multiplies its frequency up, either directly with multiplier chains or with a PLL (step 5) that copies the crystal's phase onto a VCO.

Multiplying frequency multiplies phase. A ×N multiplier makes the output phase N times the input phase: if the zero crossings of a 100 MHz signal wobble by 1° of its cycle (28 ps), the 10 GHz output made from it wobbles by the same 28 ps, which is 100° of a 10 GHz cycle. The timing error is the same in seconds, but each output cycle is N times shorter. Phase deviation ×N means sideband amplitude ×N and power ×N². In dB: +20·log₁₀ N. Dividing by N does the opposite and lowers phase noise by 20·log N, until the divider's own floor is reached.

Multiplication has a limit: the carrier collapses. The ×N rule holds only while the total phase error stays well below 1 rad. When σφ grows towards 1 rad the carrier power itself falls (to about e−σφ² of the total) and appears in the noise skirt instead. That is why millimetre-wave systems (×100 to ×1000 from a crystal) need exceptionally clean references close to the carrier.

Terms

TCXO / VCXO / OCXO: temperature-compensated, voltage-controlled, and oven-controlled crystal oscillators. An OCXO keeps the crystal at a fixed temperature (about 80 °C): best stability and lowest close-in noise.

Multiplication factor N: output frequency / reference frequency. Residual (additive) noise: noise a multiplier or amplifier adds by itself, measured with the same source on both sides.

Carrier collapse: loss of carrier power into the noise skirt when σφ approaches 1 rad.

Does dividing a noisy 10 GHz VCO by 10 to make 1 GHz improve its phase noise?

Step 5

The PLL: loop bandwidth decides who sets the noise at each offset

You already know

A feedback loop with gain makes its output follow its input inside the loop bandwidth and ignores the input outside it. Disturbances injected inside the loop (at its output) are suppressed inside the bandwidth and pass unchanged outside it. From steps 3 and 4: the VCO is noisy close in and clean far out; a crystal reference is clean close in but multiplying it up costs 20·log N.

How a PLL works. The VCO output is divided by N in a counter. A phase-frequency detector (PFD) compares the divided signal with the reference at fPD = fref (or a divided-down reference). If the divided VCO is late, a charge pump pushes current pulses into a loop filter, whose voltage tunes the VCO up; if it is early, it pulls current out. In lock, the divided VCO is exactly in step with the reference, so fout = N·fPD, and the VCO phase is N times the reference phase: a PLL is an electronic ×N multiplier.

Two transfer functions. Inside the loop bandwidth the loop tracks the reference, so the output phase noise is the reference noise ×N (+20·log N, step 4), plus the noise of the PFD and charge pump, also ×N. Outside the bandwidth the loop cannot react, so the output is the free-running VCO. The reference path is filtered by the closed-loop low-pass response H(f); the VCO noise is filtered by the high-pass error response 1 − H(f).

Where to put the bandwidth. The best total noise comes when the loop bandwidth is placed near the offset where the free-running VCO curve crosses the in-band (N-multiplied) floor. A narrower loop lets VCO noise through closer in; a wider loop passes more multiplied reference and PFD noise further out, and lets reference spurs through (step 6). Near the bandwidth edge a loop with low damping also peaks, adding a "shoulder" of a few dB.

Terms

PFD: phase-frequency detector. Charge pump (CP): current source that delivers ± ICP pulses proportional to the phase error.

Loop filter: RC network that turns charge-pump current into a smooth tuning voltage. It sets the loop bandwidth and damping.

Loop bandwidth (BW): offset where the closed-loop response H(f) falls 3 dB. Damping ζ: how much the loop overshoots and peaks (0.707 is the common choice).

PLL figure of merit (FOM): the PFD/charge-pump noise normalised to 1 Hz comparison frequency and N = 1, in dBc/Hz. Modern parts: −220 to −232 dBc/Hz.

Check yourself

A PLL datasheet shows a flat −100 dBc/Hz from 1 kHz to 200 kHz and then a fall. Which block sets the flat part?

The flat in-band region is the PFD/charge pump floor (FOM + 10·log fPD + 20·log N), possibly with reference noise ×N near its lower end. The fall after 200 kHz is the loop bandwidth, beyond which the VCO takes over.

Why can a cleaner reference sometimes make no difference to the PLL output?

If the PFD floor is above the reference ×N across the loop bandwidth, the PFD dominates; improving the reference only helps very close in, below a few hundred Hz.

Step 6

Integer-N and fractional-N: step size, noise and spurs

You already know

From step 5: fout = N·fPD and the in-band floor rises 10 dB for every decade lower fPD. A loop filter is a low-pass filter: it attenuates anything at the comparison frequency, but it can only do so if that frequency is well above the loop bandwidth.

Integer-N: the step size is the comparison frequency. If N may only be a whole number, the output can only move in steps of fPD. A radar that must tune in 1 MHz steps at 10 GHz needs fPD = 1 MHz and N = 10 000, which raises the in-band floor 20 dB compared with fPD = 100 MHz. The loop must also be at least 10 times narrower than fPD so the loop filter can suppress the reference spur: the small ripple the charge pump leaves on the tuning line at fPD (leakage, current mismatch), which frequency-modulates the VCO and makes spurs at ± fPD. Narrow loop means slow settling.

Fractional-N: N may have a fractional part. The divider switches between N and N+1 in a pattern whose average is, for example, 100.0005. A sigma-delta (ΣΔ) modulator chooses the pattern so that the error it makes is pushed to high offsets (noise shaping), where the loop filter removes it. Now fPD can be 100 MHz with steps of a few Hz (fPD/2²⁴ = 6 Hz). The trade-off: (1) ΣΔ quantisation noise, a hump rising at high offsets that a wide loop lets through; (2) fractional spurs, especially the integer-boundary spur: when the output is just above or below an exact multiple of fPD, a spur appears at the distance to that multiple. If that distance is inside the loop bandwidth, the loop cannot filter it.

Step 7

DDS: frequency made by arithmetic, and the spurs that come with it

You already know

A DAC converts a number to a voltage; it updates once per clock and holds the value in between (zero-order hold), which gives an output spectrum shaped by sin(x)/x with images at fclk ± fout. A B-bit DAC has a quantisation step of 1/2B of full scale. From step 4: dividing a frequency lowers phase noise by 20·log of the division ratio.

How a DDS makes a sine. A direct digital synthesiser (DDS) has a phase accumulator: an M-bit register (M = 32 or 48) that adds a fixed number, the frequency tuning word (FTW), every clock. The register value is the phase of the output, wrapping round at 2M (= 360°). The top P bits of the phase go to a sine look-up table; the result drives a B-bit DAC; a low-pass (reconstruction) filter removes the images. The output frequency is FTW·fclk/2M: with M = 32 and a 1 GHz clock the step is 0.23 Hz.

Why it has spurs. Two kinds of rounding make periodic errors, and periodic errors are spurs, not noise. (1) Phase truncation: only P of the M phase bits address the sine table; the dropped bits make a saw-tooth phase error that repeats in a pattern set by the FTW. Worst case it gives spurs near −6.02·P dBc. (2) Amplitude quantisation in the table and the DAC, plus DAC nonlinearity, make harmonics of fout that alias back into the band. These spurs can land anywhere, including close to the carrier, and they move when you change frequency.

Why it is clean in phase noise and fast. The DDS output is a divided-down version of its clock: Lout = Lclk − 20·log(fclk/fout), down to the DAC's own floor (−150 to −165 dBc/Hz). A new FTW takes effect after a fixed pipeline delay of tens of ns, with no loop to settle, and the change is phase-continuous. The limits: output below about 0.4·fclk, so a DDS is used at low frequency (as a fine-tuning reference for a PLL, or as a waveform source that is then up-converted).

Step 8

Switching and settling time: how fast a synthesiser can hop

You already know

A second-order feedback loop answers a step input with a damped oscillation: it rings at about its natural frequency and the ringing dies away as e−ζωnt. A wider loop has a higher natural frequency ωn, so it settles faster. From step 5: loop bandwidth also sets the noise and spurs.

Settling is set by the loop bandwidth. When N changes, the PFD sees a growing phase error and the loop drives the VCO to the new frequency. The frequency error then dies away as a damped ringing. How long until it is "settled" depends on how small the remaining error must be: a radar that hops between pulses needs the new frequency within about 1 kHz (or the phase within a few degrees) before it transmits, or the echo will not cancel in the Doppler processing.

Other things add time. Wideband integrated VCOs choose one of many switched capacitor bands at each new frequency: band calibration takes 10 to 100 µs. A large jump can drive the PFD outside its linear range and slip cycles, which makes the real time longer than the linear formula. YIG oscillators tune their main coil slowly (ms). Designers use pre-tuning (a DAC sets the tuning voltage close to the final value first), fast-lock modes (wide loop during the jump, narrow afterwards), or ping-pong pairs of synthesisers (one settles while the other is in use).

Frequency agility means changing frequency often and unpredictably: from pulse to pulse to defeat a jammer that measures and follows the radar, between dwells to avoid interference and multipath fades, or across a burst to make a stepped-frequency wideband waveform. The synthesiser settling time must be well inside the gap the timeline allows: for pulse-to-pulse agility, inside the pulse repetition interval minus the receive window.

Step 9

Reciprocal mixing and blocking: when the LO lets a strong neighbour in

You already know

A mixer multiplies the RF input by the LO. The IF output contains the RF minus the LO frequency. An IF filter then selects the wanted channel of bandwidth B. Thermal noise in that channel is −174 dBm/Hz + NF + 10·log B. A strong unwanted signal near the wanted one is called a blocker.

The LO's noise skirt translates the blocker onto the wanted channel. The mixer multiplies every RF signal by the whole LO spectrum, carrier and skirt. The wanted signal, at offset 0 from where the LO carrier puts it, lands in the IF channel. A blocker Δ away is shifted by the carrier to IF + Δ, outside the filter. But the LO also has phase noise at offset Δ: that part of the LO moves the blocker exactly onto the IF channel. The result is the blocker power multiplied by the LO noise density at offset Δ: noise in the channel that was not there without the blocker. It is called reciprocal mixing because it is the same as if the LO were clean and the blocker carried the LO's phase noise.

LO spurs do the same with discrete lines. An LO spur at offset Δ moves a blocker at Δ onto the IF as a clean, steady signal: a spurious response. In a radar this is a false target; in an ESM receiver it is a false emitter reported with a believable frequency.

Blocking is the overall loss of sensitivity caused by a strong out-of-channel signal. It has two mechanisms: reciprocal mixing (LO phase noise) and compression (the blocker drives the LNA or mixer towards P1dB and the wanted signal loses gain). Whichever happens first sets the blocking specification. In modern receivers with good linearity, reciprocal mixing is often the limit for blockers within a few MHz.

Step 10

The range-correlation effect: why phase noise limits clutter cancellation

You already know

A radar transmits, then receives the echo a time τ = 2R/c later. A moving target shifts the echo frequency by the Doppler shift fd = 2v/λ (at 10 GHz, λ = 3 cm, so 67 Hz per m/s). Clutter (ground, sea, rain) is a very strong echo at almost zero Doppler. Pulse-Doppler and MTI processing separate targets from clutter by Doppler frequency, in Doppler bins of width about 1/TCPI.

The same synthesiser makes the transmit signal and the receive LO. The echo carries the phase noise the transmitter had at time t − τ. The receiver mixes it with the LO, which has the phase noise of the same synthesiser at time t. The output has the difference: φ(t) − φ(t − τ). Wobbles much slower than 1/τ have hardly changed in that time and cancel. Wobbles faster than about 1/(2τ) are uncorrelated and add (up to +3 dB on average, +6 dB at some offsets).

As a filter: subtracting a delayed copy is a filter with power response 4·sin²(πfτ). For a clutter cell at 1.5 km, τ = 10 µs: at 2 kHz offset this factor is −18 dB; at 15 km, τ = 100 µs, it is +1.4 dB. Close-in phase noise is therefore much less harmful for near clutter than for far clutter.

What it does to a target. The clutter echo, perhaps 60 to 80 dB above a small target, now carries a residual noise skirt. In the Doppler bin of the target, that skirt adds noise: residual L(fd) + 10·log(bin width) dBc relative to the clutter. If that is not well below the target, the target is lost, however good the Doppler filters are. This is the phase-noise limit on subclutter visibility and on the MTI improvement factor.

Step 11

SAR: how phase noise and spurs blur the image and make ghosts

You already know

A SAR (synthetic aperture radar) forms a sharp azimuth (along-track) image by recording the phase of the echo from each scatterer over an aperture time Ta (0.1 to several seconds) and focusing them with a matched filter. After focusing, a single point scatterer becomes an impulse response (IRF): a narrow main lobe with sidelobes. PSLR is the highest sidelobe; ISLR is the energy in all sidelobes relative to the main lobe.

The image is made from phase, so phase errors become image errors. Every pulse adds the synthesiser phase error at that moment (after range correlation) to the scatterer's phase. Three cases, by the rate of the error compared with the aperture time:

(1) Slow errors (f < 1/Ta) bend the phase history across the aperture like a parabola: the main lobe widens and the image defocuses. (2) Fast random errors (f ≫ 1/Ta) scatter energy evenly into the sidelobes: the ISLR rises by about σφ² and shadows and low-contrast areas fill in. (3) A sinusoidal error (a spur) at fe creates two "paired echoes", ghosts at ±fe·Ta resolution cells from every bright scatterer, each at 20·log(β/2) dBc, just as in step 1.

Range correlation still applies, but SAR ranges are long (10 to 100 km, τ = 67 to 670 µs), so little cancellation remains above a few hundred Hz. Noise from offsets above PRF/2 is also sampled once per pulse and folds (aliases) into the azimuth spectrum, so the integration band for ISLR runs from about 1/Ta up to the receiver bandwidth.

Step 12

Signal identification: what the LO does to an ESM receiver's reports

You already know

An ESM (electronic support measures) receiver listens to other people's radars. For each pulse it measures a pulse descriptor word: frequency, pulse width, time of arrival, amplitude, angle of arrival and intra-pulse modulation. Emitter identification compares these with a library. From step 9: a strong signal plus LO noise raises the floor around it; a strong signal plus an LO spur makes a phantom signal.

Five ways the synthesiser enters the identification. (1) Absolute frequency comes from the reference: its accuracy in ppm becomes a frequency error on every report. (2) Short-term stability (close-in phase noise and jitter) spreads the measured frequency of each pulse. (3) Intra-pulse analysis (chirp slope, phase-code steps) is a phase measurement inside the pulse: the LO's integrated phase error over the pulse adds to it. (4) Spurs create phantom emitters next to every strong one; the library may even match them to a real radar type. (5) Reciprocal mixing hides weak emitters next to strong ones: the dense signal environment is exactly where it matters most.

Specific emitter identification (SEI) goes further: it tells two radars of the same type apart by their unintentional modulation, including their own transmitter phase noise and spurs. To measure an emitter's phase noise, the receiver's LO must be cleaner than the emitter by about 10 dB at the offsets used, otherwise the receiver measures itself.

Tuning speed matters too. A scanning superheterodyne ESM receiver steps its LO across the band and dwells on each step; a slow synthesiser (step 8) either lengthens the revisit time or misses short, agile emitters.

Reference

Glossary

Aperture time Ta
SAR integration time for one scatterer; sets azimuth resolution and the lowest offset that matters.
Blocker
Strong unwanted signal close to the wanted channel.
Blocking
Loss of receiver sensitivity caused by a blocker, through reciprocal mixing or compression.
Carrier
The wanted sine wave at f₀.
Carrier collapse
Loss of carrier power into the noise skirt when the rms phase error approaches 1 rad (high multiplication).
Charge pump (CP)
Current source in a PLL that converts the PFD phase error into current pulses for the loop filter.
Clutter
Unwanted radar echoes from ground, sea or weather, strong and near zero Doppler.
CPI
Coherent processing interval: the time over which pulses are combined; Doppler bin ≈ 1/CPI.
Damping ζ
Loop parameter controlling overshoot and peaking; 0.707 is common.
dBc, dBc/Hz
dB relative to the carrier power; per 1 Hz of bandwidth for noise densities.
DDS
Direct digital synthesiser: phase accumulator, sine table and DAC.
Desensitisation
Rise in receiver noise floor, in dB, due to a blocker.
Doppler shift fd
2v/λ: frequency shift of an echo from a target moving at radial speed v.
DRO
Dielectric resonator oscillator: very high Q, narrow tuning.
ESM
Electronic support measures: receivers that detect, measure and identify other emitters.
EVM
Error vector magnitude; for phase error alone ≈ σφ in radians.
Flicker (1/f) noise, corner fc
Low-frequency device noise rising as 1/f; its corner is where it equals the white noise.
Fractional-N
PLL whose divider ratio has a fractional part, made by dithering N with a ΣΔ modulator.
Frequency agility
Changing carrier frequency between pulses or dwells, for ECCM and interference avoidance.
FTW
Frequency tuning word of a DDS: fout = FTW·fclk/2M.
Improvement factor
MTI measure: signal-to-clutter ratio at the output over that at the input; limited by phase noise.
Integer-boundary spur
Fractional-N spur at the distance from fout to the nearest integer multiple of fPD.
Integer-N
PLL with a whole-number divider; step size equals fPD.
Integrated phase noise
10·log(σφ²) over a stated offset band, in dBc.
IRF, PSLR, ISLR
Impulse response function (image of a point); its peak and integrated sidelobe ratios.
Jitter σt
RMS timing error of zero crossings: σφ/(2πf₀).
KV
VCO tuning sensitivity in Hz/V; also converts tuning-line noise into phase noise.
L(f)
Single-sideband phase noise: noise power in 1 Hz at offset f relative to the carrier, dBc/Hz.
Leeson's equation
Model of oscillator phase noise from resonator QL, power, noise figure and flicker corner.
Loaded Q (QL)
Resonator Q including circuit loading; sets the Leeson frequency f₀/2QL.
Loop bandwidth
Offset where the PLL closed-loop response falls 3 dB; divides reference-dominated from VCO-dominated noise.
LO
Local oscillator: the signal a mixer multiplies the input with.
MTI, pulse-Doppler
Radar processing that separates moving targets from clutter by Doppler.
OCXO, TCXO, VCXO
Oven-controlled, temperature-compensated, voltage-controlled crystal oscillators.
Offset frequency f
Distance from the carrier at which noise or a spur is measured.
Paired echoes
Symmetric ghost images in SAR or pulse compression caused by a sinusoidal phase error.
PFD, fPD
Phase-frequency detector and the frequency at which it compares.
PLL
Phase-locked loop: VCO, divider, PFD, charge pump and loop filter that lock a VCO to a reference.
PLL figure of merit
PFD/charge-pump noise normalised to 1 Hz fPD and N = 1, dBc/Hz.
PRI, PRF
Pulse repetition interval and its inverse, the pulse repetition frequency.
Range correlation
Partial cancellation of transmitter phase noise in the receiver for short echo delays: factor 4·sin²(πfτ).
Reciprocal mixing
LO phase noise mixing a blocker onto the wanted channel.
Reference spur
Spur at ± fPD from charge-pump ripple on the tuning line.
Sφ(f)
Phase spectral density in rad²/Hz; equals 2·L(f) for small angles.
SEI
Specific emitter identification: telling individual emitters apart by their unintentional modulation.
Settling (lock) time
Time from a frequency command until the error stays within tolerance.
SFDR
Spurious-free dynamic range: carrier to largest spur, in dBc.
Sigma-delta (ΣΔ) modulator
Digital sequencer that dithers the divider and shapes its error noise to high offsets.
Spur
Discrete unwanted line at a fixed offset, in dBc.
Subclutter visibility
Largest clutter-to-target ratio at which a target is still detected.
VCO
Voltage-controlled oscillator.
YIG
Yttrium iron garnet sphere resonator, tuned by a magnetic field over multiple octaves.
General textbook material: Banerjee, PLL Performance, Simulation, and Design; Rohde, Microwave and Wireless Synthesizers: Theory and Design; Best, Phase-Locked Loops: Design, Simulation, and Applications; Skolnik, Introduction to Radar Systems and Radar Handbook; Carrara, Goodman and Majewski, Spotlight Synthetic Aperture Radar; Leeson's oscillator noise model as presented in these texts.
