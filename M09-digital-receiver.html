The digital receiver: DDC, FPGA processing and pulse compression

Once the ADC has turned the IF into numbers, everything else the receiver does is arithmetic: tuning to the wanted band, filtering and slowing the data down, compressing long pulses into sharp ones, separating targets by their Doppler, deciding which cells hold targets, and doing all of it in a fixed number of bits inside an FPGA clocked at a few hundred MHz. Each of those steps has design parameters (word lengths, table sizes, filter stages, window shapes, threshold constants) and every one of them shows up as a system number: range resolution, range and Doppler sidelobes, false targets, false alarms, dynamic range and reaction time. This page builds those links from first principles, one block at a time, with worked numbers and a bench for each.

Step 0

The chain after the ADC, and why it lives in an FPGA

You already know

The analog receiver ends at an ADC that samples an IF signal. Module 07 (ADC, DAC and sampling) covers what happens at the converter itself: sampling rate, aliasing, ENOB, SFDR, jitter and noise density in dBFS/Hz. This page starts where that one stops: with a stream of numbers coming out of the ADC pins.

Definitions

Sample: one number from the ADC, here a 14-bit integer, produced every 1/fs seconds. fs, sample rate: samples per second, written MSPS or GSPS (mega or giga samples per second).

FPGA (field-programmable gate array): a chip made of thousands of small logic blocks, memories and hard multipliers that can be wired into any circuit by loading a configuration file. The circuit runs all its parts at once, every clock cycle.

DSP block: a hard multiplier-and-adder inside the FPGA (typically 18 × 27 bits, running at 400–600 MHz). The number of DSP blocks is usually the scarce resource.

Firmware: in this context, the FPGA configuration: the circuit you describe in VHDL or Verilog. Its design choices (word lengths, table sizes, filter stages) are the "components" of the digital receiver.

Data rate: bits per second on a link: sample rate × bits per sample (× 2 for complex samples).

In a radar or ESM receiver the digital part does six jobs, always in roughly this order. Each is a step on this page.

Worked example used on the whole page. An X-band radar (10 GHz, wavelength λ = 3 cm) receives on an IF centred at 240 MHz. A 14-bit ADC samples it at fs = 1 GSPS. The transmitted pulse is a 10 µs chirp sweeping 20 MHz. The radar sends 64 pulses at a pulse repetition frequency (PRF) of 10 kHz for each look. The numbers below follow those samples through the chain.

The ADC produces 1 × 109 × 14 = 14 Gbit/s. After tuning to 0 Hz the samples become complex pairs (two numbers each, Step 1) and briefly 32 Gbit/s at 16 bits; after decimation by 16 (Step 3) only 62.5 MSPS × 2 × 16 = 2 Gbit/s remain. After the matched filter, Doppler FFT and CFAR, what leaves the processor is a list of detections: a few hundred numbers per look, kbit/s.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive approach sends the raw ADC samples to a general-purpose processor and does the maths in software. Count the work for the matched filter alone (Step 4): the 10 µs pulse at 62.5 MSPS is 625 complex samples, so each output sample needs 625 complex multiplies = 2 500 real multiplies, and there are 62.5 million outputs per second: 156 × 109 multiplies per second, for one channel, before Doppler or CFAR. A processor core delivers a few × 109. Worse, the 14 Gbit/s input has to reach it continuously, with no gaps. An FPGA with 2 000 DSP blocks at 400 MHz delivers 800 × 109 multiplies per second, deterministically, with data arriving on its own pins. That is why the first stages of every modern radar and ESM receiver are FPGA firmware, and why its word lengths and table sizes are as much a part of the receiver specification as the LNA noise figure.

Step 1

Complex (I/Q) signals from first principles

You already know

A mixer with a quadrature LO (two LO paths 90° apart) gives two outputs, I and Q, and an image-reject mixer uses them to cancel the image. Module 05 (Mixers, LO and frequency conversion) derives image rejection from amplitude and phase imbalance. Here we look at what I and Q mean as numbers, and why the digital receiver works with them everywhere.

Definitions

Real signal: one number per sample, for example the ADC output x[n] = cos(2π f n / fs).

Negative frequency: a cosine is the sum of two rotating arrows (phasors) turning in opposite directions: cos(θ) = ½ e+jθ + ½ e−jθ. The arrow turning anticlockwise is frequency +f, the one turning clockwise is −f. A real signal always contains both, with equal size, so its spectrum is a mirror image about 0 Hz.

Complex sample, I and Q: two numbers per sample, written I + jQ. I is the in-phase (cosine) part, Q the quadrature (sine) part. Together they give the position of one arrow, so they can describe a signal at +f without its mirror at −f: e+jθ = cos θ + j sin θ.

Image (in this step): the unwanted mirror component at −f. Image rejection ratio (IRR): wanted power over image power, in dB.

Amplitude imbalance and phase error: in an analog I/Q demodulator, the Q path gain differs from the I path gain (dB) and the two LO paths are not exactly 90° apart (degrees). Both let some of the mirror back in.

A real sample stream cannot tell +f from −f, because cos(+θ) = cos(−θ). That matters as soon as the sign carries information. In a radar the sign of the Doppler shift says whether the target approaches or recedes. At 10 GHz a target closing at 300 m/s gives fd = 2v/λ = 2 × 300 / 0.03 = +20 kHz; one opening at 300 m/s gives −20 kHz. After a real down-conversion to 0 Hz both become cos(2π × 20 kHz × t): identical.

With I and Q the two are different: for the closing target (+20 kHz, I = cos, Q = sin) Q lags I by 90° and the arrow turns anticlockwise; for the opening one (−20 kHz) Q leads I and the arrow turns clockwise. The pair of numbers keeps the direction of rotation. A second benefit is bandwidth: with real samples only 0 to fs/2 is usable (Module 07); with complex samples the whole span −fs/2 to +fs/2 is usable, so the same sample rate carries twice the bandwidth.

xcomplex[n] = I[n] + j·Q[n] = A·ej(2π f n / fs + φ)    IRR = (1 + 2g·cos ε + g²) / (1 − 2g·cos ε + g²)g = Q-path gain / I-path gain (linear), ε = phase error from 90°. With g = 1 and ε = 0 the denominator is 0: the image vanishes completely.

What goes wrong with the simplest approach

The simplest receiver samples the real signal only. Two things go wrong. (1) Every signal appears twice, at +f and at −f, with equal size (image 0 dB below the signal): the radar cannot tell a closing target from an opening one, and a signal above the tuned frequency lands on top of one below it. (2) To avoid the overlap the signal must sit entirely on one side of 0 Hz, which wastes half the bandwidth. The common analog fix, an I/Q demodulator with two ADCs, has 0.5 dB and 3° of imbalance as a typical untrimmed figure: IRR = 28.2 dB. A strong clutter return 60 dB above noise then leaves a mirror 32 dB above noise at the opposite Doppler: a false target.

How to choose the representation

(1) If the sign of frequency matters (Doppler sign, signals on both sides of the tuned frequency, ESM spectra), you need complex samples. (2) If the image level must be below about −40 dBc (radar clutter rejection, ESM spurious-free range), make I/Q digitally: sample the real IF and use an NCO and mixer (Step 2). (3) Analog I/Q is still used where the bandwidth is too wide for one ADC (several GHz), and then it needs calibration: each 0.1 dB and 1° of residual imbalance is worth about 40 dB of IRR, each 0.5 dB and 3° about 28 dB.

Step 2

The NCO and digital down-conversion

You already know

An analog mixer multiplies the RF by an LO and shifts the signal to RF − LO. A synthesiser's spurs and phase noise end up on every signal it converts (Module 02, Synthesiser and phase noise). A digital down-converter does the same with numbers, and its "LO" also has spurs, set by firmware word lengths.

Definitions

DDC (digital down-converter): the NCO, a complex multiplier (the mixer) and the decimation filter (Step 3). It shifts the wanted band from the IF to 0 Hz and turns the real ADC stream into complex samples.

NCO (numerically controlled oscillator): a digital LO. It produces cos and sin of a phase that advances by a fixed amount every sample.

Phase accumulator: an N-bit register (N = 32 here) that adds the tuning word every clock and wraps around at 2N. Its value is the phase: 0 to 2N maps to 0 to 360°.

Tuning word (FTW): the number added each clock. FTW = round(fNCO / fs × 2N). Frequency step: fs/2N, the smallest change of NCO frequency.

Phase truncation: only the top P bits of the accumulator are used to look up the sine; the lower N − P bits are thrown away. LUT (lookup table): the memory holding 2P sine and cosine values, each stored with A bits (the amplitude word).

Spur (here): a discrete unwanted tone created by the periodic pattern of the truncation error. dBc: dB relative to the wanted output. Dither: a small random number added to the phase before truncation, which breaks the pattern up into noise.

The NCO is a counter that wraps. With fs = 1 GSPS and a 32-bit accumulator, the frequency step is 109/232 = 0.233 Hz, so the NCO can be set to 240 MHz to a fraction of a hertz: FTW = 240/1000 × 232 = 1 030 792 151. Phase is exact inside the accumulator. The error comes from using only P bits of it: with P = 12, the LUT has 4 096 entries and the phase is rounded to 360°/4 096 = 0.088°. The error is a sawtooth that repeats in a pattern set by the low bits of the tuning word, so it creates spurs instead of noise.

The mixer multiplies each real ADC sample by cos − j·sin from the NCO. A 245 MHz input tone and a 240 MHz NCO give the wanted tone at 245 − 240 = +5 MHz and a "sum" product at −(245 + 240) = −485 MHz. The sum product is far outside the wanted band; the decimation filter (Step 3) removes it. NCO spurs are multiplied onto every input signal, exactly like synthesiser spurs on an analog LO.

FTW = round(fNCO·2N/fs)    Δf = fs/2N    worst phase-truncation spur ≈ −6.02·P dBc    amplitude SNR ≈ 6.02·A + 1.76 dBEach extra phase bit halves the largest phase error and lowers the worst spur by 6 dB, but doubles the table. The amplitude word acts like an ADC word length on the LO (Step 7).

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive design uses a small table to save memory: P = 8 (256 entries). Worst spur ≈ −48 dBc. In an ESM receiver a strong radar at −20 dBm then makes spurs at −68 dBm, above a −70 dBm sensitivity: the receiver reports emitters that do not exist. In a radar, clutter 60 dB above noise makes spurs 12 dB above noise at fixed offsets in Doppler or range: false targets that move with the clutter. A table of 216 entries (−96 dBc) removes both problems; what it costs is block memory.

Dither does not remove the error; it turns the discrete spurs into a slightly higher noise floor, which a detector treats as noise rather than as a signal. Quarter-wave symmetry stores only 0–90° and derives the other three quadrants by sign changes, cutting the table by 4. CORDIC (coordinate rotation digital computer) computes sin and cos by a series of shift-and-add rotations, trading memory for adders and latency.

How to choose N, P and A

(1) N from the tuning step you need: 32 bits gives 0.23 Hz at 1 GSPS, far finer than any radar needs, and costs almost nothing. (2) P from the spur level the system tolerates: the worst spur must be below the largest signal minus the dynamic range you need. For 80 dB clean range, P ≥ 80/6.02 ≈ 14 bits. (3) A about 2 bits more than the ADC word, so the NCO's amplitude noise (6.02A + 1.76 dB) stays below the ADC's own: 14-bit ADC → A = 16. (4) If memory is short, use dither or CORDIC instead of a smaller table. (5) Avoid tuning words whose low N − P bits are zero or simple fractions: those give the strongest, most regular spurs.

Step 3

Decimation and filtering: CIC, FIR, droop, alias rejection and processing gain

You already know

Sampling folds every frequency above fs/2 back into the band (aliasing, Module 07), which is why an analog anti-alias filter sits in front of the ADC. Inside the FPGA the same thing happens again every time the sample rate is reduced, and the anti-alias filter is now digital. Module 04 (Filters and preselection) covers the analog filter words used here: passband, stop band, droop and ripple.

Definitions

Decimation by R: keeping one sample in every R. The output rate is fo = fs/R (1 000/16 = 62.5 MSPS). It is done because the wanted band (here ±10 MHz) is much narrower than the input rate: carrying 1 GSPS through the matched filter and FFT would waste 16 times the multipliers.

Alias band: after decimation, any frequency f lands at f − k·fo (k = 1, 2, …). The bands k·fo ± fp land exactly on the passband ±fp, so they must be suppressed before decimating. Alias rejection: the smallest attenuation over all those bands.

Passband edge fp: the highest wanted frequency. On the bench it is set as a fraction of the output Nyquist frequency fo/2; ±10 MHz at fo = 62.5 MSPS is 32 %.

CIC filter (cascaded integrator-comb): N integrators (running sums) at the input rate, then decimation, then N combs (differences) at the output rate. It needs no multipliers, only adders, so it runs at 1 GSPS easily. Its response is a sinc to the power N, with nulls exactly at the centres of the alias bands. Stages N: how many integrator-comb pairs.

Droop: the loss at the passband edge relative to 0 Hz. A CIC droops because its sinc response is already falling at fp. Bit growth: the integrators grow by N·log2R bits and must not overflow (Step 7).

FIR filter (finite impulse response): a weighted sum of the last K samples (K = taps), one multiplier per tap. It can have any shape, including the inverse of the CIC droop (a compensating FIR), but costs multipliers.

Processing gain: the SNR improvement from removing noise outside the wanted band: 10·log10R for an ideal filter.

The ADC noise is spread over the whole 1 GHz input span. The wanted signal occupies 20 MHz of it. An ideal filter that keeps only the output band (fo = 62.5 MHz wide, complex) removes 15/16 of the noise: 10·log1016 = 12.0 dB processing gain. That is why a 14-bit ADC with 70 dB SNR in 500 MHz delivers 82 dB SNR in the decimated band, and why the words after the decimator must be longer than the ADC word (Step 7).

The filter has two jobs: keep the passband flat (low droop) and suppress the alias bands (high alias rejection). For R = 16, fo = 62.5 MHz and fp = 10 MHz, the first alias band is 52.5–72.5 MHz; anything there would fold onto 0 ± 10 MHz after decimation.

|HCIC(f)| = | sin(π f R / fs) / (R · sin(π f / fs)) |N    bit growth = N·log2R    processing gain = 10·log10R (ideal)The nulls fall at k·fs/R = k·fo, the centres of the alias bands. Each stage multiplies the attenuation in dB by one more factor: N stages give N times the dB rejection and N times the dB droop.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive decimator just drops 15 of every 16 samples. There is no filter, so every alias band folds in at full strength: alias rejection 0 dB. A jammer or a second radar at 67.5 MHz offset appears at +5 MHz, on top of the target, at its full level. And all the noise of the 1 GHz span folds into the 62.5 MHz output: processing gain 0 dB instead of 12 dB. The SNR is the same as before decimation, and the dynamic range is filled with signals from outside the band.

How to choose R, N and the FIR

(1) R from the bandwidth: the output rate must exceed the signal bandwidth with room for the filter edge, typically fo ≥ 1.25 × B (20 MHz × 1.25 = 25 MHz minimum; 62.5 MSPS leaves a generous edge). Larger R gives more processing gain (10·log R) and fewer multipliers later. (2) N from the alias rejection needed: at least the dynamic range you want protected from out-of-band signals, for example 60 dB → N = 4 at this passband fraction. Narrower passband fractions give more rejection per stage. (3) Bit growth N·log2R must fit the integrator registers: 4 × 4 = 16 extra bits on a 14-bit input, so 30-bit registers. (4) The FIR where droop matters (pulse compression sidelobes, Step 4, are sensitive to passband ripple and tilt): its taps set how flat and how sharp; more taps cost multipliers and latency ((K − 1)/2 samples of delay).

Step 4

The matched filter and pulse compression

You already know

A radar's detection range depends on the pulse energy, peak power times pulse length, and its range resolution depends on how short the pulse is (R1, and R3 Detection). A filter's group delay ripple raises compression sidelobes (Module 04, Step 4). This step shows how the digital receiver gets both energy and resolution from one long pulse.

Definitions

Matched filter: a filter whose impulse response is the expected echo reversed in time and conjugated. It gives the highest possible peak SNR for that echo in white noise: SNR = 2E/N0, where E is the echo energy and N0 the noise density. Only the energy counts, not the shape. In the FPGA it is a FIR whose taps are the transmitted waveform, or the same done with FFTs (Step 8).

Range resolution ΔR: how close two targets can be and still appear as two peaks: ΔR = c/(2B) for a waveform of bandwidth B. For an unmodulated pulse B ≈ 1/T, giving ΔR = cT/2.

Chirp (LFM, linear frequency modulation): a pulse whose frequency sweeps linearly across B during its length T. Time-bandwidth product BT: B × T; for a chirp it is the compression ratio, the factor by which the pulse is shortened.

Range sidelobes: the smaller peaks either side of the compressed pulse. PSL (peak sidelobe level): the highest of them, in dB relative to the peak.

Weighting (taper): multiplying the matched filter's taps by a window (Hamming, Taylor, Blackman) that is smaller at the ends, which lowers the sidelobes. Mismatch (weighting) loss: the SNR lost because the filter is then no longer exactly matched.

Phase code: a pulse cut into chips with phase 0° or 180°, for example the 13-chip Barker code. Doppler tolerance: how much peak and shape survive when the echo is shifted in frequency by fd. Range-Doppler coupling: for a chirp, a Doppler shift moves the peak in range by fd·T/B instead of destroying it.

Worked example: 1 kW peak, T = 10 µs, B = 20 MHz. Energy E = 1 000 × 10 × 10−6 = 10 mJ. Without modulation the 10 µs pulse resolves c·T/2 = 1.5 km: two aircraft 1 km apart are one blob. With a 20 MHz chirp the matched filter compresses it to a peak 1/B = 50 ns wide: ΔR = c/(2B) = 7.5 m. The compression ratio is BT = 200 (23.0 dB): the peak is 200 times shorter, and 200 times higher in amplitude relative to the uncompressed echo, with all 10 mJ still there.

The matched filter output is the cross-correlation of the echo with the reference. For a rectangular-envelope chirp it is a sinc-shaped peak with sidelobes at −13.3 dB, decaying slowly. A weighting window lowers them, widens the peak and costs a little SNR.

What goes wrong with the naive approaches

Naive 1: a short pulse for resolution. 7.5 m resolution with an unmodulated pulse needs T = 50 ns. At the same 1 kW the energy is 200 times smaller (−23 dB) and detection range falls by 2001/4 = 3.8 times. Getting the energy back needs 200 kW peak: a different transmitter, a different limiter, a different duplexer.

Naive 2: compression without weighting. The rectangular chirp's first sidelobes sit at −13.3 dB. A 100 m² ship and a 1 m² boat 20 m away differ by 20 dB; the ship's sidelobe is 6.7 dB above the boat. The boat is hidden, or worse, the CFAR (Step 6) reports the sidelobe as a target at the wrong range.

(3) Weighting from the sidelobe level needed: the largest target-to-target ratio you must resolve, typically −30 to −40 dB, so Taylor 35 dB at about 1 dB loss. (4) Remember that the system sidelobe floor also includes the analog chain: group-delay ripple, amplitude ripple (Module 04) and synthesiser chirp errors (Module 02) all add paired echoes; weighting cannot remove those.

Step 5

FFT and Doppler processing: windows, scalloping loss and resolution

You already know

A spectrum analyser shows a tone as a peak whose width depends on the resolution bandwidth, and a strong tone's skirts can hide a weak one next to it. The Doppler FFT is a spectrum analyser built in firmware, working on the pulse-to-pulse samples of each range cell.

Definitions

Fast time and slow time: within one pulse repetition interval (PRI = 1/PRF) the samples step through range ("fast time", 62.5 MSPS). Taking the sample at the same range from each successive pulse gives a much slower sequence ("slow time", one sample per PRI = 10 kHz) whose frequency is the target's Doppler shift fd = 2v/λ.

CPI (coherent processing interval): the M pulses processed together; it lasts M/PRF (64/10 kHz = 6.4 ms).

FFT (fast Fourier transform): an efficient way to compute the DFT, which turns M slow-time samples into M frequency bins, spaced PRF/M apart (156.25 Hz = 2.34 m/s here). The FFT needs (M/2)·log2M complex multiplies instead of M².

Coherent gain: adding M samples of a tone in phase raises its power M² times while the noise power rises M times: SNR gain 10·log10M (18.1 dB for 64).

Unambiguous velocity: slow-time sampling at the PRF folds Doppler beyond ±PRF/2, so velocities are unambiguous only within ±λ·PRF/4 = ±75 m/s here.

Window: weights applied to the M samples before the FFT, smaller at the ends. Spectral sidelobes: the skirts of a strong line, set by the window. Scalloping loss: the loss when a tone falls between two bins instead of on one; worst exactly half-way. Window SNR loss (processing loss): the coherent-gain reduction caused by the taper. Resolution: the −3 dB width of a line, in bins.

Clutter: echoes from ground, sea or rain, here at near-zero velocity and up to 70 dB above noise.

The FFT tests the slow-time sequence against M equally spaced frequencies at once. A target at exactly a bin frequency puts all its energy in that bin; one half-way between two bins splits it and its peak drops by the scalloping loss. A rectangular window (no taper) gives the sharpest line but skirts that start at −13.3 dB and fall slowly; the skirts of a 50 dB clutter line can cover the whole Doppler axis.

Worked example: M = 64, PRF = 10 kHz, λ = 3 cm. Bin spacing 156.25 Hz = 2.34 m/s. A target at 20.3 m/s has fd = 1 353 Hz = 8.66 bins: it falls 0.34 bins off a bin centre. With a per-pulse SNR of −5 dB the FFT lifts it to −5 + 18.1 = 13.1 dB, minus the window and scalloping losses.

Step 6

CFAR as implemented in hardware

You already know

R3 (Detection) builds CFAR step by step: why one fixed threshold fails when the background changes, what the cell under test, gap (guard) cells and reference cells are, and the CA, GO, SO and OS methods. Read its Step 6 first if CFAR is new. This step recaps only the four terms the bench uses, then shows what changes when CFAR is built in an FPGA.

Definitions (recap from R3, then the hardware terms)

Range cell: one sample of the matched-filter (or Doppler) output; at 62.5 MSPS a cell is 2.4 m of range. Cell under test (CUT): the cell being decided now.

Reference cells, N: cells either side of the CUT (N/2 on each side) whose average estimates the local noise. More cells give a steadier estimate. On the bench N is the total; N = 16 means 8 each side.

Guard cells, G: cells right beside the CUT that are skipped, because a real target spills energy into its neighbours and would otherwise raise its own threshold (self-masking). G is per side.

Threshold factor α: the multiplier on the estimated noise; threshold = α × average. It is set once from the wanted Pfa (probability that a noise-only cell crosses the threshold): for cell averaging, α = N·(Pfa−1/N − 1).

CFAR loss: the extra SNR a target needs, compared with a detector that knows the noise exactly, to be detected with the same probability (here Pd = 0.5 on a fluctuating target). It exists because the estimate from N cells wobbles, so α must be larger than ln(1/Pfa).

CA and GO: cell-averaging uses the average of all N cells; greatest-of averages each side separately and uses the larger, which is safer at clutter edges. Fixed threshold: α × the noise measured once in a quiet region, not CFAR at all; it is on the bench only to show the problem.

Running sum: the hardware trick that makes CFAR simple: as the window slides by one cell, add the cell entering each half and subtract the one leaving. Cost: 4 adders per cell, whatever N is.

α implementation: how firmware multiplies by α. A DSP block multiplies exactly (to its word length). A shift multiplies by a power of two for free. Shift-and-add builds α from a few powers of two (22 = 16 + 4 + 2: three shifts and two adders).

Worked numbers for Pfa = 10−6. An ideal detector that knew the noise would need 12.77 dB SNR for Pd = 0.5. CA-CFAR with N = 16: α = 16 × (106/16 − 1) = 21.94 (13.41 dB), and it needs 2.00 dB more SNR: that is its CFAR loss.

In hardware the cells stream past at 62.5 MSPS, one per clock (or a few per clock, Step 8). The firmware keeps a shift register N + 2G + 1 cells long, two running sums, one multiplier and one comparator. The decision for a cell comes out N/2 + G cells after the cell itself arrives (the window must have seen the cells after it). At the start and end of the range sweep one side of the window is missing; the firmware then uses only the side it has, or GO logic.

What makes the α implementation matter is how steep Pfa is in α. A CA-CFAR's Pfa = (1 + α/N)−N. Round α = 21.94 to the power of two 16 to save a multiplier and Pfa becomes (1 + 1)−16 = 1.5 × 10−5: 15 times more false alarms. With 10 million cells per second (1 000 range cells × 64 Doppler bins every 6.4 ms), Pfa = 10−6 means 10 false alarms per second; 1.5 × 10−5 means 153 per second, enough to overload a tracker. Shift-and-add 22 = 16 + 4 + 2 gives 9.8 × 10−7, practically exact.

Naive 1: a fixed threshold. Set at ln(106) = 13.8 times the quiet noise, it gives 10−6 in quiet cells. Step 7

Fixed-point word length, rounding, overflow and saturation

You already know

An ADC with B bits has an ideal SNR of 6.02B + 1.76 dB for a full-scale sine, and clipping it creates harmonics (Module 07). Every register inside the FPGA is a small ADC in the same sense: each time a result is stored in fewer bits, the same rule applies again.

Definitions

Fixed point: numbers stored as integers with an agreed scale. A B-bit two's-complement word holds −2B−1 … 2B−1 − 1; with the scale "full scale = ±1" it is written Q1.(B−1), for example Q1.15 for 16 bits.

LSB (least significant bit): the step between neighbouring values, 2/2B of full scale. dBFS: dB relative to a full-scale sine.

Quantisation noise: the error of storing a value in B bits, spread evenly in ±½ LSB: power LSB²/12. For a full-scale sine it gives SQNR (signal to quantisation noise ratio) = 6.02B + 1.76 dB.

Requantisation: dropping low bits after an operation that grew the word (an adder adds 1 bit, a B × C multiplier gives B + C bits, a K-tap FIR adds log2K bits, an M-point FFT up to log2M bits).

Truncation: just discard the low bits (round towards −∞). Error 0 to −1 LSB, mean −½ LSB: a DC bias. Round half up: add ½ LSB then truncate; unbiased except for values exactly half-way, which always go up. Convergent rounding (round half to even): half-way values go to the even neighbour, so on average no bias.

Overflow: a result larger than the word can hold. Wrap-around: two's-complement arithmetic simply drops the top bit, so a value just above +full scale becomes nearly −full scale. Saturation: extra logic clamps the result to the largest value instead (a clip). Headroom: how far below full scale the largest normal signal is kept.

Worked numbers. 12 bits: SQNR = 6.02 × 12 + 1.76 = 74.0 dB; 16 bits: 98.1 dB. A 1 024-point FFT can grow a full-scale tone by 210 (10 bits) while the noise grows by only 25: the FFT's coherent gain (Step 5) is real only if the words have room for it. A 14-bit ADC (about 70 dB SNR) followed by 12 dB of processing gain in the decimator (Step 3) and 18 dB in a 64-point Doppler FFT ends with a signal that may lie 100 dB above a single LSB of the ADC: the words must grow to keep it.

The bench takes a result two bits longer than the word (as from an adder or one FFT stage) carrying a strong tone and a weak tone at −80 dBFS, stores it in B bits with the chosen rounding and overflow handling, and looks at its spectrum. Dropping exactly two bits makes the rounding differences easy to see: truncation biases by −⅜ LSB (−½ LSB when many bits are dropped), round half up by +⅛ LSB, convergent by 0.

SQNR = 6.02·B + 1.76 dB    noise per FFT bin ≈ −SQNR − 10·log10(M/2) + 10·log10(ENBW) dBFS    truncation bias = −½ LSBENBW: the window's equivalent noise bandwidth in bins. A weak tone below the SQNR can still be seen in an FFT, because the noise is shared among M/2 bins; a spur cannot be averaged away like that.

What goes wrong with the naive approaches

Naive 1: keep every word at 16 bits and let it overflow. A strong return that pushes a filter output 4 dB over full scale wraps: the top of every cycle jumps to the bottom. The spectrum fills with spurs only 12 dB below the strong signal and the floor rises by 50 dB; every spur is a false target or a false emitter.

Naive 2: avoid overflow by scaling ½ in every FFT stage. A 1 024-point FFT has 10 stages, so the signal is divided by 1 024 (60 dB) before the coherent gain is applied. Small signals fall below the LSB and are lost; the noise floor of the output is set by the word, not by the input.

Naive 3: truncate everywhere. Each truncation adds −½ LSB of DC. Ten requantisations in a row add up to −5 LSB: a false "target" at zero Doppler and zero frequency, which the clutter filter then has to remove.

How to choose word lengths and rounding

(1) Start from the ADC: keep the noise of each requantisation at least 10 dB below the noise already in the signal, which means about 2 bits below the signal's own noise level. (4) Saturate at every point where overflow is possible, and count saturations in a status register. (5) Round convergently wherever a word is shortened, especially in long chains; truncate only where a DC bias does no harm. (6) DSP blocks come in 18 × 27 bits: going beyond 18 bits on one input doubles the multipliers, so word length is also a resource decision.

  • 8 bits: the floor rises by about 20 dB and quantisation spurs appear near −67 dBFS; the weak −80 dBFS tone is now −16.1 dB relative to the largest spur.
  • Raise the strong tone to +4 dBFS with wrap-around: largest spur −8.2 dBFS (12 dB below the tone) and the floor jumps to about −55 dBFS; switch to saturate: harmonics at −14.1 dBFS with the floor still near −107 dBFS. Back at −1 dBFS: no overflow.
  • B = 20 bits: the floor falls to about −150 dBFS per bin, 6 dB per bit below the 12-bit case, and the weak tone stands far clear of it.

A 14-bit ADC feeds a decimate-by-16 filter and then a 64-point FFT. How many bits should the FFT output word have?

Start at 14 bits plus 2 guard bits (16). Decimator processing gain 12 dB → +2 bits (18). FFT coherent gain up to 18 dB (log2 64 = 6 bits of growth for a tone) → 24 bits, or 18 bits with block floating point. Then saturation at the output for anything larger.

Step 8

Latency and throughput

You already know

A filter delays a signal by its group delay, and a long pulse is not "received" until its end has arrived. Digital processing adds its own delays, and it can only keep up with the ADC if enough of it runs in parallel.

Definitions

FPGA clock fclk: the rate at which the firmware's registers update, typically 250–500 MHz. Throughput: samples per second a block can accept. A block that handles one sample per clock has throughput fclk.

Parallel paths (samples per clock): when the ADC rate exceeds the clock, the firmware handles P = ⌈fs/fclk⌉ samples in every clock, with P copies of the logic (1 GSPS at 250 MHz → P = 4).

Pipeline: splitting a calculation into stages with registers between them, so the clock can be fast; each stage adds one clock of delay. Latency: the time from a sample entering a block to its result leaving it.

FIR group delay: a symmetric K-tap FIR delays the signal by (K − 1)/2 samples. Fast convolution: doing the matched filter with an FFT, a multiplication and an inverse FFT on blocks of L samples (overlap-save), which costs about L·log2L operations per block instead of L × taps, but adds a block of latency.

CPI wait: the Doppler FFT cannot start until all M pulses have arrived: M/PRF.

Real multipliers: a complex multiply needs 4 real multiplies (3 with a trick). On the bench, multipliers are counted as DSP blocks running at fclk.

Worked example: 1 GSPS ADC, 250 MHz clock → 4 samples per clock; the NCO and mixer are built four times. After decimation by 16 (62.5 MSPS), one FIR multiplier at 250 MHz can serve 4 taps, so a 63-tap complex compensating FIR needs 2 × 63 × 62.5/250 ≈ 32 DSP blocks. The matched filter for a 10 µs pulse has 625 complex taps: done directly, 4 × 625 × 62.5/250 = 625 DSP blocks; done with 2 048-point FFTs, about 18.

Latency adds up stage by stage, but one stage always dominates. The DDC, CIC, FIR and CFAR take tens to hundreds of nanoseconds. The matched filter cannot answer before the pulse has ended (10 µs), plus the FFT block if fast convolution is used. The Doppler FFT has to wait for the whole CPI: 64 pulses at 10 kHz = 6.4 ms. That is 1 000 times longer than everything else together.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive design writes each block for one sample per clock, as a software engineer would. At 1 GSPS and 250 MHz it keeps up with only a quarter of the samples: three in four are dropped, and the dropped samples alias like Naive decimation in Step 3. The second naive choice is a direct FIR matched filter: 625 DSP blocks for one channel of one waveform. A 16-channel array then needs 10 000 DSP blocks, more than most FPGAs have.

How to choose the clock, parallelism and method

(1) Clock: as fast as the design closes timing, usually 250–400 MHz; P then follows from the ADC rate. (2) Decimate early: every block after the decimator costs R times less; that is the main reason for Step 3. (3) Matched filter: direct FIR for short pulses (tens of taps, lowest latency); fast convolution for long ones (hundreds or thousands of taps). (4) CPI: latency is a waveform choice: a jammer or missile seeker that must react within microseconds cannot use a 6.4 ms Doppler FFT, and an ESM receiver that must hand a pulse to a jammer within 1 µs uses short FFTs or no FFT at all. (5) Leave 20–30 % of DSP blocks and memory free: designs that fill an FPGA fail timing.

Step 9

System effects: radar resolution and sidelobes, Doppler, ESM channelisation and SAR

You already know

Steps 1–8 each produced a firmware choice and a specification. This step collects them per system and adds the one digital block that only ESM receivers use: the channeliser.

Definitions

Channeliser: a bank of K band-pass filters that splits a wide band (1 GHz here) into K adjacent channels, each with its own decimated output, all at once. It lets an ESM receiver see every frequency all the time while each channel has a narrow noise bandwidth.

Channel spacing: fs/K (1 000/16 = 62.5 MHz). Channel bandwidth: the −3 dB width of one channel's response.

FFT channeliser: a K-point FFT on blocks of K samples; each bin is a channel, with the window's response as its filter shape. Polyphase filter bank (PFB): the same FFT preceded by a long prototype filter (8 × K taps here) split into K branches, which gives channels with a flat top and steep skirts for little extra cost.

Crossover loss: the loss for a signal exactly between two channels. Channel leakage (splatter): a strong signal appearing in channels other than its own through the filter skirts; each appearance can become a false emitter report.

Sensitivity gain: narrowing the noise bandwidth from the whole band to one channel improves sensitivity by 10·log10K, less the window loss. Shortest pulse: a pulse shorter than about 1/(channel bandwidth) is smeared by the channel filter and loses amplitude.

Radar range. Range resolution is c/(2B) widened by the weighting: 6.6 m at −3 dB unweighted, 8.9 m with Taylor 35 dB. The range sidelobe floor is the worst of: the weighting's PSL (−35 dB), the analog ripple paired echoes (Module 04), NCO spurs (Step 2, −72 dBc at P = 12) and fixed-point spurs (Step 7). The CFAR then sets the false-alarm rate on that floor (Step 6).

Radar Doppler. Velocity resolution is the window width × λ·PRF/(2M): 1.90 × 2.34 = 4.45 m/s with Blackman-Harris; clutter visibility is set by the window's far sidelobes and by the image level of the I/Q (Step 1); the reaction time includes the CPI (Step 8).

ESM. An ESM receiver digitising 1 GHz has the sensitivity of a 1 GHz noise bandwidth unless it channelises. With K = 16 channels of 62.5 MHz, sensitivity improves by 12 dB; with 64 channels of 15.6 MHz by 18 dB, but pulses shorter than about 1/BW (about 18 ns and 72 ns for an unwindowed FFT) are smeared. The channel shape decides whether a signal between two channels is lost (crossover loss) and whether a strong signal shows up in many channels (leakage).

SAR. A SAR image is two matched filters: range compression across the chirp (Step 4) and azimuth compression across the Doppler history collected as the platform flies past (Part 3, SAR imaging). The image quality numbers are the same as for radar range: resolution c/(2B) and ρaz from the synthetic aperture, PSLR (peak sidelobe ratio, Step 4) and ISLR (integrated sidelobe ratio: all sidelobe energy relative to the peak). A dark sea next to a bright ship needs ISLR below about −20 dB and words long enough to hold the 60–80 dB scene dynamic range (Step 7). SAR processing is not real-time critical in latency (Step 8) but is enormous in throughput.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive channeliser is a plain K-point FFT with no window. Each channel is a sinc with −13.3 dB first sidelobes: a strong radar pulse appears in about all 16 of 16 channels above −40 dB, so the ESM reports several emitters at wrong frequencies. A signal half-way between channels loses up to 3.92 dB, a sensitivity hole at every channel edge: at 218.75 MHz the bench reads 3.84 dB of loss.

How to choose the channeliser

(1) K from the sensitivity needed and the shortest pulse to be measured: more channels → better sensitivity (10·log K) but worse pulse-width resolution (≈ K/fs). Many ESM receivers run two channelisers with different K in parallel. (2) Shape: use a polyphase bank whenever strong and weak emitters coexist; its leakage is far below a windowed FFT's at almost the same cost (8 × K extra multipliers). (3) Oversample 2× to remove crossover loss when the sensitivity must be uniform across the band. (4) Word lengths after the channeliser grow by the 10·log K gain (Step 7).

Glossary of terms introduced on this page

α (threshold factor)
CFAR multiplier on the estimated noise; set from Pfa and N.
Alias band
Frequencies k·fo ± fp that fold onto the passband after decimation.
Bit growth
Extra bits a result needs: +1 per add, log2K for a K-tap FIR, N·log2R for a CIC.
Block floating point
A block of samples sharing one exponent, so a fixed-point FFT can scale only when needed.
CA / GO CFAR
Cell-averaging (mean of all reference cells) / greatest-of (larger of the two half means).
Cell under test (CUT)
The range or Doppler cell the CFAR is deciding.
CFAR loss
Extra SNR needed because the noise is estimated from N cells rather than known.
Channeliser
Bank of K filters splitting a wide band into channels with decimated outputs.
CIC filter
Cascaded integrator-comb: multiplier-free decimation filter with a sincN response.
Coherent gain
SNR gain from adding M samples in phase: 10·log10M.
Compression ratio
Factor by which pulse compression shortens the pulse; B·T for a chirp.
Convergent rounding
Round to nearest, half-way values to the even neighbour; no average bias.
CORDIC
Shift-and-add method of computing sin, cos and rotations without a table.
CPI
Coherent processing interval: the M pulses processed together, lasting M/PRF.
Crossover loss
Loss of a signal lying exactly between two channels or bins.
DDC
Digital down-converter: NCO, complex mixer and decimation filter.
Decimation
Keeping one sample in every R after filtering; output rate fs/R.
Dither
Small random addition that turns periodic quantisation error (spurs) into noise.
DSP block
Hard multiplier-accumulator inside an FPGA, the usual limiting resource.
Droop
Loss at the passband edge relative to the centre.
Fast convolution
Filtering by FFT, multiply and inverse FFT on blocks (overlap-save).
Fast time / slow time
Samples along range within a pulse / samples of one range cell from pulse to pulse.
FFT bin
One output frequency of an M-point FFT; spacing fsample/M.
FIR filter
Weighted sum of the last K samples; any shape, one multiplier per tap.
FPGA
Field-programmable gate array: reconfigurable logic with memories and DSP blocks.
FTW
Frequency tuning word added to the NCO accumulator each clock.
Guard cells
Cells beside the CUT left out of the CFAR estimate to avoid self-masking.
Headroom
Margin between the largest normal signal and full scale.
I/Q, complex sample
Pair of numbers (in-phase, quadrature) that represent one rotating phasor, keeping the sign of frequency.
Image rejection ratio
Wanted over mirror power; set by I/Q amplitude and phase balance.
ISLR
Integrated sidelobe ratio: total sidelobe energy relative to the mainlobe.
Latency
Time from input to result through a block or the whole chain.
LFM, chirp
Pulse with frequency swept linearly across B in time T.
LSB
Smallest step of a fixed-point word, 2/2B of full scale.
LUT
Lookup table: memory holding precomputed values such as sine samples.
Matched filter
Filter equal to the time-reversed conjugate of the expected echo; maximises peak SNR to 2E/N0.

SNR lost by weighting a matched filter: (Σw)²/(N·Σw²).
NCO
Numerically controlled oscillator: phase accumulator plus sine/cosine generation.
Overflow, wrap, saturate
Result too large for the word; two's-complement wraps to the opposite sign; saturation clamps it.

Copies of logic handling several samples per clock when fs > fclk.

Using only the top P accumulator bits to address the sine table; source of NCO spurs.
Pipeline
Registers between parts of a calculation; raises the clock rate, adds latency.

Channeliser made of a long prototype filter split into K branches followed by an FFT.

SNR improvement from filtering or integration; 10·log R for decimation, 10·log M for an FFT.
PSL, PSLR
Peak sidelobe level (ratio): highest sidelobe relative to the peak.

Cells either side of the CUT whose average estimates the local noise.
