The FMCW radar, end to end

R4 followed a pulse radar. An FMCW radar does the same jobs (measure range, measure speed, survive strong close echoes, find a small target) with a completely different trick: it never stops transmitting, and it measures range as a frequency instead of a time. This page builds it up block by block, shows why the trick lets a 150 MHz-wide radar run on a slow ADC, and explains the three problems that are special to FMCW: transmitter leakage into the receiver, phase noise, and ramp nonlinearity.

Step 0

What changes from a pulse radar

You already know

From the FMCW page and R1: in an FMCW radar the transmit frequency rises linearly (a chirp or ramp), the slope S = B/T (bandwidth over ramp time), and the frequency difference between transmitted and received signal is proportional to range. From R4: a pulse radar is blind while it transmits, and it measures range as a time.

Four differences drive everything on this page:

  1. It transmits all the time. There is no "transmit, then listen". So there is no blind time and no blind range from switching (R4 Step 3), and the transmit power can be low (milliwatts to watts) because the energy comes from the long ramp, not from a high peak.
  2. It receives at the same time as it transmits. So it cannot use a T/R switch to protect the receiver. It usually uses two antennas, and some transmit power always leaks straight into the receiver (Step 4).
  3. Range is measured as a frequency, not a time. The echo is a delayed copy of the chirp. Mixing it with the chirp being transmitted right now gives a low "beat" frequency proportional to range (Step 2).
  4. The range cells come from an FFT, not from ADC samples. In R3 a range cell was one ADC sample after the pulse. In FMCW a range cell is one frequency bin of an FFT of the beat signal (Step 3).

Step 2

Dechirp: how range becomes a low frequency

The problem. To resolve 1 m in range, a radar needs about 150 MHz of bandwidth (c/2B, R1). A pulse radar with 150 MHz of bandwidth needs an ADC sampling at more than 300 MHz, and a lot of processing. FMCW avoids that with one mixer.

The trick, step by step.

  1. The transmitter sweeps from f0 up by B in time T. Slope S = B / T.
  2. An echo from range R arrives τ = 2R/c later. It is the same ramp, shifted right by τ.
  3. At any moment, the echo's frequency is behind the transmitter's by S · τ, because the transmitter has kept climbing for τ seconds.
  4. The mixer multiplies the echo with the current transmit signal. Its output contains the difference frequency, the beat frequency fb = S · τ = 2SR/c.
  5. The beat is constant for the whole ramp (straight lines stay parallel), so it is a clean, steady tone whose frequency says the range.
fb = S · τ = (B / T) · (2R / c)    R = c · fb / (2S)
Worked: B = 150 MHz, T = 100 µs (S = 1.5 MHz/µs)Delay τBeat fb

Why this matters for hardware. The radar uses 150 MHz of RF bandwidth, yet everything after the mixer only has to handle 0–10 MHz for targets out to 1 km. A 20 MS/s ADC is enough. This "bandwidth compression" is why FMCW radars are cheap and small, and why the IF chain and ADC of an FMCW radar look nothing like those of a pulse radar.

Try it: top, frequency against time for the transmitted ramp (purple) and the echo (teal, delayed by τ, drawn exaggerated so it is visible). The orange arrow is the frequency gap, which is the beat. Bottom: the beat signal the mixer produces during one ramp. Things to check: (1) double the range: the beat doubles; (2) halve T with B fixed: the slope doubles and the beat doubles; (3) at 3 km with B = 150 MHz and T = 100 µs the beat is 30 MHz: a 20 MS/s ADC can no longer take it (Step 3).

Step 3

FFT bins are the range cells

From a tone to a range. The ADC samples the beat signal during the ramp. An FFT of those samples shows a peak at each beat frequency, one per target. Each FFT bin corresponds to a range, so each bin is a range cell, and the CFAR of R3 runs along the bins exactly as it ran along the samples of a pulse radar.

Where c/2B comes from. An FFT over a record of length T has bins 1/T apart in frequency. One bin of beat frequency equals a range step of

ΔR = c · (1/T) / (2S) = c · (1/T) / (2B/T) = c / (2B)

The ramp time cancels: range resolution depends only on the swept bandwidth. With B = 150 MHz, ΔR = 1.0 m; each bin is 10 kHz wide (1/100 µs) and each 10 kHz of beat is 1 m of range.

Maximum range is set by the IF and the ADC. A real ADC at fs can represent beat frequencies up to fs/2. So Rmax = c · (fs/2) / (2S). With fs = 20 MS/s: 10 MHz → 1 km. An anti-alias low-pass before the ADC is essential: an echo from 3 km (30 MHz beat) would otherwise fold back and appear at a false short range, the FMCW version of R4's second-time-around echo.

The window. A plain FFT has −13 dB sidelobes, so a strong target's sidelobes can hide a weak one a few bins away. A window (Hann, −31 dB) lowers the sidelobes but widens each peak, so two targets need about 2 bins of separation instead of 1. This is exactly the taper trade of R2, now in the frequency domain.

Try it: the FFT of one ramp's beat signal, with its frequency axis converted to range (bins c/2B apart). Things to check: (1) with B = 150 MHz the bins are 1 m apart; set target 2 only 1 m further: they merge; (2) raise B to 600 MHz: bins are 25 cm and the two separate; (3) set target 2 to 35 dB weaker and choose the rectangular window: it disappears into the sidelobes of the strong target; switch to Hann, the sidelobes drop and it appears.

Step 4

Leakage, phase noise and the IF high-pass

The problem. The receiver is always on while the transmitter is always on. Some transmit power leaks straight into the receiver: through the space between the two antennas, through the PCB, through reflections from the radome and mounting. To the radar this leakage looks like an enormous target at almost zero range, typically 30–60 dB stronger than any real echo. It appears at a beat frequency near zero.

Why that is worse than it sounds: phase noise. The VCO's phase noise is on every signal it makes. A strong leakage tone carries a phase-noise skirt that spreads over neighbouring beat frequencies, which are other range cells, and can bury weak targets there, just as phase noise spread clutter over Doppler bins in R4 Step 7.

The rescue: range correlation. The mixer's LO is the same VCO, delayed by only a tiny time τL relative to the leakage. Phase noise that is the same on both inputs cancels in the mixer. Only the part that changed during τL survives. The surviving fraction is

surviving phase noise = L(f) + 10·log10(4 · sin²(π · f · τL))    ≈ L(f) + 20·log10(2π f τL) for small τL
Leakage path delay τLCancellation at 100 kHz offsetat 1 MHz offset
1 ns (short on-board path)−64 dB−44 dB
10 ns (radome reflection, cable)−44 dB−24 dB

So a short leakage path is mostly harmless; a long one (a reflection from the radome or vehicle bumper) keeps more of its phase noise. Keeping leakage paths short and isolation high is one of the main layout tasks in an FMCW radar.

The IF high-pass does the job STC did in R4. Echo power falls as R⁻⁴, and beat frequency rises in proportion to R. So, on the beat-frequency axis, echoes fall at 40 dB per decade of frequency. A high-pass filter rising at +40 dB per decade (a second-order high-pass, 12 dB per octave) below its corner frequency does two things at once:

  • it suppresses the leakage near zero frequency, so the LNA, IF amplifier and ADC are not filled by it, and
  • it equalises echoes: a target at half the range has 12 dB more echo, but its beat frequency is an octave lower, so the high-pass removes 12 dB. Inside the corner range every target of the same RCS arrives at the ADC at the same level. That is exactly R4's R⁴-law STC, done by a filter instead of a timer.

Try it: the IF spectrum after the mixer (B = 150 MHz, T = 100 µs, so 1 MHz = 100 m), on a log frequency axis. Red: the leakage and its surviving phase-noise skirt. Teal markers: 1 m² targets at 30 m and 800 m, and a small 0.01 m² drone at 100 m. Dashed: thermal noise per range bin. Things to check: (1) raise the leakage delay to 20 ns and worsen the phase noise to −70 dBc/Hz: the surviving skirt climbs up to the drone and buries it; (2) set the delay back to 1 ns: range correlation cancels the skirt again; (3) switch the high-pass off: the leakage is far above everything at the ADC input; switch it on and the 30 m target and the drone's 1 m² neighbours inside the corner range come out at the same level, while the leakage is pushed down by 100 dB or more.

Step 5

Ramp nonlinearity: why the ramp must be straight

The problem. Step 2 assumed the ramp is a perfectly straight line, so the echo and the transmitter stay parallel and the beat is a single steady tone. A real VCO's frequency is not exactly proportional to its tuning voltage, so an open-loop ramp bends. If the transmitter's frequency has an error e(t) along the ramp, the beat picks up e(t) − e(t − τ), which for a smooth error is about τ times the slope of the error. The beat is no longer one tone; it wanders, and its FFT peak spreads over several bins.

Why it hurts far targets most. The wandering is proportional to τ, the target's delay. A near target barely notices; a far target is smeared over many bins, losing peak height (SNR) and resolution.

peak spread ≈ 8 · δ · τ bins    (for a bowed ramp whose frequency error peaks at δ in the middle)
Worked: B = 150 MHz, T = 100 µs, bow δ = 1 % of B = 1.5 MHzτSpread
Target at 10 m67 ns0.8 bin: hardly visible
Target at 1 km6.7 µs80 bins: target destroyed
To keep 1 km within 1 binδ < 1 / (8τ) = 19 kHz, about 0.01 % of B

How radars get a straight ramp. (1) A PLL whose divider ratio is stepped along the ramp (fractional-N ramping): the loop forces the VCO to follow, so the linearity is set by the reference, not by the VCO; the loop bandwidth must be wide enough to follow the ramp. (2) A DDS generating the ramp digitally, then multiplied or mixed up to RF. (3) An open-loop VCO with a measured pre-distortion table, cheaper but drifting with temperature.

Try it: the FFT peak of one target (B = 150 MHz, T = 100 µs, Hann window), for a ramp that bows by δ (as % of B) in the middle, against an ideal straight ramp (grey). Things to check: (1) δ = 0: one clean peak; (2) δ = 0.2 % at 500 m: the peak spreads over several bins and drops; (3) keep δ, move the target to 20 m: almost perfect again: nonlinearity hurts far targets.

Step 6

Measuring speed: the chirp sequence

The problem. A moving target adds a Doppler shift fd = 2v/λ to the beat, so fb = 2SR/c + fd. One ramp cannot tell the two apart. For a slow ramp (T of milliseconds) the Doppler shift can be as large as the range term, and the target appears at the wrong range (range-Doppler coupling).

Old fix: up and down ramps (triangle). On the rising ramp the beat is fR + fd, on the falling ramp fR − fd. The sum gives range, the difference gives speed. It works for one target, but with several targets the radar must pair up the peaks correctly, which fails in a busy scene.

Modern fix: a fast chirp sequence, exactly like R4's pulses. Use short ramps (tens of µs), so fd is tiny compared with one range bin and range is read correctly from each ramp. Then send N ramps in a row. Between two ramps the target moves v·Tc, and the beat's phase in its range bin turns by 360° × 2v·Tc/λ: the same phase step as R4 Step 6, with the ramp period Tc in place of the PRI. A second FFT across the N ramps, in every range bin, gives a range-Doppler map, exactly as in R4 Step 7.

Step 7

Where your components show up in an FMCW radar

Where your component shows up

  • VCO / PLL ramp generator: ramp linearity sets how far targets stay sharp (Step 5); phase noise sets how much the leakage skirt covers near targets (Step 4); ramp reset and settling time eat into the duty cycle of a chirp sequence.
  • Antennas, layout and radome: TX-RX isolation sets the leakage level; the length of the leakage paths sets how much phase noise survives (Step 4).
  • LNA and mixer: must not compress on the leakage, which is present all the time; mixer DC offset and flicker (1/f) noise sit exactly where near targets' beats are.
  • IF high-pass and low-pass: the high-pass corner and slope do the job of STC; the low-pass is the anti-alias filter that sets maximum range (Steps 3–4).
  • ADC: needs only the beat bandwidth (MHz, not hundreds of MHz), but its dynamic range must cover what the high-pass leaves.
  • PA: gain flatness across B causes amplitude ripple along the ramp, which raises range sidelobes.

Glossary of terms introduced on this page

Chirp, ramp, slope S
A linear frequency sweep / its rate B/T in Hz per second.
Dechirp
Mixing the echo with the current transmit chirp to get a beat frequency.
Beat frequency
fb = 2SR/c (+ fd): the difference between transmit and echo frequency.
Range bin
One FFT bin of the beat signal; the FMCW range cell, c/2B wide.
Leakage
Transmit power reaching the receiver directly, appearing as a huge near-zero-range target.
Range correlation
Cancellation of phase noise in the mixer when LO and signal come from the same source with a short delay.
IF high-pass (as STC)
A high-pass on the beat signal that suppresses leakage and equalises near and far echoes.
Ramp nonlinearity
Departure of the frequency sweep from a straight line, which smears far targets.
Fractional-N ramping, DDS
Ways of generating a linear ramp: a PLL whose divider steps along the ramp / direct digital synthesis.
Triangle sweep
Alternating up and down ramps to separate range and Doppler for single targets.
Chirp sequence
Many short ramps in a row, processed with a second FFT for Doppler.
Range-Doppler coupling
Doppler shift appearing as a range error in a single ramp.
