LNA and Receiver Noise · RF Systems for Component Engineers

The LNA and receiver noise, from kTB to detection range

The low-noise amplifier is the first active part a received signal meets. Whatever noise it adds, and whatever loss sits in front of it, decides the weakest signal the whole receiver can ever see. This page builds that idea from the thermal noise of a resistor, through noise figure, cascades, noise matching, stability and linearity, to the two numbers a system engineer actually cares about: radar detection range and ESM sensitivity. Every term is defined before it is used, every step has worked numbers, and every datasheet line is traced from your design choice to the system effect.

Step 0

Why the first amplifier sets the sensitivity of the whole receiver

You already know

Every resistor, transistor and lossy line generates random noise. An amplifier multiplies whatever arrives at its input, signal and noise alike, by the same gain, and adds some noise of its own.

A receiver is a chain: antenna, a protection limiter, a filter, the LNA, more filtering, a mixer, IF amplifiers and finally an analogue-to-digital converter (ADC). The signal coming from the antenna may be a millionth of a millionth of a milliwatt. Each block in the chain adds noise. The key question is: which block's noise matters most?

The answer is the first block with gain. Once the signal and the noise have been amplified by, say, 100 times (20 dB), a later stage that adds the same amount of noise as the LNA adds only 1/100 as much in relative terms, because it is adding to a signal and noise that are already 100 times bigger. So the LNA's own noise, and any loss placed in front of it, are nearly the whole story. Everything after the LNA matters only to the extent that the LNA's gain is not large enough to hide it.

That is why a component engineer's LNA datasheet line "NF = 1.2 dB" ends up as a radar's detection range in kilometres or an ESM receiver's sensitivity in dBm. The rest of this page makes that connection exact, with numbers.

Terms used on this page

dB, dBm: dB is a power ratio, 10·log₁₀(P₁/P₂). dBm is power relative to 1 mW: 0 dBm = 1 mW, −30 dBm = 1 µW, −174 dBm = 4 × 10⁻²¹ W.

Bandwidth B: the range of frequencies the receiver lets through to the detector, in Hz. Noise power grows in proportion to B.

SNR (signal-to-noise ratio): signal power divided by noise power in the same bandwidth, usually in dB. Detection and measurement need a minimum SNR.

ESM (electronic support measures): a receiver that listens for other people's radar and radio emissions to detect, identify and locate them.

Step 1

Thermal noise kTB from first principles

You already know

A 50 Ω resistor on the bench, unconnected, shows a tiny random voltage on a sensitive enough instrument. That is thermal (Johnson–Nyquist) noise: the random motion of electrons, which grows with temperature.

Where kTB comes from. Picture a resistor R connected by a lossless matched line to a second resistor R, both at temperature T. In thermal equilibrium each resistor must deliver exactly as much power to the other as it receives, otherwise one would warm up and the other cool down, which thermodynamics forbids. Statistical physics says each independent "mode" of the line carries an average energy kT (k is Boltzmann's constant). A line of bandwidth B supports B modes per second in each direction. So the power one resistor delivers to the other is

Pn = k · T · B    vn,open = √(4·k·T·R·B)    vn,on matched load = √(k·T·B·R)k = 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K. Pn is the available noise power: the power delivered into a matched load. It does not depend on R. At T0 = 290 K (the standard "room temperature" reference): kT0 = 4.00 × 10⁻²¹ W/Hz = −174.0 dBm/Hz.

Definitions

Available power: the maximum power a source can deliver, which it does into a conjugate-matched load.

Noise power spectral density: noise power per hertz of bandwidth, in W/Hz or dBm/Hz. For thermal noise it is flat ("white") over all RF frequencies.

T0 = 290 K: the agreed reference temperature (17 °C) used in every noise figure definition. It is why "−174 dBm/Hz" appears everywhere.

Where your component shows up

  • Every resistive part at the input (bias resistors, stabilising resistors, lossy substrates) is a kTB noise source at its physical temperature. Your input network's loss turns directly into noise, as Step 2 shows.
  • Filters set B. A preselector or IF filter twice as wide as needed doubles the noise power: 3 dB of sensitivity lost before the LNA has done anything.

Check yourself

What is the thermal noise power in a 20 MHz bandwidth at 290 K?

−174 + 10·log(2 × 10⁷) = −174 + 73.0 = −101.0 dBm.

Does a 1 kΩ resistor deliver more noise power than a 50 Ω one?

No. Its open-circuit noise voltage is larger (√(4kTRB) grows with R), but the available power into a matched load is kTB for any R.

Step 2

Noise figure and noise temperature

You already know

On a noise-figure meter an LNA reads, for example, NF = 1.0 dB and gain = 20 dB. You know a lower NF is better. Here is exactly what the number means.

Connect a matched source at exactly 290 K to an amplifier of available gain G. The noise out is G·kT0B from the source, plus whatever the amplifier adds itself. A perfect noiseless amplifier would keep the SNR the same from input to output. A real one makes the output SNR worse. Noise factor F is that ratio, with the source held at 290 K:

F = SNRin / SNRout (source at 290 K)    NF = 10·log₁₀(F)    Te = T0·(F − 1)    F = 1 + Te/T0Te is the equivalent input noise temperature: the amplifier's own noise, expressed as the temperature a resistor at the input would need to produce it. A passive loss L (as a ratio ≥ 1) at physical temperature Tp has Te = (L − 1)·Tp; at 290 K that is F = L, so NF in dB equals the loss in dB.

Definitions

Noise factor F: a linear ratio ≥ 1. Noise figure NF: the same in dB. Datasheets say "noise figure" and mean NF in dB.

Equivalent noise temperature Te: the same information as F, in kelvin. Preferred when NF is below about 1 dB (where small dB differences are large in kelvin) and when the source is not at 290 K.

Antenna (source) temperature TA: the noise temperature the antenna presents to the receiver. Looking at cold sky it may be 20–50 K; looking at the ground it is near 290 K (see the antenna module, Step 8).

Step 3

The Friis cascade, including loss before the LNA

You already know

Gains in dB add through a chain. Noise does not add that simply: a stage's noise matters less the more gain there is in front of it.

Refer every stage's noise to the receiver input. Stage 2 adds Te2 at its own input; seen from the chain input it looks like Te2/G1, because a signal at the chain input has been multiplied by G1 by the time it gets there. This gives the Friis formula:

Te,total = Te1 + Te2/G1 + Te3/(G1G2) + …    Ftotal = F1 + (F2 − 1)/G1 + (F3 − 1)/(G1G2) + …All gains and noise factors are linear ratios, not dB. A loss L in front of everything: Ftotal = L · Frest, so its dB simply adds to the NF of the rest of the chain.

Definitions

Available gain: output available power divided by source available power. Friis uses available gains.

System noise figure: the NF of the whole chain from antenna port to ADC, the number that sets sensitivity.

Loss before the LNA: cable, connector, limiter, preselector filter, T/R switch, radome. All count dB for dB.

Three things to notice. (1) Without the 1 dB limiter and cable the total is 115.7 K = 1.46 dB: the 1 dB of loss cost exactly 1 dB of system NF. (2) The LNA's own 75 K becomes 94.5 K because the loss in front of it divides the signal before the LNA sees it. (3) If the LNA gain were only 10 dB, the mixer would add 261 K instead of 26 K and the system NF would rise to 4.25 dB. LNA gain is there to hide the mixer.

Each bar is one stage's noise referred to the antenna port, in kelvin. The total sets the system NF. Raise the loss: two bars grow at once (the loss itself and the LNA behind it). Lower the LNA gain with a direct-sampling ADC behind it: the ADC bar dominates and the LNA NF hardly matters.

Where your component shows up

  • Limiter and filter designers: your insertion loss is a noise figure. 0.3 dB saved in front of the LNA is as good as a 0.3 dB better LNA, and usually cheaper.
  • LNA designers: gain is not just "nice to have". It must be high enough that NF2/G1 is small, but not so high that the mixer overloads (Step 6).
  • Mixer and ADC designers: your NF matters through the LNA gain. A 25 dB NF ADC behind 30 dB of LNA gain adds only about 92 K.

Check yourself

A 0.5 dB cable is moved from before the LNA to after it (LNA 1 dB NF, 25 dB gain). What happens?

Before: NF ≈ 1.5 dB. After: the cable adds 35 K / 316 ≈ 0.1 K. System NF falls to about 1.0 dB. Moving the LNA to the antenna gains 0.5 dB.

Why do phased-array T/R modules put the LNA right behind the circulator and limiter?

Every dB of line between the element and the LNA is a dB of system NF, multiplied over thousands of elements. The module puts the LNA as close as possible to the element.

Step 4

Noise match (Γopt) vs power match, and input/output VSWR

You already know

A conjugate match (Γs = S11*) gives maximum gain and zero reflection. You may also know that the best-noise source impedance of a transistor is not the same point. Here is why, and what the trade costs.

A transistor has two internal noise sources: a noise voltage and a noise current, partly correlated. The source impedance it sees changes how these two add. For one particular source reflection coefficient, Γopt, they combine to give the lowest possible noise factor, Fmin. Move away from Γopt and F rises, quickly if the noise resistance Rn is large. Γopt is set by the device physics, S11* by its input impedance; they are generally different points on the Smith chart.

F = Fmin + (4·Rn/Z0) · |Γs − Γopt|² / [ (1 − |Γs|²) · |1 + Γopt|² ]Fmin, Γopt, Rn are the four noise parameters (Γopt counts as two: magnitude and angle), given on good device datasheets. Lines of constant F are circles on the Smith chart: noise circles.
Input mismatch |Γin,seen| = |S11 − Γs*| / |1 − S11Γs|    VSWR = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|)    RL = −20·log|Γ|This is the reflection seen at the 50 Ω input of a lossless matching network that presents Γs to the device (unilateral approximation, S12 ≈ 0). Zero when Γs = S11*.

Definitions

Γs: the reflection coefficient of the source as seen by the transistor gate, set by your input matching network.

Noise resistance Rn: how sensitive F is to leaving Γopt. Small Rn (large-gate-periphery devices) means a forgiving match.

VSWR, return loss: two ways of stating |Γ|. VSWR 2:1 = |Γ| 0.33 = RL 9.5 dB.

Balanced amplifier: two identical LNAs between two 90° hybrid couplers. Reflections from the two LNAs arrive at the input port 180° apart and cancel; they go to the hybrid's terminated port instead. You can noise-match each LNA and still get a good input VSWR.

Where your component shows up

  • Antenna mismatch moves Γs. The LNA was noise-matched to 50 Ω. A 2:1 antenna presents a different source, so the system NF is not the datasheet NF (the antenna module, Step 2, makes the same point).
  • Inductive source degeneration moves the real part of S11* toward Re(Γopt) with little NF cost. It is the standard trick to make noise and power match nearly coincide.

Check yourself

Why does a large Rn make a design harder?

F rises in proportion to Rn for a given distance from Γopt. Process variation and antenna mismatch then cost more NF; the noise circles are small.

A balanced LNA loses one half. What happens?

Gain drops by 6 dB (half the voltage reaches the output) but the receiver keeps working: a key reason radar front ends use balanced stages.

Step 5

Stability: K and μ

You already know

An amplifier with gain and some internal feedback (S12 ≠ 0) can oscillate if the source or load presents the wrong impedance. LNAs have high gain at low frequencies, where the matching networks no longer match anything.

With a source Γs connected, the reflection looking back into the output is Γout = S22 + S12S21Γs/(1 − S11Γs). If |Γout| > 1 the output port reflects more than it receives: it has negative resistance, and with a suitable load it oscillates. The same is true at the input with Γin and a load ΓL. An amplifier is unconditionally stable when no passive source or load (any |Γ| ≤ 1) can do this.

Δ = S11S22 − S12S21    K = (1 − |S11|² − |S22|² + |Δ|²) / (2·|S12S21|)
Unconditionally stable ⇔ K > 1 and |Δ| < 1    or, in one test: μ = (1 − |S11|²) / (|S22 − Δ·S11*| + |S12S21|) > 1K is the Rollett factor; μ (Edwards–Sinsky) is the distance from the Smith chart centre to the nearest unstable source point. Check them at every frequency where the device has gain, typically from 100 MHz to well above the band.

Definitions

Stability circle: the boundary on the Smith chart between Γ values that are safe and those that make the other port negative-resistance.

Potentially unstable: K < 1. Some passive terminations oscillate; others are safe. You can still design with it if you control the terminations at every frequency, which is risky.

Resistive stabilisation: a series resistor at the gate, or a shunt resistor (with a series capacitor so it acts only at low frequency) at the drain. It lowers gain and reflection where the device is unstable.

Where your component shows up

  • Filters and limiters in front of the LNA are reactive out of band: they present |Γ| ≈ 1 at some phase. That is exactly the termination that makes a potentially unstable LNA oscillate.

Check yourself

K = 1.3 but |Δ| = 1.2. Is the amplifier stable?

No. Both conditions are needed. μ > 1 checks it in one number.

Why is low-frequency stability a problem for an X-band LNA?

Device gain rises at low frequency (about 6 dB/octave) and the X-band matching networks are near-reactive there, so |Γ| ≈ 1 terminations are likely.

Step 6

Gain and flatness, P1dB, IIP3/OIP3 and blocking

You already know

An amplifier is linear only for small signals. Its output compresses for large signals, and two tones mix to make intermodulation products. You measure P1dB with a power sweep and OIP3 with a two-tone test.

Noise sets the weakest signal the receiver can see. Linearity sets the strongest signal it can handle while still seeing the weak one. The range between them is the dynamic range. The LNA sits where every signal in the preselector band arrives at once, including your own transmitter's leakage, nearby radars and jammers, so its linearity matters as much as its noise.

IIP3 = OIP3 − G    PIM3,out = 3·Pout − 2·OIP3    OP1dB ≈ OIP3 − 10 dB (rule of thumb)
SFDR = (2/3)·(IIP3 − Nin), Nin = −174 + NF + 10·log B    1/IIP3total = 1/IIP31 + G1/IIP32 + …All in dBm except the cascade, which uses linear milliwatts and gains. IM3 rises 3 dB for every 1 dB of input: the reason IP3 is an intercept (where the two lines would cross), not a power you can actually reach.

Definitions

P1dB: the power at which the gain has dropped 1 dB below its small-signal value. Quoted at the output (OP1dB) or input (IP1dB = OP1dB − G + 1).

Third-order intercept (IP3): the extrapolated point where the third-order intermodulation products (at 2f₁ − f₂ and 2f₂ − f₁) would equal the fundamentals.

SFDR (spurious-free dynamic range): the range of two-tone input power over which IM3 stays below the noise floor.

Blocker: a strong unwanted signal inside the preselector band but outside the channel you want. Desensitisation: loss of sensitivity to weak signals caused by a blocker.

Gain flatness: peak-to-peak gain variation across the band (dB p-p), and ripple (fast variation from mismatch standing waves).

Blocking: what a strong signal does to a weak one

A strong blocker drives the LNA toward compression. The small-signal gain for everything else drops, so the noise of the mixer and IF stages behind it, which the LNA gain was hiding, comes back. With a third-order model the gain for a weak signal falls by a factor of about 1/(1 + 2·PB/PIIP3) in amplitude. A preselector filter that rejects the blocker before the LNA is the main defence; it costs insertion loss, which is noise (Step 3).

Sensitivity loss for a weak wanted signal against blocker power at the antenna (LNA 20 dB gain, 1 dB NF; mixer and IF behind it 3 000 K; IIP3 = IP1dB + 9.6 dB). Raising the rejection shifts the curve to the right, dB for dB. Real LNAs also add noise when compressed and the blocker's phase noise mixes into the channel (synthesiser module), so real desensitisation is usually worse.

Where your component shows up

  • Bias current buys linearity. OIP3 rises with drain current; NF is usually best at lower current. LNA datasheets often show both curves against ID.

Check yourself

Two −40 dBm tones enter an LNA with IIP3 = 0 dBm. Where are the IM3 products, referred to the input?

3 × (−40) − 2 × 0 = −120 dBm: 80 dB below the tones.

Why not simply put 40 dB of gain in the LNA?

System NF hardly improves beyond about 25 dB (the mixer is already hidden), but system IIP3 gets 15 dB worse and the mixer overloads on strong signals.

Step 7

Recovery from overload

You already know

A transistor driven far into compression takes time to come back: charge is trapped, bias networks shift, and decoupling capacitors recharge. A radar receiver sees its own transmitter leak through the circulator every pulse.

When a radar transmits, some kilowatts pass the circulator, and a fraction (circulator isolation is only 20–25 dB) leaks toward the receiver. A limiter, usually PIN diodes shunting the line, turns on when the power is high and reflects most of it. What still passes is the flat leakage. After the pulse, the diodes take time to turn off (stored charge), and the LNA takes time to recover its gain. Until both have recovered, the receiver is partly deaf. Targets at ranges whose echoes return in that interval are missed: a blind range.

Blind range ≈ c · trecovery / 2    (c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s; 1 µs ≈ 150 m)trecovery: time from the end of the overload until the gain is back within 1 dB (or 3 dB, read the datasheet definition) of normal.

Definitions

Limiter threshold: the input power at which the limiter starts to limit (insertion loss rises by 1 dB).

Flat leakage: the output power of the limiter when it is fully limiting. It must be below the LNA's damage level.

Recovery time: how long after the overload the insertion loss (limiter) or gain (LNA) returns to within 1 dB of its normal value.

Spike leakage: the brief energy that passes during the first nanoseconds before the diodes turn on. A Schottky pre-limiter reduces it.

Check yourself

A radar has 500 ns total recovery. What is the minimum range?

3e8 × 500e-9 / 2 = 75 m, plus the pulse length itself (a 1 µs pulse adds another 150 m).

Why can a two-stage limiter make NF worse but the system better?

It costs more insertion loss (NF) but lowers flat leakage so the LNA overloads less deeply and recovers faster.

Step 8

Bias and temperature drift

You already know

Transistor gain falls as temperature rises (lower carrier mobility, lower transconductance). Threshold voltage shifts with temperature and from wafer to wafer, so a fixed gate voltage gives a different drain current on every part.

A receiver specified from −40 °C to +85 °C sees its LNA gain and NF change across that range. Gain change matters because the receiver's calibration (amplitude measurement, AGC thresholds, monopulse and DF channel matching) assumes a gain. NF change matters because sensitivity follows it directly. A device's noise temperature is roughly proportional to its physical temperature: hot parts are noisier, which is also why cryogenic LNAs exist.

ΔG ≈ (dG/dT) · ΔT · Nstages    Te(T) ≈ Te(25 °C) · (T + 273)/(298)    Δ sensitivity = ΔNFTypical GaAs pHEMT: −0.006 to −0.012 dB/°C per stage with a fixed gate voltage; about half that with constant-current (active) bias. Values on this page are illustrative.

Definitions

Self-bias / fixed bias: gate voltage set by a resistor divider or source resistor. Simple, but drain current, and so gain, drift with temperature and from part to part.

Active bias: a control loop that measures drain current and adjusts the gate voltage to hold it constant.

Temperature-compensating attenuator: a thick-film attenuator whose loss falls with temperature, cancelling the gain drop of the amplifier.

Bias sequencing: depletion-mode GaAs and GaN devices need the gate negative before drain voltage is applied, or they draw destructive current.

  • Phased arrays need all channels to track each other in gain and phase over temperature. Active bias reduces the spread more than it reduces the average drift.

Check yourself

A 3-stage LNA with fixed bias drifts −0.01 dB/°C per stage. Gain change from −40 to +85 °C?

−0.01 × 125 × 3 = −3.75 dB across the range.

Does a temperature-compensating attenuator after stage 1 change the NF drift?

Barely. NF is set by stage 1, before the attenuator.

Step 9

Sensitivity: −174 dBm/Hz + NF + 10·log B + SNRmin

You already know

Every piece is in place: kT0 = −174 dBm/Hz (Step 1), NF from the cascade (Steps 2–3), bandwidth B from your filters, and a minimum SNR that detection or measurement needs.

The receiver's noise referred to its input is kT0·B·F. A signal can be detected when it is SNRmin above that. In dB, the multiplications become additions, which is why receiver engineers can do sensitivity in their heads.

Smin (dBm) = −174 + NFsys + 10·log₁₀(B) + SNRminAssumes the source (antenna) is at 290 K. For another antenna temperature use −174 + 10·log(Tsys/290) + 10·log B + SNRmin, with Tsys = TA + Te,sys. With SNRmin = 0 dB the result is called the minimum detectable signal (MDS) or noise floor.

Definitions

SNRmin: the signal-to-noise ratio needed at the detector for the job. A radar needs about 13 dB for 90 % detection probability with a false alarm once in a million looks (R3 covers where this comes from). An ESM receiver measuring pulse parameters typically needs 12–18 dB.

Sensitivity: the weakest input signal that achieves SNRmin. More negative dBm is better.

Worked examples

The sensitivity budget as a waterfall: start at −174 dBm/Hz, add NF, add 10·log B, add the required SNR. The NF bar is the only one the LNA designer controls; the bandwidth bar is usually much larger, which is why narrow filtering and long integration matter so much at system level.

Where your component shows up

  • LNA and everything in front of it: the NF bar.
  • IF and channel filters, digital filters: the B bar. A filter with a noise bandwidth 1.2 times its 3 dB bandwidth costs 0.8 dB.
  • Signal processing: integration over many pulses lowers the SNRmin needed per pulse; that is outside the LNA but often worth more dB than the LNA itself.

Check yourself

An ESM receiver: 2–18 GHz, NF 10 dB, 2 GHz-wide channel, 15 dB SNR. Sensitivity?

−174 + 10 + 93 + 15 = −56 dBm. Wide-open receivers are insensitive; channelising to 20 MHz would gain 20 dB.

Which gives more sensitivity: halving B, or cutting NF from 3 dB to 1 dB?

Halving B gives 3 dB; the NF change gives 2 dB. Halving B wins, if the signal still fits in it.

Step 10

From noise to radar range, G/T and ESM sensitivity

You already know

The radar equation (R1) gives the received echo power falling as 1/R⁴. An ESM receiver hears the radar one way, so its received power falls only as 1/R². The antenna module (Step 8) defines G/T and Tsys.

A radar detects a target at range R when the echo power equals the sensitivity. Echo power ∝ 1/R⁴, sensitivity ∝ Tsys (or F, with a 290 K antenna). So the maximum range is proportional to the fourth root of 1/Tsys. An ESM receiver's signal falls as 1/R², so its detection range is proportional to the square root of 1/Tsys: ESM gains twice as much range per dB of NF as radar.

Radar: Rmax ∝ (1/(k·Tsys·B·SNRmin))1/4 → with TA = 290 K: R ∝ F−1/4    ESM: Rmax ∝ Tsys−1/2
Tsys = TA + (L − 1)·290 + L·Te,rx    G/T (dB/K) = G (dBi) − 10·log₁₀(Tsys)L is the loss ratio in front of the LNA, Te,rx the receiver (LNA plus following stages) noise temperature. The same NF improvement is worth more when TA is small.

Definitions

System noise temperature Tsys: all the noise, antenna included, referred to the antenna port.

G/T: the receive figure of merit: received SNR is proportional to G/T for a given incoming power density.

Equivalent transmit power: the transmitter power increase that would give the same range gain as an NF improvement. In the radar equation, power and 1/Tsys are interchangeable dB for dB.

  • ESM (Part 4): NF enters as F−1/2, twice as strongly, and the ESM receiver must also survive strong emitters (Steps 6–7). Expect a fight between NF, IP3 and limiter loss.
  • Satcom and space-looking radar: TA is small, so G/T is dominated by the LNA and the loss in front of it.

Check yourself

A radar's range is 100 km with a 4 dB NF receiver. A 2 dB NF LNA is fitted. New range?

100 × (10^(0.4)/10^(0.2))^(1/4) = 100 × 10^(0.05) = 112 km.

Why does an ESM receiver gain twice as much range per dB as a radar?

The ESM signal travels one way (1/R²), the radar echo two ways (1/R⁴). Range is the square root vs the fourth root of the sensitivity.

Glossary of terms introduced on this page

Active bias
A control loop that holds the transistor's drain current constant by adjusting its gate voltage.
Antenna temperature TA
The noise temperature an antenna presents to the receiver, set by what it looks at (sky, ground) and its own loss.
Available gain
Output available power divided by source available power; the gain used in Friis' formula.
Available power
The maximum power a source can deliver, into a conjugate-matched load.
Balanced amplifier
Two identical amplifiers between 90° hybrids; reflections cancel at the input, giving good VSWR with a noise match.
Blind range
Ranges at which a radar cannot detect because its receiver is still recovering from the transmit pulse: c·t/2.
Blocker
A strong unwanted signal inside the receiver's front-end band but outside the wanted channel.
Boltzmann's constant k
1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K; links temperature to noise energy per hertz.
Desensitisation
Loss of sensitivity to weak signals caused by a strong blocker compressing the front end.
ESM
Electronic support measures: receivers that detect, identify and locate other emitters.
Flat leakage
The power a limiter passes while fully limiting.
Fmin
The lowest noise factor a device can achieve, reached when the source is Γopt.
Friis formula
F = F₁ + (F₂ − 1)/G₁ + …: how the noise of each stage in a chain adds, referred to the input.
G/T
Antenna gain divided by system noise temperature, in dB/K; the receive figure of merit.
Gain flatness
Peak-to-peak variation of gain across the band.
Γopt
The source reflection coefficient that gives minimum noise figure.
Γs, ΓL
Source and load reflection coefficients presented to the transistor by the matching networks.
IIP3 / OIP3
Input- and output-referred third-order intercept points; OIP3 = IIP3 + G.
IM3
Third-order intermodulation products at 2f₁ − f₂ and 2f₂ − f₁, rising 3 dB per dB of input.
K (Rollett factor)
Stability factor; with |Δ| < 1, K > 1 means unconditionally stable.
kTB
Thermal noise power available from a matched source at temperature T in bandwidth B; −174 dBm/Hz at 290 K.
Limiter
A diode circuit before the LNA that passes small signals and reflects large ones to protect the LNA.
MDS
Minimum detectable signal: the noise floor, −174 + NF + 10·log B dBm.
μ (Edwards–Sinsky)
Single-number stability test; μ > 1 means unconditionally stable.
Noise circle
A circle on the Smith chart of source impedances giving the same noise figure.
Noise factor F / noise figure NF
SNRin/SNRout with a 290 K source, linear (F) or in dB (NF).
Noise resistance Rn
How quickly the noise factor rises as Γs moves away from Γopt.
Noise temperature Te
A component's added noise expressed as the input resistor temperature that would produce it: Te = 290(F − 1).
P1dB
The power at which gain has fallen 1 dB below its small-signal value.
Paired echo
A false copy of a compressed radar pulse caused by gain or phase ripple across the band.
Preselector
The band-pass filter before (or just after) the LNA that rejects out-of-band signals.
Recovery time
Time after an overload until gain (or limiter loss) is back within 1 dB of normal.
Return loss
−20·log|Γ|; how far below the incident wave the reflected wave is.
Sensitivity
The weakest signal that achieves the required SNR: −174 + NF + 10·log B + SNRmin dBm.
SFDR
Spurious-free dynamic range: input range over which IM3 stays below the noise floor.
SNRmin
The signal-to-noise ratio needed at the detector for detection or measurement.
Stability circle
The Smith-chart boundary between terminations that are safe and those that cause oscillation.
System noise temperature Tsys
Antenna temperature plus all receiver noise, referred to the antenna port.
T0
290 K, the reference temperature for noise figure.
Transducer gain
Power delivered to the load divided by the power available from the source; includes mismatch loss.
VSWR
Voltage standing-wave ratio, (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|).
