Power amplifiers, from the transistor to the system

The power amplifier (PA) is the last active stage before the antenna. Every watt the system radiates, every watt of heat it must remove and most of the spectral junk it sprays into other people's bands start here. This page opens the PA from first principles: what compression, efficiency, AM-PM and back-off really mean, how pulses, heat and a bad antenna match load the device, how GaN, GaAs and TWTs differ, and how each datasheet number ends up as EIRP, radar range, Doppler performance, spectral purity and jammer effectiveness. Every term is explained before it is used. Every step has worked numbers and, where it helps, a demo you can change.

Step 1

What a PA does, and where its watts go

You already know

A transistor is a controlled current source: a small voltage on the gate (or base) sets a large current in the drain (or collector). Put a load resistor in the drain and the changing current becomes a changing voltage, so a small input signal becomes a large output signal. A small-signal amplifier (an LNA, a gain block) does this with tiny swings, far from any limit. A power amplifier does the same thing with the largest swing the device can survive: the drain voltage swings from almost zero up to nearly twice the supply, and the drain current from almost zero to the device's maximum. All of the interesting PA behaviour comes from working right at those limits.

Terms used in this step

dBm: power relative to 1 mW, P(dBm) = 10·log₁₀(P / 1 mW). 1 W = 30 dBm, 100 W = 50 dBm. dBW: power relative to 1 W, so dBW = dBm − 30. Gain: Pout / Pin, in dB the difference Pout(dBm) − Pin(dBm).

EIRP (effective isotropic radiated power): the power an ideal isotropic antenna would need to radiate to produce the same field strength on boresight. EIRP = P at the antenna port × antenna gain; in dB, EIRP(dBW) = P(dBW) − losses(dB) + G(dBi).

Drain supply VDD: the DC voltage feeding the output transistor (28 V or 50 V for GaN, 5–10 V for GaAs). DC power PDC: VDD × average drain current. Every watt of PDC that does not leave as RF becomes heat in the device.

The PA is a power converter. It takes DC power from the supply and turns part of it into RF power at the carrier frequency, under the control of a small RF input. Three things leave the device: the wanted RF output, heat, and unwanted RF (harmonics and distortion products). The datasheet tells you how the split falls, and the rest of this page shows what each part costs the system.

Where it sits. In a transmitter, a low-level waveform from the exciter (a DAC, a DDS or a synthesiser with a modulator) is raised by one or more driver stages to a few watts, and the PA raises it to the final level. After the PA come passive parts that only lose power: an isolator to protect the PA from reflections (step 9), a harmonic filter (step 6), the duplexer or circulator that shares the antenna with the receiver, and the cable or waveguide. Then the antenna adds gain in the wanted direction.

Worked example: one PA, one EIRP

The PA delivers 100 W = 50 dBm at its output flange. The isolator, filter, circulator and cable lose 0.3 + 0.4 + 0.4 + 0.4 = 1.5 dB, so 48.5 dBm (70.8 W) reaches the antenna. With a 33 dBi antenna, EIRP = 48.5 + 33 = 81.5 dBm = 51.5 dBW, about 141 kW "isotropic". Two lessons follow at once:

  • Every dB after the PA costs 21 % of the PA's power. To win back 1 dB of post-PA loss you need a PA with 26 % more output, which also needs 26 % more DC and makes 26 % more heat.
  • The PA power enters the system weakly or strongly, depending on the system. A radar's detection range grows only as the fourth root of power (R ∝ Pt¼, step 12): doubling the PA buys 19 % more range. A jammer's jamming-to-signal ratio grows in direct proportion to its power: doubling the PA buys 3 dB of J/S.

Note also the DC figure: the PA draws 182 W to make 100 W of RF. The other 82 W (plus the 6 W of drive) is heat in a die a few millimetres across. Steps 3 and 8 are about where that heat goes.

Where your component shows up

  • Isolator, filter, circulator, cable designers: your insertion loss is subtracted directly from EIRP, and it is paid for in PA watts, DC power and heat. 0.1 dB at 100 W is 2.3 W of PA output.
  • Power-supply designers: the PA's DC current is large (3.6 A here, tens of amps in a pulsed radar), and every ripple or droop on VDD becomes amplitude and phase modulation of the RF (steps 4 and 7).

Check yourself

A 200 W PA feeds a 35 dBi antenna through 2 dB of loss. What is the EIRP in dBW?

200 W = 23.0 dBW. 23.0 − 2 + 35 = 56.0 dBW (about 400 kW).

Which is cheaper: 0.5 dB less loss after the PA, or a PA with 0.5 dB more output?

They give the same EIRP. The loss reduction costs no DC power and makes no extra heat; the bigger PA needs 12 % more DC and gets 12 % hotter. Low-loss passives after the PA are almost always the cheaper watts.

Step 2

Psat, P1dB and gain compression

You already know

An amplifier's output cannot exceed what its supply and its device allow. In a transistor the drain voltage cannot swing below the knee voltage Vk (where the device leaves saturation and becomes a resistor) or above breakdown, and the current cannot exceed Imax. As the input grows, the output first follows it in proportion, then follows less and less, then stops growing at all. You have seen this on every gain block you have measured with a power sweep.

Terms used in this step

Small-signal gain G: Pout/Pin when the drive is so low that the output is far from any limit. Gain compression: the drop of the actual gain below G as the drive rises, in dB.

P1dB: the output power at which the gain has fallen by 1 dB from G (some datasheets give the input power at that point instead; read the axis label). P3dB: the same at 3 dB compression. Psat: saturated output power, the level the output approaches when the input is pushed hard. Datasheets often quote Psat at a stated drive, or at a stated compression such as P3dB.

Back-off: how far below a reference level the PA is operated. Output back-off (OBO) = Psat − Pout in dB. Input back-off (IBO) is the same measured at the input.

Rapp model: a simple description of the compression curve, Pout = G·Pin / (1 + (G·Pin/Psat)p)1/p. The single number p is the "smoothness" of the knee: p ≈ 1 is a soft, gradual compression (class A GaAs at low drive), p ≈ 2–3 is typical of GaN class AB, and p ≥ 5 is a hard limiter.

Why the curve bends. At low drive the RF drain current and voltage swing are small, so the device is a linear current source. As drive rises, the voltage swing reaches the knee on one side; the current waveform starts to clip; the fundamental component of the current stops growing in proportion. The output power approaches Psat ≈ (VDD − Vk)² / (2·RL), where RL is the load resistance presented to the transistor. This is why designers choose the load (by load-pull, step 9): RL sets Psat.

How sharp is the bend? The difference Psat − P1dB tells you. With the Rapp model you can compute it exactly: at 1 dB compression, (1 + xp)1/p = 1.259, where x = G·Pin/Psat.

x₁ = (1.259p − 1)1/p, P1dB = Psat · x₁ / 1.259 p = 1: P1dB = Psat − 6.9 dB (very soft). p = 2: Psat − 2.2 dB. p = 3: Psat − 1.0 dB. p = 6: Psat − 0.2 dB (hard limiter). A GaN datasheet with P1dB = 44.8 dBm and Psat = 47 dBm is a p ≈ 2 device.

Worked example

A PA has G = 12 dB, Psat = 47 dBm (50 W) and p = 2. Then P1dB = 47 − 2.16 = 44.8 dBm (30.4 W). The input at that point is 44.8 + 1 − 12 = 33.8 dBm. P3dB is at 46.4 dBm (43.2 W), only 0.6 dB below Psat, reached with 35.4 dBm in. Pushing the input another 3 dB above the P3dB drive raises the output by only 0.4 dB: the extra drive power becomes gate current and heat, and the device is stressed for almost nothing.

Where your component shows up

  • Exciter and attenuators: the drive level sets where the PA works on this curve. A 1 dB drift in drive at saturation changes the output by only 0.1–0.2 dB, but changes the phase (step 4) and the heat.
  • Test engineers: P1dB and Psat depend on pulse width and temperature (a hot device has lower Psat). Ask for the conditions behind the number.

Check yourself

Datasheet: Psat 50 dBm, P1dB 49 dBm. Is this PA soft or hard?

Psat − P1dB = 1 dB, which corresponds to p ≈ 3. It is a fairly hard knee: good for efficiency close to saturation, but distortion rises abruptly once the envelope reaches the knee.

Why is "Psat" alone not enough to compare two PAs?

Because the drive and conditions at which it is quoted differ. One vendor quotes Psat at 3 dB compression, another at a fixed high drive where the device may be at 5 dB compression. Always compare P1dB, P3dB and the drive used.

Step 3

Drain efficiency, PAE and the heat they leave behind

You already know

Energy is conserved. Whatever DC and RF power goes into a device and does not come out as RF output becomes heat. A linear voltage regulator dropping 5 V at 2 A makes 10 W of heat, and you size its heat sink from that. A PA is no different: its "efficiency" is just the fraction of input power that leaves as wanted RF.

Terms used in this step

Drain efficiency ηD = Pout / PDC. Power-added efficiency PAE = (Pout − Pin) / PDC: it charges the PA for the drive power it needs. PAE ≈ ηD·(1 − 1/G). At high gain they are almost equal; at low gain (wideband or high-frequency PAs) PAE is much lower.

Dissipated power Pdiss = PDC + Pin − Pout: the heat in the device. Thermal resistance Rth (°C/W): temperature rise per watt of heat from the junction (the hot channel of the transistor) to a reference surface such as the flange or base plate. Junction temperature Tj = Tbase + Pdiss·Rth.

Amplifier class: the bias point and conduction angle. Class A: the device conducts all of the cycle, the DC current is constant, ηmax = 50 %. Class B: biased at pinch-off, conducts half the cycle, the DC current rises with the signal, ηmax = π/4 = 78.5 %. Class AB: in between, used by most real PAs. Doherty: two amplifiers whose load is actively modulated so efficiency stays high over 6 dB of back-off.

Why class matters at back-off. At full swing, class B wastes less because its DC current follows the signal. When you back off the output (to keep a signal linear, step 5), efficiency falls, and how fast depends on class. Let x = √(Pout/Pmax) be the relative voltage swing. For ideal devices:

Class A: η = 0.5·x² Class B: η = (π/4)·x Doherty: η = (π/2)·x (x ≤ ½), (π/2)·x² / (3x − 1) (x > ½) 6 dB back-off is x = 0.5: class A falls to 12.5 %, class B to 39 %, an ideal Doherty stays at 78.5 %. Real devices lose a further 10–20 points to the knee voltage, the output match loss and the bias current. The "typical GaN class AB" curve in the demo is an illustrative fit, η ≈ 68 %·x1.2.

Worked example: efficiency is temperature

A GaN PA delivers 50 W with G = 12 dB, so it needs Pin = 3.15 W. At ηD = 55 %: PDC = 50/0.55 = 90.9 W, PAE = (50 − 3.15)/90.9 = 51.5 %, and Pdiss = 90.9 + 3.15 − 50 = 44.1 W. With Rth = 1.2 °C/W on a 70 °C base plate, Tj = 70 + 44.1 × 1.2 = 123 °C.

Now run the same 50 W at ηD = 40 % (a worse match, a different band edge, or 3 dB of back-off): PDC = 125 W, Pdiss = 78 W, Tj = 164 °C. Fifteen points of efficiency raised the junction by 41 °C. For a typical semiconductor wear-out activation energy (about 1.7 eV), every 10 °C at these temperatures cuts the life by a factor of 2 to 3 (step 8). That is why a PA's efficiency is a reliability spec, not only a power-bill spec.

Where your component shows up

  • Output matching network: 0.3 dB of loss in the match at 50 W is 3.4 W of heat right next to the die, and it lowers ηD by about 4 points.
  • Bias and sequencing circuits: the quiescent current sets where class AB sits between A and B. Drift of the gate voltage with temperature moves the quiescent current and with it efficiency, gain and AM-PM.
  • Mechanical and thermal engineers: Rth of the die is fixed by the vendor; the rest of the thermal path (flange, TIM, cold plate) is yours, and it adds directly to Tj.

Check yourself

A 10 W PA with 8 dB gain has ηD = 50 %. What is its PAE and its heat?

Pin = 10/6.31 = 1.58 W. PDC = 20 W. PAE = (10 − 1.58)/20 = 42 %. Heat = 20 + 1.58 − 10 = 11.6 W.

Why does an ideal class-A PA get hotter when you turn the RF drive off?

Its DC current is constant. With RF on, part of PDC leaves as RF; with RF off, all of PDC is heat. Class AB and B draw less current at low drive, so they cool down instead.

Step 4

AM-AM and AM-PM: amplitude and phase depend on the drive

You already know

A transistor's capacitances (Cgs, Cgd, Cds) are not fixed; they change with the voltages across them. In a varactor you use this deliberately. In a PA, the large RF swing moves these capacitances through a whole cycle, so their average value, and with it the phase shift through the amplifier, depends on how hard it is driven.

Terms used in this step

AM-AM: the change of output amplitude with input amplitude, i.e. the compression curve of step 2 seen as a distortion. Often given as gain compression (dB) or as a slope in dB/dB (1 when linear, 0 at hard saturation).

AM-PM: the change of insertion phase with input amplitude, in degrees per dB of input change (°/dB), or as the total phase change from small signal to a stated compression. Typical solid-state PAs show 2–10° of total phase change up to 3 dB compression, concentrated near the knee.

Envelope: the slowly varying amplitude of an RF signal, |x(t)|. A constant-envelope signal (CW, a pulse's flat top, a chirp) has one envelope value; two tones, multi-tone and noise have a varying envelope. Phase pushing: change of phase with supply voltage, in °/V. Stability (dBc): the variation, expressed as a power ratio to the carrier, e.g. a phase error of σ radians gives 20·log₁₀(σ) dBc.

Why AM-PM matters even for "constant-envelope" radar. A chirp or a pulse looks like a constant envelope, so you might think AM-PM is irrelevant. But the drive level is never perfectly constant: the exciter level drifts with temperature, the driver droops along the pulse (step 7), the supply ripples. Each of these is a small amplitude change at the PA input, and AM-PM turns it into a phase change at the output. Radar processing (Doppler filters, MTI, pulse compression, SAR) measures phase. A phase wobble looks exactly like a moving target or a smeared image.

Model used here. AM-AM is the Rapp curve of step 2. AM-PM is a Saleh-type curve: φ = 2φ₁·x² / (1 + x²), with x the drive relative to the saturation input and φ₁ the phase change at x = 1. Phase changes little at low drive, fastest around the knee, and levels off in saturation.

Δφ_out ≈ (dφ/dPin) · ΔPin phase stability = 20·log₁₀(Δφrms in rad) dBc Example: AM-PM slope 4 °/dB near P1dB. A 0.05 dB rms pulse-to-pulse variation of drive gives 0.2° rms of phase: 20·log₁₀(0.2·π/180) = −49 dBc. Step 7 shows that a −49 dBc stability limits clutter cancellation to about 49 dB.

Worked example

A GaN PA with p = 2 shows 8° of phase change at the saturation input. Feed it a drive that varies by 0.05 dB rms (demo defaults). At P1dB the phase has moved 5.9° from small signal, the AM-PM slope is 1.7 °/dB and the AM-AM slope 0.63 dB/dB: the output varies by 0.032 dB and 0.086° rms, a stability of −48 dBc. 6 dB harder the phase curve has levelled off (0.65 °/dB) and the output barely follows the drive (0.10 dB/dB): −62 dBc. 10 dB below P1dB the phase is quiet (0.04 °/dB) but the amplitude follows the drive one-for-one: −45 dBc. A saturated PA cleans up drive amplitude noise; a backed-off one passes it straight through. The best point depends on where the noise comes from, which is why both AM-AM and AM-PM curves belong on the datasheet.

Why can a deeply saturated PA still have poor phase stability?

AM-AM is flat in saturation, so amplitude is stable, but phase can still move with drive and strongly with supply voltage (phase pushing) and temperature. Saturation stabilises amplitude, not phase.

Step 5

PAPR and back-off: chirps, pulses and multi-tone jamming

You already know

Add two sine waves of slightly different frequency and you get beats: the sum swings between twice the amplitude (when they are in phase) and zero (when they are out of phase). Add many sine waves with random phases and the sum looks like noise, with occasional large peaks. The PA has to pass the peaks; the system is paid by the average.

Terms used in this step

PAPR (peak-to-average power ratio): peak envelope power divided by average power, in dB. CW, a pulse's flat top and a linear FM chirp have PAPR = 0 dB. Two equal tones: 3.0 dB. N equal tones all in phase: 10·log₁₀(N) dB. Gaussian noise has no strict peak; its PAPR is quoted at a probability, about 9.6 dB at 0.01 % of the time. (The demo's short 1024-sample noise record peaks at about 7.7 dB; a longer record would reach the rarer, higher peaks.)

PEP (peak envelope power): the power of the envelope at its highest point. A PA must handle the PEP without excessive compression. CCDF (complementary cumulative distribution function): the fraction of time the envelope power exceeds a given level above the average. Signal-to-distortion ratio (SDR): power of the wanted (linearly scaled) signal at the output divided by the power of everything else the PA added.

Multi-tone jamming: one jammer transmitting several CW tones at once, for example one on each channel of a frequency-hopping radio or a spot on each of several radars. Noise jamming: transmitting band-limited noise to raise the noise floor of a victim receiver.

The rule. To pass a signal with little distortion, its peaks must stay below the PA's compression. So its average must sit PAPR dB below that, and the average output power is lower than the PA's rating by roughly the PAPR. Constant-envelope radar waveforms (pulses, chirps, phase codes) are exactly why most radar PAs can be run in saturation at full efficiency. Jammers and multi-channel transmitters are not so lucky.

What happens if you do not back off. The peaks are clipped. Clipping does three bad things: (1) it removes energy from the peaks and turns it into distortion products, which land in the gaps between the tones and in the adjacent channels (step 6); (2) the wanted signal's power falls compared with a linear PA; (3) for multi-tone, it creates intermodulation tones that waste power on frequencies nobody wanted. You can see all three in the demo through the signal-to-distortion readout.

Pavg,max ≈ Pcompression − PAPR per-tone power (N equal tones) = Pavg − 10·log₁₀(N) Two tones in a 100 W PEP-rated amplifier: average 50 W, 25 W per tone. Eight tones all in phase: PEP is 8 × average, so average 12.5 W and 1.6 W per tone, each tone 18 dB below a single 100 W CW. With random or optimised (Newman) phases the PAPR of eight tones is only about 2.5–5 dB, so phasing the tones buys back 4–6 dB.

Worked example: one PA, three waveforms

A 100 W (Psat) GaN PA with p = 2. Radar chirp: PAPR 0 dB, run it at Psat: 100 W average, efficiency at its best, almost no distortion from AM-AM (phase still matters, step 4). Two-tone test or two channels: 3 dB PAPR; at 6 dB average back-off (about 23 W) the peaks sit 3 dB below Psat and the SDR is about 35 dB. Eight tones in phase: at the same back-off the 9 dB peaks are crushed and SDR falls to 17 dB. Noise jamming: at 6 dB back-off the noise peaks still reach saturation and SDR is about 24 dB; for 30 dB SDR it must back off about 8.5 dB, to 14 W average, where an ideal class-B PA runs at 34 % efficiency instead of 66 % on the saturated chirp. The demo gives exact numbers for any case.

Where your component shows up

  • DAC and exciter designers: the tone phases you choose set the PAPR. Newman or Schroeder phases on a multi-tone jammer are worth several dB of jammer power for free.
  • PA designers: a PA rated "100 W" is a 100 W CW or pulsed PA. On a 10 dB PAPR signal it is a 10 W PA. Quote the signal it was rated for.
  • Power-supply designers: a backed-off class AB PA on a noise waveform draws a fluctuating current; the supply's transient response appears as extra distortion.

Check yourself

Sixteen equal tones, all in phase, through a 200 W PA. How much power per tone if the PEP must stay at 200 W?

PAPR = 10·log₁₀(16) = 12 dB. Average 200/16 = 12.5 W, per tone 12.5/16 = 0.78 W, i.e. 24 dB below a single 200 W tone.

Why can a chirp radar PA run in saturation when a noise jammer PA cannot?

The chirp's envelope is constant, so saturation does not distort its shape and the phase information in the chirp survives (apart from AM-PM on any residual drive variation). Noise has peaks well above its average; saturation clips them and turns them into distortion spread over the band and beyond.

Step 6

Harmonics and spectral regrowth: the PA's unwanted output

You already know

Any nonlinear transfer curve, written as a power series y = a₁x + a₂x² + a₃x³ + …, turns a sine at f₀ into sines at 2f₀, 3f₀ and so on (harmonics), and turns two sines at f₁ and f₂ into products at 2f₁ − f₂, 2f₂ − f₁ and other combinations (intermodulation). The x² term makes even-order products, the x³ term odd-order ones. A PA run near its limits is a strongly nonlinear device, so it makes both.

Terms used in this step

dBc: dB relative to the carrier (the wanted output). −60 dBc is one millionth of the carrier power. Harmonic: an output at an integer multiple n·f₀ of the carrier. IM3: third-order intermodulation products at 2f₁ − f₂ and 2f₂ − f₁, right next to the two tones and impossible to filter. IM5: fifth-order products, one tone spacing further out.

OIP3 (output third-order intercept point): the fictitious output power per tone at which the extrapolated IM3 line (slope 3) would meet the extrapolated fundamental line (slope 1). Below compression, IM3 (dBc) = −2·(OIP3 − Ptone). Spectral regrowth: the skirts of distortion that grow on both sides of a modulated signal after a nonlinear PA. ACPR (adjacent channel power ratio): power falling in the neighbouring channel, relative to the power in the wanted channel, in dBc.

Harmonics. A PA that clips symmetrically (both half-cycles flattened equally) makes mostly odd harmonics; any asymmetry (class AB bias, knee on one side and pinch-off on the other) adds even ones. Unfiltered, broadband GaN PAs show second harmonics around −15 to −25 dBc and third harmonics around −20 to −30 dBc. Narrowband PAs with harmonic terminations (class F, class J) reflect the harmonics back into the device on purpose to shape the waveforms, which improves efficiency and reduces harmonic output, but a harmonic filter is still needed to reach the −60 dBc that spectrum rules and co-site receivers usually ask for.

Spectral regrowth. Harmonics are far away and can be filtered. Intermodulation of a modulated signal lands right next to it: a two-tone test grows IM3 at one tone spacing outside each tone; a wideband signal grows skirts that spill into the adjacent channel. No filter can remove them without removing the signal. Only back-off, a better knee, or linearisation (predistortion) reduces them. AM-PM makes regrowth worse, because a phase that depends on the envelope is another form of nonlinearity.

IM3 (dBc) = −2·(OIP3 − Ptone) each 1 dB of back-off improves IM3 by 2 dB (below compression) Two tones at 40 dBm each, OIP3 = 55 dBm: IM3 = −2 × 15 = −30 dBc (10 dBm). Back off 3 dB to 37 dBm per tone: −36 dBc. Near and above P1dB the rule fails: IM3 stops improving with drive and IM5, IM7 rise. The demo uses the full Rapp curve, so you can see where the 2:1 rule holds and where it breaks.

Worked example: harmonics through a filter

The filter must also absorb or reflect up to 4 W of harmonic power; if it reflects it, the PA sees a harmonic load it was not designed for, which changes its efficiency and waveform (step 9).

Step 7

Pulsed operation: droop, rise time and pulse-to-pulse stability

You already know

A capacitor supplying a load current I for a time Δt loses voltage ΔV = I·Δt / C. A pulsed radar PA draws a large current only during the pulse, so its supply is usually a capacitor bank recharged between pulses. During each pulse that bank sags, and the PA's gain, power and phase all follow the supply. On top of this, the device heats up during the pulse (it cools between pulses), so its gain and phase change along the pulse too.

Terms used in this step

Pulse width τ, PRI (pulse repetition interval) and PRF = 1/PRI. Duty cycle = τ/PRI. Rise time / fall time: time for the RF envelope to go from 10 % to 90 % of its final amplitude (or back). Droop: the fall of output power from the start to the end of the pulse, in dB. Pulse-to-pulse stability: the variation of amplitude and phase between successive pulses, measured at the same point in each pulse, as an rms value or in dBc.

Clutter: echoes from ground, sea or rain, much stronger than the target and nearly stationary (zero Doppler). MTI / Doppler processing: comparing successive pulses (or taking an FFT across them, a CPI of N pulses) so that stationary clutter cancels and moving targets remain. CNR: clutter-to-noise ratio of a single pulse. Improvement factor I: how much the processor suppresses clutter relative to targets. Transmitter instability sets a hard ceiling on I.

Why pulse-to-pulse stability is a PA spec. A Doppler processor sees clutter as a strong, steady echo. If every pulse is identical, the clutter echo is identical from pulse to pulse and lands in the zero-Doppler bin where it can be rejected. If the transmitted pulses differ randomly by a small amplitude ε and phase δφ, the clutter echo inherits the same variation. That random part is not at zero Doppler; it spreads evenly over all Doppler bins as a new noise floor, CNR + S dB above thermal noise, where S is the stability in dBc. A moving target that would have been clearly visible against thermal noise may now sit below the clutter residue.

S = 10·log₁₀(σφ² + σa²) dBc (σφ in rad, σa = relative amplitude error = σdB·ln10/20) Imax ≈ −S σφ = 0.1° rms = 1.75 mrad → −55.2 dBc. σa = 0.01 dB rms = 1.15·10⁻³ → −58.8 dBc. Together S = −53.6 dBc. With 60 dB CNR the clutter residue is 6.4 dB above thermal noise, and every target loses about 7.3 dB of SNR. The same PA with 40 dB CNR (lighter clutter) loses only 0.1 dB.

Droop and rise time. Droop does not change from pulse to pulse, so it does not limit MTI on its own, but it lowers pulse energy, and through AM-PM and phase pushing (step 4) it becomes a phase slope or curve along the pulse. In a chirp, a phase error along the pulse raises the range sidelobes after pulse compression. Rise and fall times set the spectral width of the pulse edges: the edge occupies roughly 0.35/tr of bandwidth, so a 20 ns rise time spreads energy over ±17 MHz, which the spectrum mask and nearby receivers see.

Worked example: sizing the capacitor bank

A PA draws 10 A from 50 V during a 100 µs pulse. To keep droop small, the bank must hold ΔV small: with C = 1000 µF, ΔV = 10 × 100·10⁻⁶ / 10⁻³ = 1 V, 2 % of VDD. Saturated output scales roughly as VDD², so power droops 4 % (0.18 dB). If the PA's phase pushing is 1 °/V, the phase walks 1° along the pulse. If the charger does not recharge the bank to exactly the same voltage before every pulse (for example ±10 mV rms because of the charger's regulation and the PRF pattern), that is a pulse-to-pulse phase error of 0.01° and an amplitude error of 0.02 % rms. Both are well below the budget above, so here the supply is not the limit; the exciter and the PA's thermal transients usually are.

Check yourself

A system needs 60 dB of clutter cancellation. What pulse-to-pulse phase stability does the transmitter need, if amplitude is perfect?

S ≤ −60 dBc, so σφ ≤ 10⁻³ rad = 0.057° rms. The PA can have only a fraction of this, because the exciter, the LO and the receiver share the same budget.

Why does droop matter less for MTI than pulse-to-pulse variation?

Droop is the same in every pulse, so the clutter echoes are still identical from pulse to pulse and still cancel. Only the part that changes between pulses spreads clutter into the Doppler bins.

Step 8

Duty cycle and thermal limits

You already know

An RC circuit driven by a pulse train charges during each pulse and discharges between pulses. If the pulses are short compared with the time constant, the capacitor voltage barely ripples and settles at the average. If the pulses are long, it nearly reaches the full value each time. Heat flow behaves the same way: thermal resistance is the R, the heat capacity of the die, attach and flange is the C, and temperature is the voltage.

Terms used in this step

Thermal impedance Zth(τ, D): the peak temperature rise per watt of peak dissipation for pulses of width τ at duty cycle D. For very short pulses Zth → D·Rth (only the average heat counts); for CW Zth = Rth. Datasheets give it as a family of curves.

Thermal time constant: how fast each layer heats. Roughly: the die channel in tens of µs, the die and attach in hundreds of µs, the flange in milliseconds, the heat sink in seconds. The demo uses an illustrative three-layer model: 20 % of Rth with 20 µs, 30 % with 400 µs and 50 % with 8 ms.

Tj,max: the maximum junction (channel) temperature allowed. GaN devices are often rated to 225 °C absolute and derated to 150–200 °C for long life; GaAs to 150–175 °C. MTTF: mean time to failure. It falls exponentially with Tj (Arrhenius law): MTTF ∝ exp(Ea / kTj). With an activation energy Ea ≈ 1.7 eV, 10 °C hotter at about 200 °C cuts life by a factor of about 2.4.

Average heat is not the whole story. A pulsed PA at 10 % duty dissipates on average one tenth of its peak dissipation. If the pulses were very short the junction would see only that average. But real pulses (tens to hundreds of µs) are longer than the die's own time constants, so the junction heats up during each pulse and the peak temperature is well above what the average suggests. The peak is what ages the device.

ΔTpeak = Σi Ppk·Ri·(1 − e−τ/τᵢ) / (1 − e−PRI/τᵢ) Tj,peak = Tbase + ΔTpeak Each layer i (thermal resistance Ri, time constant τi) reaches a periodic steady state. Duty 100 % gives ΔT = P·Rth (CW). τ ≪ τi gives ΔT → D·P·Ri (the average).

Worked example

A GaN PA delivers 200 W peak at ηD = 50 %, so it dissipates 200 W during each pulse. Rth = 0.8 °C/W, base 70 °C, duty 10 %. Average-only estimate: 20 W × 0.8 = 16 °C, Tj = 86 °C. With 100 µs pulses: the three-layer model gives ΔTpeak = 52 °C, Tj = 122 °C. With 1 ms pulses at the same 10 % duty: 89 °C rise, Tj = 159 °C. CW: 160 °C rise, Tj = 230 °C, beyond any rating. Same average power, same device: the pulse width alone moves the junction by 37 °C, which with Ea = 1.7 eV is a factor of about 75 in life. This is why a "10 % duty" rating is meaningless without the pulse width, and why long-pulse modes (for energy on target) are thermally the hardest.

Step 9

Load VSWR tolerance: what a bad antenna match does to the PA

You already know

A wave on a line that meets a load other than Z₀ is partly reflected, with reflection coefficient Γ = (ZL − Z₀)/(ZL + Z₀). Forward and reflected waves add to a standing wave with peaks of (1 + |Γ|) and troughs of (1 − |Γ|) times the forward amplitude. Where along the line the PA sits relative to those peaks depends on the line length, i.e. on the phase of Γ. A small-signal amplifier tolerates this quietly. A PA, already at its voltage and current limits, does not.

Terms used in this step

VSWR = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|). Return loss RL = −20·log₁₀|Γ|. Mismatch loss = −10·log₁₀(1 − |Γ|²): power that never reaches the load. Load-pull: measuring a device's output power, efficiency and so on while a tuner moves its load impedance over the Smith chart; the result is a set of contours around the optimum load Zopt.

Isolator: a ferrite three-port circulator with one port terminated in a load. Forward power passes with 0.2–0.5 dB loss; reflected power is steered into the load, attenuated by the isolation (20–30 dB) on its way back. Load VSWR tolerance (ruggedness): the datasheet statement that the device survives, e.g., 10:1 VSWR at all phases at a stated power and supply, without damage. Survival is not performance: output power, efficiency and spectrum can all be far off spec while it survives.

Current-source model used in the demo: the transistor delivers a fundamental current up to I₁ and a voltage swing up to VDD − Vk, whichever limit it hits first. At the optimum load Ropt both limits are reached together; that is how Ropt is chosen.

Two ways a mismatch hurts. If the load seen by the transistor rises above Ropt (high |Z|), the voltage limit is hit first: the output clips earlier, power drops, harmonics rise and the drain voltage peak sits at its limit for longer, which is where breakdown and gate-drain stress happen. If the load falls below Ropt (low |Z|), the current limit is hit first: power drops, but DC current stays high, so dissipation rises and the device runs hotter. Which of the two you get depends only on the phase of the reflection, i.e. on the cable length between the PA and the antenna. That is why ruggedness is always specified "at all phases".

The isolator's job. With an isolator, the PA sees the isolator's own good input match plus the antenna reflection attenuated by the isolation. A VSWR 3 antenna (|Γ| = 0.5) behind a 20 dB isolator looks to the PA like |Γ| ≈ 0.5 × 0.1 + 0.05 = 0.10 (VSWR 1.22) at worst. The reflected power is not gone: it is in the isolator's load, which must be rated for it (up to the full PA power if the antenna is disconnected or a radome is iced).

|Γ| = (VSWR − 1)/(VSWR + 1) Prefl = |Γ|²·Pfwd Vpeak,line = (1 + |Γ|)·Vfwd VSWR 2: |Γ| = 0.333, RL 9.5 dB, 11 % reflected, mismatch loss 0.51 dB. VSWR 3: |Γ| = 0.5, RL 6.0 dB, 25 % reflected, 1.25 dB. Peak line voltage 1.5 × forward: a 100 W PA on a 50 Ω line (100 V peak forward) puts 150 V peaks on the connectors and filter, which matters for high-power breakdown and multipaction.

Worked example

A 100 W GaN PA (VDD = 50 V, Vk = 5 V, so Ropt = 45²/200 = 10.1 Ω) drives an antenna with VSWR 3 and no isolator. Matched, the simple current-source model gives 100 W out and 41.5 W of heat (ηD 71 %). At VSWR 3, over all cable phases, the output swings between 33 W and 60 W, and at the low-impedance phases the heat rises to 108 W, 2.6 times the matched value; on Rth = 0.8 °C/W the junction runs 53 °C hotter. With a 20 dB isolator the PA sees VSWR 1.22: it delivers 82–98 W, the heat rises to at most 60 W (+15 °C), and 25 W goes into the isolator load. Real devices differ in detail (load-pull contours are not this symmetric), but the size of the effect is typical.

Step 10

GaN, GaAs and TWT: three ways to make RF power

You already know

Power is voltage times current. A device that can stand a higher voltage can deliver the same power at a lower current, into a higher (easier to match) impedance. A material that conducts heat well lets you pack more power into a small die. These two facts, breakdown field and thermal conductivity, decide most of the differences between PA technologies.

Terms used in this step

GaAs pHEMT: gallium arsenide high-electron-mobility transistor, low breakdown (drain supply 5–10 V), power density about 0.5–1 W/mm of gate width, excellent at high frequency and low noise. GaN HEMT: gallium nitride on silicon carbide, high breakdown (28–50 V supply), power density 4–8 W/mm, SiC substrate conducts heat about 3× better than GaAs. TWT (travelling-wave tube): a vacuum tube in which an electron beam travels along a slow-wave helix and gives energy to the RF wave travelling with it; kilovolt supplies, very high power and very wide bandwidth from one device.

SSPA: solid-state power amplifier, many transistors combined (step 11). EPC: the electronic power conditioner, the high-voltage supply of a TWT. MDC: multi-stage depressed collector, which recovers energy from the spent electron beam and gives TWTs their high overall efficiency. Graceful degradation: the property of a combined SSPA that losing one device lowers power a little instead of losing the transmitter.

Why GaN replaced GaAs for power. With 5–10× the breakdown voltage, a GaN device delivers its power at higher voltage and lower current, so its optimum load is several ohms instead of a fraction of an ohm, the matching network is lower in loss and wider in bandwidth, and fewer devices are needed for a given power. The SiC substrate removes the heat. GaAs remains strong at low power, very high frequency, in low-noise roles, and where its lower supply voltage is convenient (T/R modules in some arrays).

Why TWTs are still built. One tube gives hundreds of watts to kilowatts over an octave or more, with overall efficiency (with a depressed collector) of 50–60 %, high enough to compete with or beat SSPAs at high power. The costs are a kilovolt power supply, warm-up time, a finite cathode life, a single point of failure, a little more noise and spurious output from the HV supply, and different behaviour in saturation (sharper knee, larger AM-PM, typically 30–50° from small signal to saturation).

Check yourself

Why does a higher supply voltage make a PA easier to match?

Name two reasons an airborne jammer might still choose a TWT.

Very wide instantaneous bandwidth from one device (for example 6–18 GHz) and high power per kilogram and per litre at high efficiency. The costs are the HV supply, warm-up and a single point of failure.

Step 11

Combining: many devices into one output

You already know

A Wilkinson divider used backwards is a combiner: two equal, in-phase inputs add their power at the output, and anything that is not equal and in phase is dumped into the isolation resistor. That resistor is what lets the combiner tolerate a failed input, and it is also where imbalance turns into heat.

Terms used in this step

Binary (corporate) combiner: a tree of 2-way combiners, k stages for N = 2k inputs. Spatial or radial combiner: N inputs combined in one step in a waveguide or radial line, lower loss for large N. Combining efficiency ηc = output power / sum of input powers. Amplitude and phase imbalance: the spread (rms) of the device outputs' amplitude (dB) and insertion phase (°) about their mean.

Three losses. (1) Dissipative loss of the combiner itself, about 0.15–0.3 dB per 2-way stage in microstrip or stripline at X-band, lower in waveguide or spatial combiners. For a binary tree it grows as k × loss per stage, so deep trees cost real power. (2) Imbalance: the outputs do not add perfectly in phase. For random errors with σφ (rad) and σa (natural-log amplitude), the expected combining efficiency is close to 1/N + (1 − 1/N)·e−σφ²·e−σa². (3) Failures: if f of N devices fail (and present their matched off-state to the combiner), the output is ((N − f)/N)² of nominal: losing 1 of 8 costs 1.2 dB, not 0.6 dB, because the dead input's share of the live devices' power goes into the isolation resistors.

Pout = N·Pdev · 10−k·L/10 · ηimb · ((N − f)/N)² ηimb ≈ 1/N + (1 − 1/N)·e−σφ²·e−(σdB·ln10/20)² 8 × 50 W, 3 stages × 0.2 dB: 400 W × 0.871 = 348 W. σφ = 5° rms adds only 0.03 dB. One device failed: 348 × 0.766 = 267 W. The array version of this formula (with spatial combining in free space) is in module 10, T/R modules.

Worked example: when doubling stops paying

With 0.25 dB per stage, going from 64 to 128 devices doubles the device power (+3 dB) but adds a stage (−0.25 dB): +2.75 dB net. Going from 1 to 128 devices gives 21.07 dB of device power but loses 1.75 dB in the tree. With 0.5 dB per stage (long lines at Ku-band) the tree loses 3.5 dB, more than one whole doubling. This is why large solid-state transmitters use low-loss spatial combiners or, in active arrays, combine in free space after the antenna element.

Step 12

From PA watts to EIRP, radar range and J/S

You already know

From step 1: EIRP = PPA − losses + G. From the radar pages: a radar's echo falls as 1/R⁴ (out and back, each 1/R²), so the received echo power is proportional to Pt/R⁴. A jammer's signal at the radar falls only as 1/R² (one way). These two powers of R are why the PA matters so differently in a radar and in a jammer.

Terms used in this step

Radar equation (detection range): Rmax⁴ = Pt·τ·N·G²·λ²·σ / ((4π)³·k·T₀·F·(S/N)min·L). Pt peak power, τ pulse width, N pulses integrated coherently, G antenna gain, λ wavelength, σ target RCS (radar cross-section, m²), k Boltzmann's constant, T₀ = 290 K, F receiver noise figure, L losses. Energy on target Pt·τ·N is what really counts, which is why pulse width and duty (step 8) matter as much as peak power.

J/S (jamming-to-signal ratio): jammer power divided by target echo power inside the radar receiver. For a self-protection jammer on the target: J/S = (EIRPj / EIRPr) · (4π R² / σ) · (Br / Bj). Bj/Br: jammer bandwidth over radar receiver bandwidth; noise spread over a wider band than the radar listens to is wasted. Burn-through range: the range at which the echo has grown enough that J/S falls to the value the jammer needs; inside it the radar detects the target despite the jamming.

Radar: R ∝ Pt¼. Double the PA power and detection range grows by 2¼ = 1.19. A 1 dB loss after the PA costs 5.6 % of range. To double the range you need 16 × the power (12 dB). This weak dependence is why radar designers also buy range with antenna gain (G² in the equation), pulse width, integration and receiver noise figure.

Jammer: J/S ∝ Pj. J/S rises dB for dB with jammer power, and only falls as R² as the target closes (the echo rises as 1/R⁴, the jamming as 1/R²). Every dB of jammer PA power, every dB of post-PA loss saved, every dB of jammer antenna gain, and every dB of bandwidth not wasted (matching Bj to Br) is a dB of J/S, and moves burn-through inward by a factor 10−1/20 = 0.89 per dB.

ΔR/R = 10ΔP(dB)/40 − 1 ΔJ/S (dB) = ΔPj (dB) Rbt = R · 10(J/S − J/Sreq)/(−20) Radar at 10 GHz, G = 35 dBi, σ = 1 m², τ = 10 µs, N = 16, F = 4 dB, L = 6 dB, (S/N)min = 13 dB: 1 kW gives 30.9 km; 2 kW gives 36.7 km. Jammer: EIRP 30 dBW (100 W, 10 dBi) against a radar EIRP of 85 dBW, σ = 10 m², R = 50 km, Bj/Br = 20 dB: J/S = 30 − 85 + 95.0 − 20 ≈ 20 dB. Needing 10 dB, burn-through is at 50 × 10−10/20 = 15.9 km.

Where your component shows up

  • Everyone in the transmit chain: in a jammer, your 0.1 dB is 0.1 dB of J/S; in a radar it is 0.6 % of range. The same component is far more valuable, per dB, in a jammer.
  • PA designers: for a jammer, the average power on the actual jamming waveform (step 5), not Psat, is the number to quote.

Check yourself

A radar needs 26 % more range. How much more PA power, everything else unchanged?

1.26⁴ = 2.52, so 4.0 dB (2.5 × the power).

A jammer has 20 dB J/S at 50 km and needs 10 dB. The target closes to 25 km. Is it still jamming effectively?

J/S falls 6 dB per halving of range: 14 dB at 25 km, still above 10 dB. Burn-through is at 15.9 km.

Reference

Glossary

ACPR
Adjacent channel power ratio: power in the neighbouring channel relative to the wanted channel, dBc.
AM-AM
Change of output amplitude with input amplitude (the compression curve treated as distortion).
AM-PM
Change of insertion phase with input amplitude, °/dB or total degrees to a stated compression.
Back-off (IBO, OBO)
How far below saturation (or P1dB) the PA is operated, at the input or the output, in dB.
Burn-through range
Range at which a radar's echo overcomes the jamming, i.e. J/S falls to the required value.
CCDF
Fraction of time the envelope power exceeds a given level above its average.
Class A / AB / B
Bias classes: conduction over the full cycle, a bit more than half, or exactly half. Maximum ideal efficiency 50 %, between, 78.5 %.
Clutter
Unwanted echoes from ground, sea, rain; strong and near zero Doppler.
CNR
Clutter-to-noise ratio of a single pulse.
Combining efficiency
Combined output power divided by the sum of the device output powers.
CPI
Coherent processing interval: the group of pulses processed together in a Doppler FFT.
dBc, dBm, dBW
dB relative to the carrier, to 1 mW, to 1 W.
Doherty amplifier
Main and peaking amplifiers with load modulation; high efficiency over about 6 dB of back-off.
Drain efficiency ηD
RF output power divided by DC input power.
Droop
Fall of output power along a pulse, in dB, mostly from supply sag and heating.
Duty cycle
Pulse width divided by pulse repetition interval.
EIRP
Effective isotropic radiated power: power at the antenna port times antenna gain.
Envelope
Instantaneous amplitude of the RF signal, |x(t)|.
EPC
Electronic power conditioner: the high-voltage supply of a TWT.
GaAs pHEMT
Gallium arsenide pseudomorphic high-electron-mobility transistor: low voltage, very high frequency.
GaN HEMT
Gallium nitride HEMT, usually on SiC: high breakdown voltage and power density.
Harmonic
Output at an integer multiple of the carrier frequency.
IM3, IM5
Third- and fifth-order intermodulation products of two tones, at 2f₁ − f₂ and 3f₁ − 2f₂.
Improvement factor
Clutter suppression of an MTI / Doppler processor relative to targets; limited by transmitter stability.
Isolator
Ferrite device that passes power forward and diverts reflected power into a load.
J/S
Jamming-to-signal ratio inside the victim receiver.
Knee voltage Vk
Drain voltage below which the transistor stops acting as a current source.
Load-pull
Measurement of PA performance while the load impedance is moved over the Smith chart.
MDC
Multi-stage depressed collector: recovers energy from a TWT's spent electron beam.
MTI
Moving target indication: cancelling stationary clutter by comparing pulses.
MTTF
Mean time to failure; falls exponentially with junction temperature.
OIP3
Output third-order intercept: where extrapolated IM3 and fundamental lines would meet.
P1dB, P3dB
Output power at 1 dB and 3 dB gain compression.
PAE
Power-added efficiency: (Pout − Pin)/PDC.
PAPR
Peak-to-average power ratio of the signal envelope.
PEP
Peak envelope power.
Phase pushing
Change of PA phase with supply voltage, °/V.
PRF, PRI
Pulse repetition frequency and interval.
Psat
Saturated output power: the level the output approaches under heavy drive.
Pulse-to-pulse stability
Random variation of amplitude and phase between pulses, rms or dBc.
Rapp model
Pout = G·Pin / (1 + (G·Pin/Psat)p)1/p; p sets the sharpness of the knee.
RCS σ
Radar cross-section of a target, m².
Ropt
Load resistance at which the transistor reaches its voltage and current limits together.
Rth, Zth
Thermal resistance (°C/W, steady state) and thermal impedance (peak rise per watt for a given pulse width and duty).
Rise / fall time
10 % to 90 % time of the RF envelope at the start and end of a pulse.
SDR
Signal-to-distortion ratio: wanted output power over the power of everything the PA added.
Spectral regrowth
Distortion skirts that grow beside a modulated signal after a nonlinear PA.
SSPA
Solid-state power amplifier, usually many combined transistors.
Tj
Junction (channel) temperature of the transistor.
TWT
Travelling-wave tube: vacuum amplifier with an electron beam and a slow-wave helix.
VSWR, Γ, return loss
Measures of mismatch: (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|), reflection coefficient, −20·log₁₀|Γ|.
Wilkinson combiner
Isolated in-phase power combiner; imbalance and failed-input power go to its resistor.
