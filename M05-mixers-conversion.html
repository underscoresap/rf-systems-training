Mixers, the LO and frequency conversion, from two sine waves to the frequency plan

A mixer moves a signal from one frequency to another. It does this by multiplying the signal with a local oscillator, and every imperfection of that multiplication shows up somewhere in the system: as lost sensitivity, as a false target, as a signal heard at the wrong frequency, or as a tone that an enemy receiver can detect. This page starts from the product of two sines, builds the switching picture of a real diode or FET mixer, and then follows each datasheet line (conversion loss, noise figure, LO drive, isolation, IIP3, spurs, image rejection, IQ balance) through to its system effect, with numbers. It ends with the choice of frequency plan and the FMCW dechirp mixer.

Step 0

Why a receiver converts frequency at all

You already know

A filter is easiest to build when its bandwidth is a sensible fraction of its centre frequency. Gain is cheaper and more stable at low frequency. An ADC samples a limited bandwidth, and its cost rises steeply with sample rate.

A radar at 10 GHz needs to separate its own echo from a jammer 20 MHz away. A 20 MHz wide filter at 10 GHz has a fractional bandwidth of 0.2 %, which needs resonators with an unloaded Q of several thousand and drifts with temperature. The same 20 MHz filter at 140 MHz is 14 % fractional: a routine design. An ESM receiver that must cover 2 to 18 GHz cannot digitise 16 GHz at once with good dynamic range, but it can move any 500 MHz slice of that band to a fixed frequency where one well-designed filter, amplifier and ADC do all the work.

So almost every receiver moves the signal: from the radio frequency (RF) where it arrives, to an intermediate frequency (IF) or straight to baseband (around 0 Hz), where filtering, gain and digitising are easy. The part that does the move is the mixer; the reference tone it uses is the local oscillator (LO). If the LO is tuned, the receiver is tuned: the IF stays fixed while the RF it listens to changes. This is the superheterodyne idea, and it has been the core of radio receivers for a hundred years.

Transmitters use the same part in reverse (an upconverter): the waveform is made at low frequency, where it is easy to generate precisely, and mixed up to RF.

Terms used on this page

RF, LO, IF ports: a mixer has three ports. The signal enters at RF, the local oscillator at LO, and the converted signal leaves at IF. The same device works in both directions, so an upconverter feeds IF in and takes RF out.

Local oscillator (LO): a clean, strong, single-frequency tone made inside the equipment, usually by a synthesiser. Its purity (phase noise and spurs) is covered in the Synthesiser and phase noise module; this page treats the LO as a given tone of known power.

Baseband: a band that starts at or near 0 Hz. A zero-IF receiver converts straight to baseband.

dBc: power relative to a reference carrier, here usually the wanted IF output. −60 dBc is one millionth of its power.

ESM (electronic support measures): a receiver that listens for other people's emissions to detect, identify and locate them. ESM appears twice on this page: as a user of mixers, and as the threat that can detect a mixer's LO leakage.

Step 1

Frequency conversion is multiplication of two sines

You already know

A linear amplifier cannot create new frequencies: what comes out is the input, scaled and delayed. New frequencies need a non-linear or time-varying part. You also know the school identity cos A · cos B = ½ cos(A − B) + ½ cos(A + B).

Definitions

High-side and low-side LO: high-side means fLO > fRF (LO above the signal), low-side means fLO < fRF. With high-side LO the IF spectrum is mirrored: a chirp rising in frequency at RF falls at IF.

Image frequency: the other input frequency that lands on the same IF, on the opposite side of the LO, 2 × fIF away from the wanted signal.

Put a signal cos(2π fRF t) and an LO cos(2π fLO t) into an ideal multiplier. The identity says the output contains exactly two tones: one at the difference |fRF − fLO| and one at the sum fRF + fLO, each with half the product of the amplitudes. Nothing at fRF, nothing at fLO. A filter after the multiplier keeps the one you want: the difference for a downconverter, the sum (or difference) for an upconverter.

The amplitude and phase of the signal are carried over unchanged. If the RF signal is a radar pulse with a chirp on it, the IF signal is the same pulse with the same chirp, just centred somewhere else. That is why a mixer is called a frequency translator: it is linear in the signal (double the RF, double the IF) even though it is a multiplier.

Now notice a problem that runs through the rest of the page. The difference |fRF − fLO| does not care which side of the LO the RF is on. With the LO at 11 GHz and the IF at 1 GHz, a signal at 10 GHz gives 1 GHz, and so does a signal at 12 GHz. That second input is the image. The multiplier cannot tell them apart. Only something before the mixer (a filter, Step 7) or a cleverer mixer (the IQ mixer, Step 8) can.

cos(ωRFt) · cos(ωLOt) = ½ cos((ωRF − ωLO)t) + ½ cos((ωRF + ωLO)t)    fimage = 2 fLO − fRFω = 2πf. A real mixer is not a perfect multiplier: it multiplies by a square-ish wave (Step 2), so it also makes |fRF ± 3 fLO|, |fRF ± 5 fLO| and, because it is not perfectly linear, |m fRF ± n fLO| (Step 6).

What goes wrong with the simplest approach

Naive idea 1: add the LO to the signal and amplify. A sum of 1.2 GHz and 1.0 GHz through a linear amplifier contains only 1.2 GHz and 1.0 GHz. There is no 0.2 GHz at all: adding is not mixing. You need a product.

Naive idea 2: a perfect multiplier with nothing around it. The output then holds 0.2 GHz and 2.2 GHz at the same 0.5 V, so without an IF filter the 2.2 GHz sum reaches the IF amplifier at full strength. And without a filter in front, an emitter at the 0.8 GHz image also produces 0.2 GHz at 0.5 V: the receiver cannot tell it from the wanted 1.2 GHz signal.

Why does a linear amplifier with 30 dB of gain not mix two tones?

A perfectly linear system obeys superposition, so the output only contains the input frequencies. Mixing needs a product term, which only a non-linear or time-varying element provides. Real amplifiers do mix a little: that is intermodulation (Step 5).

Step 2

LO drive and the switching picture: why a diode mixer loses about 6 dB

You already know

A Schottky diode conducts once its forward voltage passes a turn-on value (about 0.2 to 0.6 V depending on barrier height). A FET channel switches between a low on-resistance and open as its gate swings past threshold. Transformers and baluns turn a single-ended signal into a balanced pair.

Definitions

Conversion loss (CL): RF input power minus IF output power, in dB, for the wanted conversion. A Gilbert cell has negative loss, called conversion gain.

Commutating (switching) mixer: a mixer in which the LO switches the signal path, so the RF is multiplied by a square-like wave rather than a sine.

Starved LO: LO drive below the level the mixer was designed for. Conversion loss, noise figure and IIP3 all get worse, often steeply.

The double-balanced diode mixer. The most common microwave mixer is four Schottky diodes in a ring, with a balun on the LO port and one on the RF port, and the IF taken from the balun centre taps. The LO is made much larger than the RF signal. On one half of the LO cycle, one pair of diodes is driven hard on and connects the RF to the IF with one polarity; on the other half, the other pair is on and connects it with the opposite polarity. So the RF signal is simply multiplied by a square wave that flips between +1 and −1 at the LO rate. The diodes act as a reversing switch (a commutator), and the small RF signal barely affects when they switch. That is why the mixer is linear in the RF signal.

Why a square wave gives 3.9 dB of loss even with perfect switches. A ±1 square wave is (4/π)[cos ωt − ⅓ cos 3ωt + ⅕ cos 5ωt − …]. Multiplying the RF by its first term gives, by Step 1, a difference tone of amplitude ½ × 4/π = 2/π = 0.637 of the RF. That is −3.92 dB. The rest of the RF power is not lost as heat: it goes to the sum frequency and to |fRF ± 3fLO|, |fRF ± 5fLO|, each with amplitude 2/(πn). Add up the powers of all of them, 2 × Σ (2/πn)² over odd n, and you get exactly 1. So the ideal commutating mixer has 3.92 dB conversion loss because it splits the RF power among many output frequencies and you keep only one.

Why the LO must be strong: the conduction angle. Real diodes need a voltage before they conduct. In a ring, each conducting path has two diodes in series, so the LO must exceed about twice the diode turn-on voltage before anything happens. While the LO swings through the middle of its cycle, neither pair conducts: the switch is open and the RF is not passed. The fraction of the cycle in which a pair is on is the conduction angle. With a sine LO of peak Vpk and a threshold Vth, a pair is on while |cos ωt| > Vth/Vpk. The fundamental of this "square wave with gaps" shrinks to (4/π)·sin φ0, where φ0 = arccos(Vth/Vpk). Less LO means bigger gaps, a smaller fundamental, and more conversion loss. Add about 1 dB for balun loss and diode series resistance, and the numbers below appear.

LO drive levels. Mixer families are sold by the LO power they need: "level 7" (+7 dBm), "level 13", "level 17", up to +23 dBm. A higher barrier diode, or more diodes in series per arm, needs more LO but tolerates a larger RF signal before the RF itself starts to switch the diodes. That is the trade at the heart of mixer linearity (Step 5): IIP3 rises roughly dB for dB with LO drive, and so does the power your LO chain must deliver.

FET and active mixers. A passive FET ring (GaAs pHEMT or CMOS switches) works the same way with the gates as the switch control: lower on-resistance, sharper switching, higher IIP3 for a given LO, and almost no DC power. An active Gilbert cell (a differential transistor pair whose tail current is switched by a second, LO-driven pair) gives conversion gain instead of loss, needs only about 0 dBm of LO, but has a higher noise figure and a lower IIP3. It is common in integrated transceivers.

What goes wrong with the simplest approach

Under-driving the ring. A low-barrier ring needs about 0.4 V across each arm before anything conducts. At +1 dBm the LO peak is only 0.35 V: no diode ever turns on and there is no conversion at all. At +4 dBm (0.50 V) the diodes conduct only 41 % of the time and conversion loss is 9.2 dB, 2.7 dB worse than at the rated +7 dBm.

Using one small-signal diode as a multiplier. A single diode with a weak LO works by its square-law curve rather than by switching. Conversion loss is typically 20 to 30 dB, IIP3 is near the LO level, and with no balance the LO comes straight out of the RF port. The switching ring exists to fix all three.

How to choose

  • Start from the IIP3 you need (Step 5). IIP3 ≈ PLO + 8 to 10 dB, so a mixer IIP3 of +25 dBm means a level-17 class part.
  • Leave margin above the knee. LO power varies 2 to 3 dB over band and temperature. On the low-barrier curve, +7 dBm already sits on the knee; +10 dBm gives 5.6 dB loss and room for a 3 dB droop.
  • Count the cost: each 3 dB more LO doubles the LO amplifier's output power and raises the leaked LO by 3 dB (Step 4).
  • Pick the element: diode ring for wide band and robustness, FET ring for the highest IIP3 per mW of LO, Gilbert cell when LO power is scarce and gain is wanted.

Why is the ideal switching mixer loss 3.9 dB and not 6 dB?

A sine LO gives IF amplitude ½; a ±1 square wave has a fundamental of 4/π, so the IF amplitude is 2/π = 0.637, which is −3.9 dB. The square wave puts more of the RF power into the wanted product.

Step 3

Conversion loss, conversion gain and noise figure: SSB vs DSB

You already know

A passive attenuator of loss L at 290 K has a noise figure of L dB. Friis' formula: Fsys = F1 + (F2 − 1)/G1 + …, so a stage with gain in front hides the noise of the stages behind it. Both are developed in the LNA and receiver noise module.

Definitions

Image-band noise: the thermal noise that sits at the image frequency and converts to the IF together with the wanted band's noise.

Equivalent noise temperature Te: the noise a stage adds, expressed as the temperature of a matched resistor at its input that would produce the same noise: Te = 290 (F − 1). Contributions in kelvin simply add when referred to the same point.

A passive mixer behaves like an attenuator for noise. To first order, a diode or FET mixer is a lossy, time-varying network at room temperature. Its noise figure is therefore close to its conversion loss: a 6.5 dB CL mixer has an NF of about 7 dB. The extra half dB comes from diode shot noise and from noise in the conduction gaps.

Two input bands land on one IF, and so does their noise. From Step 1, both the signal band (around fRF) and the image band (around 2fLO − fRF) convert to the IF with equal conversion loss. Thermal noise exists in both bands. So the IF output carries the noise from two RF bands, while a radar echo or a radio signal sits in only one of them.

This gives two ways of quoting noise figure:

  • SSB (single-sideband) NF: the signal is in one band only, the noise comes from both. This is the case for a radar, an ESM receiver and nearly every communication link. Use this number.
  • DSB (double-sideband) NF: the signal is assumed to be in both bands, as in a radiometer that measures broadband noise. The signal power doubles for the same noise, so DSB NF reads about 3 dB lower than SSB NF. A datasheet that quotes DSB NF looks 3 dB better than the mixer will be in your radar.

The image noise problem in a receiver. With an LNA in front of the mixer, the LNA amplifies noise across its whole band, image band included. If nothing removes that noise before the mixer, the amplified image-band noise folds onto the IF as well and the system noise is almost doubled: up to 3 dB of sensitivity lost before anyone notices. An image filter between LNA and mixer (or an image-reject mixer, Step 8) removes it. This is a separate job from rejecting image signals (Step 7), but the same filter does both.

Active mixers. A Gilbert cell with +10 dB conversion gain and 11 dB NF looks worse than a 7 dB passive mixer on its own. Behind a 20 dB LNA, its gain then hides the IF amplifier, and the system NF ends up nearly the same. The comparison only means something in the full cascade.

Step 4

Isolation (LO-RF, LO-IF, RF-IF) and LO leakage out of the antenna

You already know

A balun converts single-ended to balanced. If the two halves are perfectly equal in amplitude and phase, anything common to both cancels at a balanced port. Isolation in a coupler or a switch is the leakage from one port to another, in dB.

Why a double-balanced mixer isolates its ports. In the diode ring, the LO drives the ring through one balun and the RF through another, at right angles electrically: the LO voltage appears at the RF balun's terminals as a common-mode voltage, which a balanced port does not see, and vice versa. The IF is taken from the centre taps, where both RF and LO cancel. With perfect baluns and perfectly matched diodes the ports would be completely isolated. In practice, balun amplitude and phase imbalance and diode mismatch leave residues:

  • LO-RF isolation (typically 25 to 45 dB): how much LO comes out of the RF port. Since the LO is the strongest signal in the receiver (+7 to +17 dBm), even 35 dB of isolation leaves −22 dBm heading back towards the antenna.
  • LO-IF isolation (20 to 40 dB): LO at the IF port. It can overload the IF amplifier or ADC, and in an upconverter it appears in the transmitted spectrum.
  • RF-IF isolation (15 to 30 dB): RF passing straight to the IF port without conversion. A strong signal that happens to sit at the IF frequency gets through (the "IF feedthrough" response, Step 6).

LO leakage out of the antenna. The leaked LO travels backwards through the chain: through the LNA in reverse (its reverse isolation |S12|, typically 20 to 50 dB), through the preselector (which rejects it only if the LO lies outside the passband), and out of the antenna with the antenna's gain. A receiver that is supposed to be silent now radiates a steady, narrowband tone. An ESM receiver can detect it, and its frequency tells the ESM operator the receiver's IF and therefore what it is tuned to. For a passive sensor, a submarine's ESM mast, or a radar in receive-only mode, this is an emission-security problem. On the same platform, the leaked LO can also appear in your own ESM as a false emitter (co-site interference).

The defences are, in order of effect: choose the LO outside the antenna and preselector band (Step 9), put an LNA with good reverse isolation in front, buy or build a mixer with better LO-RF isolation, and use the lowest LO power the mixer allows.

Step 5

IIP3 and P1dB: the mixer's share of the dynamic range

You already know

Two tones at f1 and f2 through a slightly non-linear amplifier produce third-order products at 2f1 − f2 and 2f2 − f1, which rise 3 dB for every 1 dB of input. IIP3 is the input power at which the extrapolated product would equal the fundamental. P1dB is where the gain has dropped by 1 dB.

Why a mixer is non-linear in the RF signal. The switching picture of Step 2 assumes the RF is too small to affect when the diodes switch. As the RF grows, it adds to the LO across the diodes and moves the switching instants: the switch timing now depends on the signal, which is a non-linearity. The stronger the LO compared with the RF, the less the RF can do this. That is why IIP3 tracks LO drive: a diode double-balanced mixer typically has IIP3 ≈ PLO + 8 to 10 dB and an input P1dB ≈ PLO − 6 dB. A level-7 mixer: IIP3 about +15 dBm, P1dB about +1 dBm. Level 17: about +25 dBm and +11 dBm.

Why the mixer usually limits the receiver. In a cascade, the input-referred IIP3 is set by the stage that sees the largest signal compared with its own IIP3. The mixer sits after the LNA, so it sees the signal multiplied by the LNA gain. A mixer IIP3 of +15 dBm behind a 20 dB LNA acts like −5 dBm at the antenna, which is below a typical LNA's own IIP3. So the LNA gain that hides the mixer's noise (Step 3) also exposes the mixer's linearity. This is the central trade in receiver design: enough LNA gain for noise, not so much that the mixer limits the dynamic range.

Spur-free dynamic range. The useful range runs from the noise floor N (−174 dBm/Hz + NF + 10·log B) up to the input at which third-order products just reach N. That span, the SFDR, is (2/3)(IIP3 − N). Above it, two strong emitters make a false third emitter, or a jammer and a target make a false target.

1/IIP3sys = 1/IIP3LNA + GLNA/IIP3mix    SFDR = ⅔ (IIP3sys − N)    N = −174 + NFsys + 10·log BPowers in mW and gains linear in the first formula. The same form, with P1dB in place of IIP3, gives the cascade P1dB to a good approximation.

SFDR in 10 MHz falls from 64.7 to 58.3 dB: two −45 dBm emitters now make a false emitter above the noise.

Step 6

The spur chart: every m×RF ± n×LO that lands in the IF

You already know

A non-linear device driven by a tone makes harmonics. Driven by two tones, it makes every combination of their harmonics. Balanced circuits cancel even-order products when they are well balanced.

Where spurs come from. The mixer multiplies by a square-ish LO, so it contains all odd LO harmonics (n = 1, 3, 5 …), and imbalance lets the even ones through weakly. It is also slightly non-linear in the RF, so it makes RF harmonics m = 2, 3 … Every pair (m, n) produces outputs at |m·fin ± n·fLO|. The wanted conversion is (1, 1). Any input frequency fin for which some |m·fin ± n·fLO| falls in the IF passband is a spurious response: the receiver hears a signal there as if it were at the tuned frequency. Solving for fin:

fin = (n · fLO ± fIF) / m    order = m + n(1,1) gives the wanted signal and the image. (2,2) gives the half-IF spur, fLO ∓ fIF/2, only fIF/2 away from the wanted signal. (1,0) is IF feedthrough: an input at the IF frequency itself. Lower order means stronger.

How strong is a spur? Mixer datasheets give a spur table: the level of each (m, n) product in dBc below the wanted IF, measured at a stated RF input (often −10 dBm) and LO drive. Two rules make it usable at other levels:

  • A product with RF order m changes by m dB for each dB of RF input, while the wanted IF changes by 1 dB. So its rejection relative to the wanted output improves by (m − 1) dB for each dB the RF is reduced. A (2,2) spur at −60 dBc with RF −10 dBm is −80 dBc with RF −30 dBm.
  • Products with m = 1 (only LO harmonics) do not improve with lower RF: their rejection is fixed by the LO waveform and balance. The image (1,1) has no mixer rejection at all; only filtering or an IQ mixer removes it.

The system effect. A spurious response is a hole in the receiver's out-of-band rejection. For a radar it lets an out-of-band emitter (or jammer) into the receiver as if it were in band. For an ESM receiver it is worse: the receiver reports an emitter at the wrong frequency, and the identification library may name the wrong radar. A typical ESM requirement is that all spurious responses are 60 to 70 dB below the wanted response. The total rejection of each response is the spur-table rejection plus the preselector's attenuation at fin, which is why the frequency plan aims to push every low-order response outside the preselector.

What goes wrong with the simplest approach

Choose the IF for convenience and never draw the chart. A 0.5 GHz IF is attractive because filters and ADCs are easy there. With RF 10 GHz and a 1 GHz preselector, the image is at 11 GHz with only 28 dB of rejection, and the half-IF response at 10.25 GHz is inside the preselector, protected only by the mixer's 62 dB. Against a 60 dB spurious-response specification, the image alone fails by 32 dB.

Step 7

Image frequency and image rejection by filtering

You already know

A band-pass filter's rejection at a given offset rises with the number of poles (resonators) and falls with its bandwidth. A Butterworth filter of N poles falls by 20·N dB per decade of normalised offset far from the passband.

The image sits 2 × fIF from the wanted signal, on the far side of the LO. A filter in front of the mixer (the preselector and the image filter of Step 3) must attenuate it. The higher the IF, the further away the image, and the easier the filter. But a high IF makes the IF filter's fractional bandwidth smaller (harder), needs a higher-frequency LO, and needs a mixer whose IF port reaches that high. A low IF makes the IF filter and ADC easy and the image filter impossible. The frequency plan (Step 9) is the negotiation between these.

System effect. An emitter at the image frequency with power P appears in the IF as a signal of power P − Rimage, indistinguishable from a real signal at the tuned frequency. For a radar, a jammer at the image frequency jams the receiver as though it were in band. For ESM, the receiver reports a 12 GHz emitter as a 10 GHz one: wrong frequency, wrong identification. Typical requirements are 60 to 80 dB of image rejection.

Filter design itself is covered in the Filters and preselectors module (module 04 of this series). Here we only need its rejection at one frequency.

Step 8

IQ mixers: amplitude and phase imbalance set the image rejection

You already know

A 90° hybrid splits a signal into two equal outputs 90° apart. A complex number x + jy can be pictured as a phasor; e+jωt rotates one way and e−jωt the other, so positive and negative frequencies are different.

Why one mixer cannot separate the image. A single mixer output is a real signal. A real signal at +f and the same at −f are the same thing (cos is even), so the wanted signal (fRF − fLO = +fIF) and the image (fimage − fLO = −fIF) collide.

The IQ (quadrature) mixer. Use two mixers, fed by the same RF, with LOs 90° apart: cos(ωLOt) for the I (in-phase) mixer and sin(ωLOt) for the Q (quadrature) mixer. Treat the two outputs as one complex signal I + jQ. Now +fIF and −fIF are different: the wanted signal comes out as a phasor rotating one way and the image as one rotating the other way. A digital processor (or an analogue 90° IF hybrid in a Hartley image-reject mixer) keeps one and cancels the other. No filter is needed to separate them.

How well it cancels depends on balance. Cancellation needs the I and Q paths to have exactly equal gain and exactly 90° between them. Write the gain ratio as g (amplitude imbalance ε dB = 20·log g) and the phase error as φ. The residual image is set by how far the two paths are from perfect. One dB and one degree, both ordinary numbers for a hybrid plus two mixers over a wide band, give only about 25 dB. Forty dB needs about 0.1 dB and 1°; 50 dB needs 0.05 dB and 0.3°. Analogue hardware alone rarely beats 30 to 40 dB over a wide band, so systems measure the imbalance (inject a tone, see the image) and correct it digitally. A single-frequency calibration helps at that frequency; imbalance that varies across the band needs a wideband (frequency-dependent) correction.

IRR = (1 + 2g·cos φ + g²) / (1 − 2g·cos φ + g²)    ≈ 4 / (εlin² + φ²) for small errorsg = 10ε/20 is the I/Q gain ratio, φ the deviation from 90° in radians, εlin = g − 1. IRR is the power ratio of wanted to image output. 1 dB and 1°: g = 1.122, φ = 0.0175 rad, IRR = 296 = 24.7 dB.

What goes wrong with the simplest approach

One mixer and no filter: 0 dB of image rejection; wanted and image are identical at the output.

An uncorrected wideband IQ mixer: 1 dB and 1° of imbalance give 24.7 dB. A −30 dBm emitter on the image appears at −54.7 dBm, 35 dB above a −90 dBm floor.

Step 9

Frequency-plan choices: superhet, double conversion, zero-IF, low-IF and DC offset

You already know

Everything from Steps 3 to 8: image noise, LO leakage, spurs, image rejection by filters and by IQ balance. The frequency plan is the choice of IF(s) and LO(s) that balances them.

Single-conversion superheterodyne. One mixer to a fixed IF, typically 5 to 15 % of the RF. Image rejection comes from the preselector; an image-reject (IQ) mixer can add its 25 to 35 dB on top. The LO lies outside the RF band, so the preselector also cuts LO leakage. Simple and good, if the IF filter at that IF is buildable.

Double conversion. A high first IF (here 3 GHz) puts the first image far away (RF + 6 GHz, easy to reject), then a second mixer moves to a low second IF (here 140 MHz) where channel filtering and the ADC are easy. The second conversion has its own image (2 × 140 MHz from the first IF), which only the first IF filter can reject, and a new set of spurs and LO leakage paths. This is the standard plan for wideband ESM receivers and for radars that must hop across a wide band.

Zero-IF (direct conversion). The LO equals the RF; an IQ mixer converts straight to baseband. There is no image filter because the image is the signal's own mirror within the channel, separated only by IQ balance. No IF filter, no second LO: small, cheap, wideband, and common in integrated transceivers. Its weaknesses are:

  • DC offset by self-mixing. LO that leaks to the RF port (Step 4) reflects back and is mixed with the LO itself, giving a DC voltage. Equally, a strong RF signal that leaks into the LO port mixes with itself. The DC lands exactly in the middle of the wanted channel. With LO +7 dBm and 50 dB of LO-RF isolation, the leak is −43 dBm: about 47 dB above a −90 dBm sensitivity, eating ADC headroom and hiding weak signals near 0 Hz. It is removed by a high-pass filter or a DC-offset correction loop, both of which also remove the wanted signal's lowest frequencies. Doppler and CW returns near zero beat frequency suffer.
  • Flicker (1/f) noise. Transistor and diode noise rises at low frequency. In a superhet it is far below the IF; in zero-IF it lands on the signal.
  • Even-order distortion (IIP2). A strong pulsed or amplitude-modulated signal anywhere in band is squared by the mixer's second-order non-linearity, giving an envelope product at baseband. Zero-IF receivers specify IIP2 (often +50 dBm or more) for this reason; superhets filter it out.
  • LO in band. The LO sits at the RF, inside the preselector and the antenna band, so the leakage radiates freely (Step 4).

Low-IF. The LO is offset by a small IF (one or two channel widths, here 20 MHz). The signal lands at +20 MHz, clear of DC and most flicker noise, so a high-pass filter removes the DC offset without touching the signal. The price: the image is now an adjacent channel only 40 MHz away, which no filter can reject, so IQ balance alone sets the image rejection, and a strong neighbour leaks onto the wanted channel at −IRR.

What goes wrong with the simplest approach

A single conversion straight to a low IF. A 10 GHz receiver converting to a 20 MHz IF with only a filter for protection has its image at 9.96 GHz, inside any practical preselector: 0 dB of rejection. A −40 dBm emitter there appears at −40 dBm on top of the −80 dBm wanted signal.

Zero-IF with an ordinary mixer. With 30 dB of LO-RF isolation and a +7 dBm LO, the DC offset is −23 dBm input-referred: 67 dB above a −90 dBm sensitivity, enough to saturate the baseband chain.

Check yourself

Why does low-IF remove the DC offset problem but not the image problem?

The signal sits at +20 MHz, so a high-pass filter can remove DC without touching it. But the image is only 40 MHz away, far too close for an RF filter, so only IQ balance rejects it.

In the double-conversion plan, why can the preselector not reject the second image?

The second image enters at the RF as fRF + 2 × 140 MHz = 10.28 GHz, inside the 1 GHz preselector. It is only separated from the wanted signal after the first conversion, by the 3 GHz IF filter.

Step 10

The FMCW dechirp mixer: turning range into frequency

You already know

A chirp is a tone whose frequency rises linearly with time. Mixing two tones gives their difference frequency (Step 1). Radar range is R = c·τ/2, where τ is the round-trip delay.

A frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) radar transmits a chirp of bandwidth B over a time T, continuously. The echo from a target at range R is the same chirp, delayed by τ = 2R/c. Now use the transmitted chirp itself as the LO of the receive mixer. At every instant the two chirps differ in frequency by the slope times the delay, S·τ, where S = B/T. So the mixer output is a steady tone, the beat frequency, proportional to range. An FFT of the beat signal is a range profile. This "dechirp" or "stretch" processing replaces a GHz-wide receiver with a mixer and an ADC that only needs to cover the beat frequencies, often a few MHz.

What the mixer specs now mean.

  • IF bandwidth and ADC rate set maximum range: Rmax = fbeat,max · c / (2S).
  • Range resolution is c/(2B), set by the chirp bandwidth, not by the IF bandwidth.
  • TX-RX leakage (circulator or antenna coupling, typically only 20 to 30 dB below the transmitter) is a very strong "target" at nearly zero range: a near-DC beat that the mixer's linearity must handle and the IF high-pass must suppress. It is the FMCW form of the DC offset problem.
  • LO phase noise is partly cancelled for short ranges, because the echo and the LO carry the same phase noise, delayed by τ (the range-correlation effect). Leakage from long delays is not cancelled. See the Synthesiser and phase noise module.
  • Chirp linearity: any non-linearity in the chirp makes the beat frequency wander, which smears the range peak. This is a synthesiser spec seen through the mixer.
S = B/T    fbeat = S · 2R/c    ΔR = c/(2B)    Rmax = (fs/2) · c / (2S)fs is the ADC sample rate of a real (not IQ) beat signal, so the beat must stay below fs/2. Example: B = 150 MHz, T = 100 µs, R = 1 km: S = 1.5 MHz/µs, τ = 6.67 µs, fbeat = 10.0 MHz, ΔR = 1.0 m; with fs = 20 MS/s, Rmax = 1 km.

What goes wrong with the simplest approach

Receive the chirp like a pulsed radar. Digitising the whole 150 MHz chirp needs at least 300 MS/s. Dechirping first turns a target at 800 m into an 8.0 MHz tone that a 20 MS/s ADC handles.

Pick the ADC without checking range. With 5 MS/s and the same chirp, the maximum range is only 250 m: targets beyond it alias or are lost.

  • Chirp bandwidth B from the range resolution: ΔR = c/(2B).

Why is FMCW TX-RX leakage harder than pulsed-radar leakage?

A pulsed radar's receiver is switched off (or protected) while it transmits. An FMCW radar transmits and receives at the same time, so the leakage is always present at the mixer, and the mixer must stay linear with it.

Glossary of terms introduced on this page

Balun
A transformer or network that converts a single-ended (unbalanced) signal into a balanced pair, equal and opposite. Its balance sets mixer isolation and even-order spur suppression.
Baseband
A band starting at or near 0 Hz.
Beat frequency
The difference frequency at a mixer output; in FMCW, proportional to target range.
Co-site interference
Interference between equipment on the same platform, such as one receiver's LO leakage seen by another.
Commutating mixer
A mixer in which the LO switches the signal path, multiplying the RF by a square-like wave.
Conduction angle
The fraction of the LO cycle during which a diode pair or FET switch conducts.
Conversion loss / gain
RF input power minus wanted IF output power (loss), or its negative (gain), in dB.
DC offset
A DC voltage at a zero-IF mixer output caused by LO self-mixing or strong-signal self-mixing; it lies in the middle of the wanted channel.
Dechirp (stretch) processing
Mixing a received chirp with a replica of the transmitted chirp so that delay becomes a beat frequency.
Double-balanced mixer
A mixer balanced at both RF and LO ports (typically a four-diode ring with two baluns), giving port isolation and suppression of even-order products.
Double conversion
A receiver with two mixers: a high first IF for easy image rejection, then a low second IF for channel filtering.
DSB noise figure
Mixer noise figure measured with the signal in both the wanted and image bands; about 3 dB lower than SSB.
Flicker (1/f) noise
Device noise whose power rises as frequency falls; a problem when the signal is converted to near 0 Hz.
FMCW
Frequency-modulated continuous wave: a radar that transmits a continuous chirp and receives at the same time.
Frequency plan
The choice of IF(s) and LO frequencies for a receiver or transmitter.
Gilbert cell
An active, transistor-based double-balanced mixer with conversion gain and low LO drive.
Half-IF spur
The (2,2) spurious response at fLO ∓ fIF/2, only half an IF from the wanted signal.
Hartley image-reject mixer
An IQ mixer followed by an analogue 90° IF hybrid that cancels the image.
High-side / low-side LO
LO above or below the wanted RF.
IF (intermediate frequency)
The fixed frequency a superheterodyne receiver converts to, where filtering, gain and digitising are done.
IF feedthrough
The (1,0) response: an input at the IF frequency passing straight through the mixer, limited by RF-IF isolation.
IIP2
Input second-order intercept point: the input power at which the extrapolated second-order product equals the fundamental. Critical in zero-IF receivers.
IIP3
Input third-order intercept point; see the LNA module. For a diode mixer, roughly PLO + 8 to 10 dB.
Image frequency
The other input frequency that converts to the same IF, at 2fLO − fRF.
Image rejection ratio (IRR)
Wanted output power divided by image output power, in dB, for equal inputs.
IQ (quadrature) mixer
Two mixers driven by LOs 90° apart, giving a complex output that separates positive from negative frequencies.
Isolation (LO-RF, LO-IF, RF-IF)
Leakage between mixer ports, in dB below the driving signal.
LO (local oscillator)
The strong reference tone that drives the mixer.
LO drive level
The LO power a mixer is designed for ("level 7" = +7 dBm).
LO leakage
LO power escaping through a mixer port, and possibly out of the antenna.
Low-IF
A receiver that converts to a small IF of one or two channel widths, using an IQ mixer for image rejection.
P1dB
The input power at which gain has fallen 1 dB below its small-signal value.
Preselector
The band-pass filter at the receiver input that removes images and other out-of-band signals.
Range resolution
The smallest range separation at which two targets are resolved: c/(2B).
Reciprocal mixing
The transfer of LO phase noise onto a strong nearby signal, raising the noise around it (synthesiser module).
Self-mixing
A signal (LO or strong RF) mixing with itself through leakage, producing DC.
SFDR
Spur-free dynamic range: from the noise floor to the input at which third-order products reach the noise, (2/3)(IIP3 − N).
Spur chart / spur table
A chart of output frequencies |m fin ± n fLO| against input frequency, and the datasheet table of their levels in dBc.
Spurious response
An input frequency other than the tuned one that produces an output in the IF passband.
SSB noise figure
Mixer noise figure with the signal in one band and noise from both: the number to use for radar and ESM.
Superheterodyne
A receiver that converts to a fixed IF with a tunable LO.
Upconverter
A mixer used to move a signal up in frequency, in a transmitter.
Zero-IF (direct conversion)
A receiver whose LO equals the RF, converting straight to baseband with an IQ mixer.
