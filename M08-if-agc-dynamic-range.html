IF chain, AGC, STC and dynamic range: keeping weak and strong signals alive from antenna to ADC

A receiver has to hear a target echo a few dB above thermal noise and, in the same second, survive a clutter return or a nearby emitter 80 or 90 dB stronger. No single amplifier, mixer or converter has that much range. The IF chain is where the receiver designer shares the job out: how much gain goes in front of the mixer and how much after it, where the gain is turned down automatically (AGC), where it is turned down on a schedule (STC), and where the signal is compressed on purpose (log amplifiers and detectors). This page builds those choices from the definition of dynamic range, through the cascaded noise and linearity line-up, to AGC, STC, log detection, video bandwidth, saturation and recovery, and ends with what they do to radar detection near clutter, to ESM receivers with strong and weak emitters together, and to pulse measurement accuracy. Every term is defined before it is used, every step has worked numbers, and every datasheet line is traced from your design choice to the system effect.

Step 0

The problem: a 90 dB spread of signals and a 66 dB window

You already know

Gain in dB adds along a chain; a passive part with loss L dB has noise figure L dB; the LNA sets most of the receiver noise figure (LNA and receiver noise); a mixer converts RF to an intermediate frequency (Mixers and frequency conversion); filters set which frequencies reach each stage (Filters and preselection).

Definitions

IF (intermediate frequency): the fixed frequency the mixer converts the received signal to, for example 160 MHz, so that most gain and filtering can be done at one frequency whatever the RF tuning.

IF chain: everything between the mixer output and the digitiser or detector: IF amplifiers, IF filters, variable attenuators or variable-gain amplifiers (VGAs), and the gain control circuits.

Gain distribution (gain line-up): how the total gain from antenna to ADC is split between the stages, stage by stage, in dB.

ADC full scale (FS): the largest input an analogue-to-digital converter can digitise without clipping, given in dBm for a sine wave. Levels relative to it are written dBFS. The ADC module (module 07, ADC, DAC and sampling) covers converters in detail; here the ADC is just the last stage with a ceiling and a noise floor.

Video: the baseband signal after a detector has removed the carrier, for example the pulse envelope. A video amplifier amplifies it.

Why the receiver needs a gain plan at all: the antenna delivers signals from about −110 dBm to about −10 dBm, but the ADC at the end needs its input near its own full scale (+4 dBm in this page's example) to be heard above its own noise. So the chain must add roughly 30 to 40 dB of gain for weak signals, and yet must not push strong ones past any stage's limit. What goes wrong with the simplest approach

The simplest receiver is one fixed gain from antenna to ADC. Take the line-up in the diagram: 31.5 dB total gain, ADC full scale +4 dBm, so anything stronger than 4 − 31.5 = −27.5 dBm at the antenna clips the ADC. At the bottom, the ADC's own noise referred to the antenna is about −111 dBm in 10 MHz, 10 dB under thermal noise, so the window is about −101 to −27.5 dBm: 73.5 dB of single-signal range. The table above spans −110 to −10 dBm: 100 dB. Add 10 dB more gain to reach the weak end and you lose 10 dB at the strong end; there is no fixed setting that covers both. The rest of this page is the set of tools for this: share gain correctly (Steps 2 to 4), change it with signal level (AGC, Step 5) or with time after the pulse (STC, Step 6), compress the signal on purpose (log amplifiers, Step 7), and survive the moments when nothing is enough (saturation and recovery, Step 8).

Where your component shows up

  • Every stage designer: your stage's gain, noise figure, OIP3 and P1dB are four numbers in a spreadsheet the system engineer keeps. Step 2 shows how they combine.
  • IF amplifier and VGA designers: your gain control range, its speed and its linearity at minimum gain decide what AGC and STC can do (Steps 5 and 6).
  • Detector and log amp designers: your log range, slope, conformance and video bandwidth decide how accurately a pulse is measured (Step 7).
Step 1

What dynamic range means: from the noise floor to compression

You already know

Thermal noise power is kTB: −174 dBm in 1 Hz at 290 K. A stage with noise figure NF adds its own noise so the input-referred noise is −174 + NF + 10·log B. P1dB is the level at which gain has fallen 1 dB below its small-signal value.

Definitions

Noise floor (N): the input-referred noise power in the receiver bandwidth B: N = −174 + NF + 10·log10B dBm. Any signal much below it is invisible.

Minimum detectable signal (MDS): the smallest input the receiver can detect reliably, the noise floor plus the signal-to-noise ratio the detector needs (often 10 to 13 dB for a single look).

Input P1dB (IP1dB): the input level that compresses the gain by 1 dB. From a datasheet's output P1dB: IP1dB = OP1dB − G + 1 dB.

Linear (compression) dynamic range: DR = IP1dB − N, in dB: the span of input levels the stage or chain handles with less than 1 dB of gain error and the signal above the noise.

Why it matters: a weak signal needs to be above the noise; a strong one must not be compressed, because compression changes its amplitude (a pulse amplitude measurement is wrong), flattens its waveform (it creates harmonics and intermodulation, Step 3) and, in a coherent radar, changes its phase, which spoils Doppler processing. Dynamic range is the distance between those two limits. Worked example: NF 3 dB, B 10 MHz, gain 40 dB, OP1dB +20 dBm. Noise floor N = −174 + 3 + 70 = −101 dBm. IP1dB = 20 − 40 + 1 = −19 dBm. DR = −19 − (−101) = 82 dB.

N = −174 + NF + 10·log10B    IP1dB = OP1dB − G + 1    DR = IP1dB − NAll levels in dBm referred to the input. Notice what sets each end: the bottom is set by NF and bandwidth, the top by the output capability (OP1dB) and the gain in front of it. Gain moves the top end down dB for dB.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

Two naive moves. (1) "Add gain to see weaker signals." Raising the gain from 40 to 60 dB with the same output stage leaves the noise floor at −101 dBm, because the signal-to-noise ratio is fixed by the NF, but moves IP1dB from −19 to −39 dBm: DR falls from 82 to 62 dB and the weak signals are no easier to detect. (2) "Open the bandwidth to catch every signal." From 10 MHz to 1 GHz the noise floor rises 20 dB to −81 dBm and DR falls to 62 dB. Both lose 20 dB of range and gain nothing at the weak end.

How to choose the parameters that set dynamic range

(1) Set B to the signal: about 1/τ for a pulse of width τ (Step 7 shows what too little bandwidth does to the pulse). (2) Set NF from the sensitivity requirement, using the LNA module. (3) Set the gain to the least that lifts the receiver noise clearly above the next stage's noise (Step 4 makes this exact: about 10 dB above the ADC noise). (4) Then choose the output stage so that OP1dB − G + 1 reaches the strongest signal that must stay linear. If no OP1dB is enough, the remaining range has to come from AGC, STC or a log amplifier.

Step 2

The cascaded NF and IP3 line-up, stage by stage

You already know

The Friis formula from the LNA module: Ftotal = F1 + (F2 − 1)/G1 + (F3 − 1)/(G1G2) + …, with F and G as linear ratios. The third-order intercept IP3 from the LNA and mixer modules: the level where the extrapolated third-order product equals the wanted signal. OIP3 = IIP3 + G.

Definitions

Cascade: stages in series. A cascaded value (NF, IIP3, P1dB) is the value of the whole chain referred to its input.

Cascaded IIP3: 1/IIP3total = 1/IIP31 + G1/IIP32 + G1G2/IIP33 + …, with IIP3 in milliwatts and G linear. Each stage's IIP3 is divided by the gain in front of it.

Level diagram: a plot of signal, noise and distortion level (dBm) at the output of every stage, with each stage's limit marked. It shows at a glance which stage runs out of headroom first.

Headroom: the distance in dB between the signal level at a stage and that stage's P1dB (or the ADC full scale).

Why both formulas matter together: Friis says a stage's noise counts less the more gain there is in front of it, so you want gain early. The IIP3 formula says a stage's linearity counts more the more gain there is in front of it, so you want gain late. Gain is the same quantity in both, used in opposite directions. That tension is the whole subject of gain distribution (Step 4). Worked example with the chain in Step 0 (all numbers illustrative): limiter and preselector −1.5 dB, LNA 20 dB (NF 1.5 dB, OIP3 +25 dBm), image filter −2 dB, passive mixer −7 dB (NF 7 dB, IIP3 +15 dBm), IF amp 1 15 dB (NF 3 dB, OIP3 +30 dBm), IF filter −3 dB, IF amp 2 10 dB (NF 5 dB, OIP3 +35 dBm), ADC (NF 25 dB, full scale +4 dBm). The chain has 31.5 dB gain, cascaded NF 3.89 dB and cascaded IIP3 −4.5 dBm. The mixer alone contributes half the IIP3 budget, and the LNA 40 % of the noise.

PIM3 is the input-referred third-order product of two equal tones of Pin each. The ADC appears in Friis as a stage with a large NF (here 25 dB, from full scale +4 dBm, 72 dB SNR and 250 MS/s).

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive line-up checks each part against its own datasheet: "the LNA is linear to +5 dBm IIP3, the mixer to +15 dBm, so the chain is fine to about +5 dBm." The cascade says otherwise: with 16.5 dB in front of it, the mixer's +15 dBm IIP3 is worth 15 − 16.5 = −1.5 dBm at the antenna, and the chain as a whole reaches only −4.5 dBm. Likewise "the ADC has 25 dB NF, so it must dominate the noise" is wrong in the other direction: with 31.5 dB in front, it adds only 0.4 dB. You cannot judge any stage without the gain around it.

How to read and use a level diagram

(1) Start at the input with the strongest signal that must stay linear and the noise floor. (2) Add each stage's gain to get the level at its output. (3) At each stage, mark its OP1dB: the gap to the signal line is the headroom; the smallest gap is the stage that will compress first. (4) Track the noise line: it should rise by exactly the gain at each stage (a stage that lifts it by more is adding noise of its own). (5) At the ADC, the noise line should sit about 10 dB above the ADC's own noise, and the signal line should not reach full scale. The demo draws exactly this.

Step 3

SFDR and blocking dynamic range: two more honest measures of range

You already know

Two tones at f1 and f2 through a non-linear stage create third-order products at 2f1 − f2 and 2f2 − f1. These land close to the wanted band, so no filter after the stage can remove them. The product rises 3 dB for every 1 dB rise of the tones (filters module, Step 6).

Definitions

Spurious-free dynamic range (SFDR): the range of input levels from the noise floor up to the level at which the IM3 product of two equal tones reaches the noise floor: SFDR = ⅔·(IIP3 − N). Inside this range, two signals never create a false third signal you can see.

Blocker: a strong unwanted signal, in or near the band, that degrades a weak wanted one without itself being wanted.

Blocking dynamic range (BDR): the ratio of the largest blocker the receiver tolerates, while still receiving a weak wanted signal, to the noise floor. On this page the tolerance limit is 1 dB compression of the wanted signal, so BDR ≈ IP1dB − N. Some datasheets use 3 dB desensitisation or quote it relative to the MDS; always read the definition.

Desensitisation: the rise in noise floor or the loss of gain for a weak signal caused by a blocker.

Why a second and third measure: the linear DR of Step 1 is a single-signal measure. Real receivers see many signals at once, and the first thing that goes wrong with two strong signals is not compression but intermodulation. For a cubic non-linearity IP1dB is about 9.6 dB below IIP3. Worked example: IIP3 −10 dBm, NF 3 dB, B 10 MHz. N = −101 dBm. SFDR = ⅔·(−10 + 101) = 60.7 dB, so the IM3 product reaches the noise when each tone is (2·IIP3 + N)/3 = −40.3 dBm. IP1dB ≈ −19.6 dBm, so BDR ≈ 81.4 dB. The two-signal range is 20.7 dB smaller than the single-signal range.

PIM3 = 3Pin − 2·IIP3    SFDR = ⅔·(IIP3 − N)    Ptop = (2·IIP3 + N)/3    BDR ≈ IP1dB − N ≈ IIP3 − 9.6 − NAll input-referred, dBm and dB. SFDR rises only ⅔ dB per dB of IIP3 and falls ⅔ dB per dB of noise floor, so a decade of bandwidth costs 6.7 dB of SFDR, against 10 dB of linear DR.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive specification gives only P1dB and reads the 81 dB BDR as "the receiver handles anything from −101 to −20 dBm". Two signals of −30 dBm each, 11 dB below compression and comfortably inside that range, produce IM3 = 3 × (−30) − 2 × (−10) = −70 dBm: 31 dB above the noise floor. In a radar this is a false target at a fixed frequency in every range cell; in an ESM receiver it is a third emitter that does not exist. The receiver is "linear" by the P1dB test and useless by the SFDR test.

How to choose the linearity target

(1) Decide which measure the mission needs. A radar looking at one echo at a time in a clean band needs linear DR and phase linearity; a receiver that must see two or more signals at once (ESM, a radar with interferers) needs SFDR. (2) Write the required SFDR as the spread between the strongest pair of simultaneous signals and the weakest signal that must not be masked. (3) Solve for IIP3: IIP3 = N + 1.5 × SFDR. For 70 dB SFDR in 10 MHz with NF 3 dB that is −101 + 105 = +4 dBm at the antenna. (4) Check BDR separately against the strongest single blocker. (5) If the IIP3 is unreachable, narrow the bandwidth at the first point possible, or move gain later in the chain (Step 4).

Step 4

Gain distribution: where to put the gain

You already know

From Step 2: gain early helps noise (Friis), gain late helps linearity (cascaded IIP3). From Step 1: total gain moves the top of the window down dB for dB.

Definitions

RF gain: the gain in front of the mixer (here the LNA gain, since the other RF parts are passive).

IF gain: the gain after the mixer, before the ADC. In this page's line-up it is IF amp 1 (fixed 15 dB) plus IF amp 2, which is adjusted to keep the total fixed. A negative IF amp 2 gain means a pad (attenuator).

Total gain: antenna to ADC. It decides where the receiver's noise sits relative to the ADC's own noise, and so how much the ADC adds to the NF, and where the ADC clips referred to the antenna.

Noise loading of the ADC: the amount, in dB, by which the analogue noise at the ADC input exceeds the ADC's own noise. About 10 dB keeps the ADC's NF penalty near 0.4 dB.

Why there is an optimum: with the total gain fixed, moving 1 dB from IF to RF lowers the noise contribution of everything after the LNA (better NF) but raises the signal at the mixer by 1 dB (worse IIP3, almost dB for dB once the mixer dominates). Worked example with the Step 2 chain, total 31.5 dB, passive mixer: LNA 10 dB gives NF 6.54 dB and IIP3 −0.4 dBm; LNA 20 dB gives NF 3.89 dB and IIP3 −4.5 dBm; LNA 30 dB gives NF 3.50 dB and IIP3 −13.0 dBm. From 20 to 30 dB the NF improves 0.4 dB and the IIP3 falls 8.5 dB: past a point, RF gain buys almost nothing for noise and costs a lot of linearity.

What goes wrong with the naive approach: all the gain in one place

(a) All gain at RF: LNA 40 dB, IF amp 2 a 10 dB pad. NF 3.47 dB (only 0.4 dB better than the balanced line-up), IIP3 −22.8 dBm (18 dB worse), SFDR 51.8 dB, and the mixer, not the ADC, compresses first, at −30.5 dBm. A single −25 dBm interferer now compresses the mixer. (b) All gain at IF: LNA 10 dB, IF amp 2 20 dB. IIP3 is good (−0.4 dBm), but NF is 6.54 dB: 2.7 dB of sensitivity lost, worth 14 % of radar range. (c) Too little total gain (20 dB): the analogue noise now sits 1.6 dB below the ADC's own noise and NF rises to 7.29 dB. (d) Too much total gain (45 dB): the ADC now clips at −41 dBm and IIP3 is −13.4 dBm. A single gain block of 50 to 60 dB has a further practical problem: any feedback from output to input of more than −60 dB makes it oscillate, so large gain is always split and isolated.

How to choose the gain distribution

(1) Fix the NF requirement from sensitivity (here 4 dB). (2) Fix the total gain so the analogue noise sits about 10 dB above the ADC noise (here 31.5 dB gives 10.0 dB). (3) Raise the RF gain only until the NF requirement is just met, then stop: every extra dB is a dB of mixer IIP3 thrown away. With the passive mixer that is LNA 19 dB: NF 3.99 dB, IIP3 −3.8 dBm. (4) Choose the mixer for IIP3 per milliwatt of LO and per dB of NF. (5) Put the most linear amplifier last, where the signal is largest. (6) Do not optimise SFDR alone: SFDR keeps rising as RF gain falls because a deaf receiver has a high IIP3 relative to its noise. Meet the NF first.

Check yourself

Why does SFDR keep rising when the LNA gain is turned down, even though the receiver is getting worse?

SFDR is a ratio of IIP3 to noise. Lowering RF gain raises IIP3 by nearly 1 dB per dB but raises the noise floor less, so the ratio improves while the absolute sensitivity gets worse. Meet the NF first, then maximise IIP3.

Step 5

AGC: loop speed, range and settling, and why radar and ESM often cannot use it

You already know

From Step 0: with a fixed gain the window from noise to ADC full scale is about 56 to 74 dB wide, smaller than the 90 to 100 dB the signals span. Changing the gain moves the window.

Definitions

Automatic gain control (AGC): a feedback loop that measures the output level with a detector, compares it with a reference level, and adjusts a variable attenuator or VGA to hold the output at the reference.

Reference level: the output level the loop aims for, set some dB below ADC full scale to leave headroom for peaks. Here −60 dBm referred to the antenna at maximum gain, 15 dB below the full scale of −45 dBm.

Gain control range: how far the loop can turn the gain down, in dB (here 10 to 70 dB).

Attack time τa: the time constant with which the loop reduces gain when the signal rises. Decay (release) time τd: the time constant with which it restores gain when the signal falls. Usually τd ≫ τa, so the loop does not follow noise.

Settling time: the time after a step for the gain to come within 1 dB of its final value. For a first-order loop working in dB: tsettle = τa·ln(Δ/1 dB), for a step Δ dB above the reference.

Why AGC exists: communications receivers and CW tracking receivers see one signal whose level changes slowly (fading, range). Moving the window with the signal gives them a total range of the fixed window plus the control range: 56 + 50 = 106 dB. How it changes the outcome: the receiver's instantaneous dynamic range (what it handles at one moment) is unchanged; only its total range grows. Worked example: τa = 10 µs, a signal steps from −80 to −30 dBm, 30 dB above the −60 dBm reference. Settling takes 10 × ln 30 = 34 µs, and for the first 7 µs the output is above the −45 dBm full scale: clipped.

dG/dt = −(Pout − Pref)/τ    (τ = τa if Pout > Pref, else τd)    tsettle = τa·ln(Δ/1 dB)G in dB, limited to between −range and 0. This is the first-order "log-domain" loop used in the demo; real loops add detector delay and may be second order, which adds overshoot.

What goes wrong with the naive approach: a slow AGC in a pulsed receiver

A communications-style AGC has τa around 1 to 10 ms. A radar pulse lasts 0.1 to 10 µs and an echo from a target is over before the loop has moved 1 %. Three failures follow. (1) Radar: echoes from one transmitted pulse arrive from every range in turn, with clutter from 1 km 30 to 60 dB above a target at 15 km. One gain for the whole repetition interval must fit the strongest echo, so it buries the weak one. (2) ESM: pulses from many emitters interleave every few microseconds. A fast-attack, slow-decay AGC is grabbed by the strongest emitter and holds the gain down; the weak emitter's pulses fall into the ADC noise. In the demo's ESM scenario with τa 1 µs and τd 1 ms, only 1 of 12 weak pulses survives. (3) Measurement: any gain change during or between pulses changes measured pulse amplitude, which ESM uses to follow an emitter's antenna scan. If the gain is not reported with every pulse, the amplitude data are wrong.

How to choose the AGC parameters, when AGC is right

(1) Use AGC for one slowly varying signal: a tracking channel locked on one target, a data link, a CW signal. (2) Set the range to the expected signal spread minus the instantaneous window (here 100 − 56 = about 45 to 50 dB). (3) Set τa faster than the signal changes you must follow, but at least 10 times longer than the pulse or symbol so the loop does not distort it. (4) Set τd 5 to 20 times τa. (5) Set the reference 10 to 15 dB below full scale, so the overshoot during attack stays below clipping. (6) Report the gain with every measurement, so amplitude can be corrected.

Step 6

STC: turning the gain down on a schedule, because near echoes are R⁴ stronger

You already know

The radar equation: received power from a target of radar cross-section σ at range R is Pr = PtG²λ²σ / ((4π)³R⁴L). An echo arrives at time t = 2R/c after the pulse leaves, so in one pulse repetition interval (PRI) the receiver hears near ranges first and far ranges later.

Definitions

Clutter: echoes from things that are not targets: ground, sea, rain, birds. Surface clutter has cross-section σc = σ⁰·R·θaz·(cτ/2): σ⁰ is the reflectivity per square metre (for example −20 dB for farmland at low grazing angle), θaz the azimuth beamwidth in radians and cτ/2 the range cell depth.

Sensitivity time control (STC): an attenuation that is large just after the transmit pulse and falls with time, following a law A(R) = n·10·log10(R0/R) dB for R < R0 and zero beyond. n = 4 is the "R⁴ law".

STC end range R0: the range at which the attenuation reaches zero and the receiver returns to full sensitivity.

Signal-to-clutter ratio (SCR): target power divided by clutter power in the same range cell. Separating a target from clutter needs Doppler processing (MTI), which needs the receiver to stay linear.

Why STC exists: the same 1 m² target gives −51.0 dBm at 1 km and −91.0 dBm at 10 km (illustrative X-band radar: 20 kW, 32 dB antenna gain, 5 dB losses): 40 dB more at one tenth the range, the R⁴ law. Clutter in a range cell falls more slowly, as R⁻³, because the illuminated patch grows with range: −42.8 dBm at 1 km, −72.8 dBm at 10 km (σ⁰ −20 dB, 2.5° beam, 15 m cells). Both are strongest at short range, exactly when the gain must be highest for the far, weak echoes later in the same PRI. AGC cannot help (Step 5): the level changes by 40 dB in 60 µs. But the change is predictable from the time since the pulse left, so the receiver applies it on a schedule. How it changes the outcome: with an R⁴ law to R0 = 10 km, the 1 m² target reads −91.0 dBm at every range inside 10 km, so a fixed detection threshold becomes a fixed size threshold, and near clutter that would have saturated the receiver (anything inside 1.2 km at full gain) is brought below full scale.

Pr ∝ σ/R⁴    C ∝ σ⁰R/R⁴ = σ⁰/R³    A(R) = n·10·log10(R0/R)    R = c·t/2STC is applied after the LNA in this demo, so it attenuates the receiver's own noise as well; at short range the floor becomes the ADC noise, 10 dB below thermal. STC never changes SCR: target and clutter in the same cell are attenuated equally.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

Without STC, at full gain: clutter inside 1.2 km exceeds the −45 dBm full scale and clips the ADC, so MTI cannot cancel it and any target there is lost; a 30 dBsm ship at 1 km gives −21 dBm, 24 dB into clipping; and a bird of 0.01 m² at 1 km gives −71 dBm, 30 dB above noise, a false track. The naive fix, a fixed 30 dB attenuator, stops the clipping but makes the 1 m² target at 10 km (−91 dBm) disappear 20 dB under the ADC noise.

Step 7

Log amplifiers, DLVA and detectors; video bandwidth and pulse measurement

You already know

A diode detector converts an RF signal to a DC or video voltage. At low levels its output is proportional to input power (square-law region); at higher levels it becomes proportional to voltage (linear region), then saturates.

Definitions

Square-law detector: a diode detector used in its square-law region, output volts ∝ input milliwatts. Its useful range is about 40 dB, from its tangential sensitivity to about −20 dBm.

Tangential sensitivity (TSS): the input pulse level at which the pulse on the video output just sits on top of the noise, about 8 dB video SNR; a common detector figure of merit.

Logarithmic amplifier: a circuit whose output voltage is proportional to the input level in dB: V = slope × (P − Pmin). It compresses 70 to 95 dB of input into about 2 V, instantly, with no loop.

DLVA (detector log video amplifier): a wideband RF detector followed by a video amplifier with log response; works directly at RF, for example 2 to 18 GHz. SDLA (successive-detection log amplifier): a chain of limiting IF amplifiers with a detector at each stage, summed; wider log range and better accuracy, but only at an IF.

Log slope and log conformance: volts per dB (here 25 mV/dB for the DLVA, 20 mV/dB for the SDLA) and the maximum deviation from the ideal straight line, in dB.

Video bandwidth Bv: the bandwidth of everything after the detector. It sets the rise time of the detected pulse: tr (10 to 90 %) ≈ 0.35/Bv.

Pulse descriptor words (PDW): what an ESM receiver measures for every pulse: time of arrival (TOA), pulse width (PW), pulse amplitude (PA), frequency and angle of arrival.

Why log detection: it is the only way to fit 80 dB of simultaneous level spread into a detector and an ADC without any gain change, so a −70 dBm pulse and a −10 dBm pulse a microsecond apart are both measured. The cost: phase is lost (no Doppler, no coherent processing), and when two signals overlap in time the log amplifier reports the stronger one (Step 9). Why video bandwidth matters: the detector output cannot rise faster than tr. Worked example: Bv = 10 MHz gives tr = 35 ns. On a square-law (linear-video) output, PW is measured between the two crossings of half the peak; the rise and fall are mirror images, so the delays cancel and a 200 ns pulse reads 199 ns (the demo samples in 1 ns steps). On a log output, PW is measured 3 dB below the peak; the log of an exponential rise reaches peak − 3 dB late while the fall drops 3 dB almost at once, so the same 200 ns pulse on a DLVA at −50 dBm reads 168 ns, 31 ns short. And TOA jitter: σTOA ≈ tr/√(2·SNRv); at −50 dBm the video SNR is 41 dB and σTOA = 0.22 ns.

Widening Bv shortens tr but lets in more noise: σTOA still improves as 1/√Bv, until Bv reaches half the RF bandwidth.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

(1) A square-law detector alone: about 40 dB of range, so in an ESM receiver either the strong pulses saturate it or the weak ones sit under its TSS. (2) A narrow video bandwidth "to reduce noise": at Bv = 2 MHz, tr = 175 ns and a 200 ns pulse on the DLVA never reaches its full height: it reads PA 1.97 dB low and PW 85 ns, less than half the truth. An ESM library that sorts emitters by PW would put this pulse in the wrong class. (3) Assuming a log amplifier's PW reading is independent of amplitude: on the DLVA the same 200 ns pulse reads 203 ns at −70 dBm, 168 ns at −50 dBm and 158 ns at −30 dBm, because the 3 dB threshold sits further up a taller step.

Step 8

Saturation and recovery: what happens after a signal too strong for everything

You already know

From the limiter and duplexer module (Front-end protection and duplexing): a limiter protects the LNA from damage and takes a recovery time to return to low loss after a high-power pulse.

Definitions

Saturation: a stage driven so hard that its output no longer rises with the input; the signal is clipped.

Overdrive OD: how far, in dB, the input exceeds the stage's linear limit (its P1dB, or full scale for an ADC).

Recovery time: the time after the overdriving signal ends until the stage's gain is back within 1 dB (or a stated amount) of normal. Causes: charge stored in transistors and diodes, bias networks pulled off their operating point, AC-coupling capacitors charged by the large signal (a baseline shift), and AGC or STC circuits that were driven to their limits.

Blind time: the interval after a strong signal in which a weak one cannot be received or is measured wrongly.

Why it matters: every chain eventually meets a signal it cannot hold, such as the radar's own transmit leakage, a ship at short range, or a jammer. What happens after it is as important as what happens during it. In the simple model used here the gain deficit after the overload decays exponentially: D(t) = OD·e−t/τr. Worked example: a stage with linear limit −45 dBm (input-referred) is hit by a −10 dBm pulse: OD = 35 dB. With τr = 300 ns, it is back within 1 dB after 300 × ln 35 = 1.07 µs. A −80 dBm pulse arriving 1 µs after the strong one is read 1.25 dB low; one arriving 0.3 µs after is read 12.9 dB low, below the detection threshold of −88 dBm, and is lost.

OD = Pstrong − Plimit    D(t) = OD·e−t/τr    tblind,1 dB = τr·ln(OD)An illustrative model: real stages often recover more slowly from deeper overdrive than the logarithm suggests, which is why datasheets quote recovery at a stated overdrive.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive specification asks only "survives +20 dBm without damage". A part can meet that and still take microseconds to recover. In a radar with a 1 km minimum range (6.7 µs after the pulse), a front end that takes 3.6 µs to recover from transmit leakage (τr 1 µs, OD 35 dB) is fine; one with τr 2 µs (7.1 µs) is blind to its own minimum range. In an ESM receiver that sees a strong pulse every 25 µs, a 3.6 µs blind time after each removes 14 % of all listening time.

Step 9

System effects: small targets near clutter, strong and weak emitters together, and pulse accuracy

You already know

From Steps 1 to 8: a linear channel has an instantaneous window from its noise floor to its clip level; AGC and STC move the window; a log amplifier widens it but drops phase; overload leaves a blind time.

Definitions

Instantaneous dynamic range (IDR): the window the receiver handles at one moment without any gain change: from the noise floor to the clip level. Total dynamic range: IDR plus any gain control range.

Required IDR: for a strong signal S and a weak signal W present together: (S − W) + the SNR the weak signal needs (13 dB here) + headroom for the strong one (6 dB here).

MTI (moving target indication) and Doppler processing: radar processing that separates a moving target from stationary clutter in the same range cell by phase change from pulse to pulse. It needs a linear, coherent channel: clipping or a log amplifier destroys it.

Capture: a log amplifier or limiter given two overlapping signals responds to the stronger one; the weaker is suppressed.

Why the receiver architecture is decided here: the strong and weak signals that must coexist set a required IDR, and each architecture offers a different window. Worked example, radar: clutter in the target's own range cell at −40 dBm and a 1 m²-class target at −80 dBm, separated only by Doppler. Required IDR = 40 + 13 + 6 = 59 dB, and it must be coherent. A fixed-gain chain with a 12-bit ADC offers 55.6 dB (−100.6 to −45 dBm): the clutter clips. Worked example, ESM: a −30 dBm emitter and a −80 dBm emitter at the same moment on different frequencies: required 50 + 13 + 6 = 69 dB.

Nout(G) combines the attenuated receiver noise with the fixed ADC noise (−111 dBm referred to the antenna at full gain). The 16-bit option assumes 20 dB less ADC noise relative to its full scale, used as 20 dB more headroom at the same gain.

What goes wrong with the naive approach

The naive choice is a fixed-gain linear chain plus a slow AGC, the communications-receiver answer. In the radar example the AGC sets its gain for the strongest echo in the PRI (near clutter about 20 dB above the target cell's clutter, −20 dBm), so it turns the gain down 31 dB and the target's SNR falls to 0 dB: lost. In the ESM example it turns the gain down 21 dB for the −30 dBm emitter and the −80 dBm emitter reaches only 9.7 dB SNR: lost. Fixed gain clips the strong signal in both, and clipping makes harmonics and intermodulation that can hide the weak one.

How to choose the architecture

(1) List the simultaneous signal pairs and compute the required IDR for each. (2) If the strong signal is predictable in time (own clutter, own transmit), use STC or blanking. (3) If phase is needed (Doppler, coherent ESM, direction finding by phase), stay linear: buy IDR with ADC bits, a channeliser, or two parallel ADCs at different gains for signals separated in time. (4) If only envelope parameters are needed and signals rarely overlap, a log amplifier gives the widest window per dollar. (5) Use AGC only for single, slowly varying signals. (6) Then go back to Steps 2 to 4 and set the gain line-up for the chosen window.

Glossary of terms introduced on this page

ADC full scale (FS, dBFS)
The largest sine-wave input an ADC digitises without clipping; levels relative to it are in dBFS.
AGC
Automatic gain control: a loop that adjusts gain to hold the output at a reference level.
Attack time τa
The time constant with which an AGC loop reduces gain when the signal rises.
Blind time
The interval after an overload during which a weak signal is lost or mismeasured.
Blocker
A strong unwanted signal that degrades reception of a weak wanted one.
Blocking dynamic range (BDR)
Largest tolerated blocker relative to the noise floor; on this page IP1dB − N.
Capture
A log amplifier's or limiter's response to the stronger of two overlapping signals, suppressing the weaker.
Cascade
Stages in series; a cascaded figure is that of the whole chain referred to its input.
Clutter
Echoes from ground, sea, rain or birds; surface clutter falls as R⁻³.
Decay time τd
The time constant with which an AGC loop restores gain when the signal falls.
Desensitisation
Rise in noise floor or loss of gain for a weak signal caused by a strong one.
DLVA
Detector log video amplifier: wideband RF detector with logarithmic video output.
Dynamic range (linear)
IP1dB − noise floor, in dB.
Gain distribution
How the total antenna-to-ADC gain is split stage by stage.
Headroom
Distance in dB from the signal level to a stage's P1dB or the ADC full scale.
IF
Intermediate frequency: the fixed frequency the mixer converts to.
IIP3 / OIP3
Input / output third-order intercept point; OIP3 = IIP3 + G.
IM3
Third-order intermodulation product at 2f1 − f2 or 2f2 − f1.
Instantaneous dynamic range (IDR)
The window handled at one moment with no gain change.
Level diagram
Plot of signal, noise and distortion level after each stage, with each stage's limit.
Log amplifier
Circuit whose output voltage is proportional to the input level in dB.
Log conformance
Maximum deviation of a log amplifier from its ideal straight line, in dB.
MDS
Minimum detectable signal: noise floor plus the SNR the detector needs.
MTI
Moving target indication: separating moving targets from stationary clutter by pulse-to-pulse phase.
Noise floor N
Input-referred noise in the bandwidth: −174 + NF + 10·log B dBm.
Noise loading (of the ADC)
How far the analogue noise at the ADC input exceeds the ADC's own noise.
Overdrive OD
How far the input exceeds a stage's linear limit, in dB.
P1dB (IP1dB, OP1dB)
Input or output level at 1 dB gain compression.
PDW (TOA, PW, PA)
Pulse descriptor word: time of arrival, pulse width, pulse amplitude and other per-pulse measurements.
Recovery time
Time after an overload for gain to return within a stated amount of normal.
Reference level
The output level an AGC loop aims for.
Rise time tr
10 to 90 % rise of the detected pulse, about 0.35/Bv.
SCR
Signal-to-clutter ratio in the same range cell.
SDLA
Successive-detection log amplifier: limiting IF stages with a detector at each, summed.
SFDR
Spurious-free dynamic range: ⅔·(IIP3 − N).
Settling time
Time for an AGC loop's gain to come within 1 dB of final after a step: τa·ln(Δ/1 dB).
Square-law detector
Diode detector whose output voltage is proportional to input power.
STC
Sensitivity time control: attenuation scheduled against time since the transmit pulse, n·10·log(R0/R).
TSS
Tangential sensitivity: pulse level just visible above the video noise, about 8 dB video SNR.
VGA
Variable-gain amplifier, the control element of AGC and sometimes STC.
Video, video bandwidth Bv
The detected baseband envelope; the bandwidth of everything after the detector.
