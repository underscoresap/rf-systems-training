The antenna and the beam

In R1 the antenna was just a number, G, that appeared twice in the radar equation. Here we open it up: why a radar needs a narrow beam, what sets the beam's width and sidelobes, how a phased array steers a beam with no moving parts, how a radar sweeps that beam to search the sky, and what the sweeping beam looks like to someone outside listening.

Step 0

Why a radar needs a narrow beam

You already know

An antenna datasheet: a radiation pattern plot, a −3 dB beamwidth, a gain in dBi, and a front-to-back or sidelobe figure. A radar antenna is described with exactly the same terms.

A radar antenna does three jobs at once, and all three want a narrow beam:

  1. Range. Gain appears twice in the radar equation (R1, step 3). A narrow beam means high gain, so a longer detection range for the same transmitter.
  2. Direction. The time delay of the echo gives range, but says nothing about direction. The simplest way to know where the target is: the echo only comes back when the beam points at it. So the antenna's pointing angle at that moment is the target's angle.
  3. Rejection. A narrow beam hears less ground, sea and rain (called clutter), and less interference and jamming from other directions.

The cost: a narrow beam sees only a small piece of the sky at any moment. To search a large area it must be moved, and the time it spends on each piece is limited. That trade between beamwidth, gain and search time runs through this whole page.

Step 1

Beamwidth, aperture size and gain

You already know

Diffraction: the larger an aperture is compared with the wavelength, the narrower its beam. A 1 m dish is a narrow-beam antenna at 10 GHz (33 wavelengths across) but a broad one at 300 MHz (one wavelength across).

Each small piece of the aperture radiates. In the direction straight ahead (broadside) all pieces are the same distance away, so their fields add in phase. Move off-axis and pieces at one edge become closer than pieces at the other edge. When that path difference across the aperture reaches about one wavelength, the contributions cancel: that is the first null. The main beam lies between the two nulls.

Definitions

Aperture: the radiating face of the antenna (dish mouth, array face) with size L.
−3 dB beamwidth θ3: the angle between the two directions where the power has fallen to half of its peak.
Main lobe: the central beam, between the first nulls.
Taper (aperture weighting): feeding the edge of the aperture with less power than the centre. It lowers the sidelobes but widens the beam and loses a little gain.

θ3 ≈ 51·λ/L degrees (uniform) … 65–70·λ/L (typical taper)    G = η·4πA/λ²    G ≈ 26 000 / (θaz° · θel°)A = physical aperture area, η = aperture efficiency (0.5–0.7 for real antennas). The last form links gain directly to the two beamwidths.

Check yourself

The same 1 m dish is used at 20 GHz. What happens to beamwidth and gain?

λ halves, so the beamwidth halves to about 1° and the gain rises 4× (+6 dB) to about 44 dBi. Size in wavelengths is what counts.

A radar has a fan beam: 1.5° in azimuth and 30° in elevation. What is its gain?

26 000 / (1.5 × 30) ≈ 580 = 27.6 dBi. Fan beams trade gain for covering many heights at once (common on 2-D search radars).

Step 1b

How a dish is built, and why two 1 m dishes at 10 GHz can differ by more than 2 dB

You already know

Horn antennas and their E- and H-plane patterns, return loss, and that a smooth metal surface reflects RF like a mirror. Step 1 said gain is G = η·4πA/λ². This step is about η: everything that stops a real dish reaching its ideal gain. Size and frequency fix the ideal; construction decides how much of it you get.

A reflector antenna has two parts. The feed is a small antenna, usually a horn, placed at the focus. It sets the frequency band, the polarisation and how the dish is lit. The reflector is the large curved metal surface. It sets the aperture size. The reflector adds no power. It only reshapes the feed's spherical wave into a flat (plane) wave across the dish mouth.

Why the shape is a parabola. A paraboloid has one special property: every ray that leaves the focus, bounces off the surface and travels on to the mouth of the dish covers exactly the same total distance. So every part of the mouth is lit in phase, which is what Step 1 assumed. Any departure from the ideal shape, or a feed not sitting exactly at the focus, becomes a phase error across the aperture.

The taper comes from the feed itself. In an array, a power-divider network sets the taper (Step 2). In a dish there is no such network. The taper is simply the feed horn's own radiation pattern as seen by the reflector, plus one geometric effect, described below. Choose a different horn and you get a different taper, beamwidth, sidelobe level and gain, at the same frequency and with the same metal.

Definitions

Focal length F: distance from the dish centre (vertex) to the focus.
f/D: focal length divided by diameter. Small f/D (0.25–0.35) is a deep dish with the feed close in; large f/D (0.6–1) is a shallow dish with the feed far out.
Rim half-angle ψ0: the angle from the feed's axis to the dish edge, seen from the focus. The feed must light ±ψ0.
Edge taper: power density at the dish edge compared with the centre, in dB.
Spillover: feed power that misses the reflector and goes past its edge.
Blockage: the feed, its support struts and any subreflector sit in front of the dish and shadow part of the mouth.
Surface error ε: rms deviation of the real surface from the ideal paraboloid.
Phase centre: the point inside a horn that its radiation appears to come from. That point, not the horn mouth, must sit at the focus.

ψ0 = 2·atan(1 / (4·f/D))    depth = D²/(16F)    space taper = cos⁴(ψ0/2)f/D = 0.4 gives ψ0 = 64°. f/D = 0.25 gives 90°: the focus lies exactly in the plane of the rim. The space taper exists because the rim is further from the feed than the vertex is (ρ/F = 1/cos²(ψ/2)), so the same feed power is spread more thinly there. At f/D = 0.4 it is −2.9 dB. At f/D = 0.25 it is −6 dB.

The central trade: spillover against illumination

Picture a horn with a wide beam. It lights the dish almost evenly, which uses the whole area well (high illumination efficiency). But a wide beam also sends a lot of power past the rim (low spillover efficiency). A narrow-beam horn wastes almost nothing past the rim, but it lights the edge of the dish weakly, so the outer ring of metal, which is most of the area, does little work.

Gain depends on the product of the two, and that product peaks when the edge is lit about 10–12 dB below the centre. This is the most-quoted rule in reflector design. The table comes from the demo below: a 1 m dish with f/D = 0.4, ideal surface, no blockage, and the horn's −10 dB beamwidth varied.

Spillover is worse than the lost gain suggests. Power that goes past the rim does not vanish: it forms wide-angle sidelobes and backlobes. On receive, the same path collects thermal noise from the warm ground behind and around the dish, which raises the system noise temperature (R1, step 5). Low-noise receive dishes therefore use a slightly heavier taper than the gain optimum.

The efficiency budget

η = ηspill · ηillum · ηblock · ηsurface · ηother    ηsurface = exp(−(4π·ε/λ)²)ηother collects cross-polarisation loss, feed phase error, ohmic loss and feed mismatch, typically 0.9 (−0.5 dB) in total. The surface term is the Ruze formula.
  • Blockage. The feed, its struts or a subreflector sit in front of the centre of the mouth, which is the brightest part of a tapered aperture. Removing a central disc removes a share of the field, and gain falls as (1 − blocked field share)². A feed 10 % of D across covers only 1 % of the area but costs about 4 % of the gain. Blockage also raises the near-in sidelobes, because subtracting a small disc subtracts a broad pattern from the main one. Struts add thin shadow lines that throw energy into specific planes.
  • Surface accuracy. A bump of height h changes the path by about 2h (in and out again), so the phase error is 4πε/λ. Losses stay small until ε approaches λ/30. The lost energy does not vanish either: it forms a broad, raised floor of sidelobes around the main beam.
  • Feed placement. A phase centre sitting in front of or behind the focus (axial defocus) gives a phase error that grows towards the rim: the gain drops and the nulls fill in. A phase centre offset sideways tilts the beam the other way by about 0.8–0.95 times the offset angle. Done on purpose, this is how conical scan and multi-beam feeds work.
  • Feed mismatch from the dish. The vertex reflects part of the power straight back into the feed. This appears as ripple in the feed's return loss across frequency. A small vertex plate or cone breaks up that reflection.

Where your component shows up

  • Feed horn: the E- and H-plane patterns should match (corrugated and dual-mode horns do this), or the dish is lit unevenly and cross-polarisation rises. The phase centre location must be known and stable across the band.
  • Mechanics: surface rms, thermal distortion and sag under gravity and wind set the highest usable frequency.
  • Struts, subreflector and radome: blockage and scattering raise sidelobes. Every 0.1 dB of radome loss counts twice in a radar (out and back).
  • Feed return loss measured on the dish differs from the bench measurement, because of the vertex reflection.

Check yourself

A dish with 1 mm rms surface works well at 10 GHz. What happens at 35 GHz?

At 10 GHz: 4π × 1/30 = 0.42 rad, so ηsurface = exp(−0.175) = 0.84, or −0.8 dB. At 35 GHz: 4π × 1/8.6 = 1.47 rad, so ηsurface = exp(−2.15) = 0.12, or −9.3 dB, and most of the lost energy lands in a raised sidelobe floor. The dish is still the same size in wavelengths, but now it is too rough for the job.

You swap the horn for one with a narrower beam. List what changes.

The edge taper gets heavier, spillover falls, illumination efficiency falls, the beam widens and the sidelobes drop. Past the optimum the gain falls slightly, while the noise picked up from the ground falls too. Same dish, same frequency, different antenna.

The same horn is moved from an f/D 0.4 dish to an f/D 0.8 dish. Why does the gain drop?

The rim half-angle shrinks from 64° to 35°. The horn's wide beam now goes mostly past the rim, so spillover efficiency falls to about 52 %. A shallow dish needs a narrower, larger horn. That is why f/D and the feed are always designed as a pair.

Step 2

Sidelobes: the energy that goes the wrong way

Outside the main lobe, the pattern does not drop to zero. It has a series of smaller lobes, called sidelobes, and a back lobe. A uniformly fed aperture has its first sidelobe at −13 dB: one twentieth of the peak power, in the wrong direction. In the demo above, switch between tapers and read the "first sidelobe" value.

Why sidelobes matter in a radar:

  • Clutter. Strong ground echoes enter through the sidelobes while the main beam looks at a weak target in the sky.
  • Jamming and interference enter through sidelobes from directions the radar is not looking at. Sidelobe level directly sets how much a jammer off to the side can disturb the radar (Part 6).
  • False directions. A very strong target in a sidelobe can be reported at the angle the main beam happens to point at.
  • Being heard. An outside receiver hears the radar through its sidelobes even when the main beam is pointed elsewhere (step 8).

Typical figures: simple antennas −13 to −20 dB, good search radars −25 to −30 dB, "low-sidelobe" military arrays −35 to −45 dB.

Where your component shows up

  • Feed network and power dividers create the taper. Amplitude and phase errors between their outputs fill in the nulls and raise the sidelobes. A random phase error of a few degrees rms across many elements limits sidelobes to roughly −35 to −40 dB.
  • Radome: uneven thickness and joints add phase errors and reflections, which raise sidelobes and bend the beam (boresight error).
Step 3

Getting angle from a beam: resolution and accuracy

Two different questions, often confused:

  • Angular resolution: can two targets at the same range, close together in angle, be seen as two? Only if they are separated by more than about one beamwidth. At 20 km, our 2° beam cannot separate two aircraft flying 500 m apart side by side.
  • Angular accuracy: for a single target, how precisely is its angle measured? Much better than a beamwidth, because the radar compares echo strengths at nearby beam positions or between two antenna halves (monopulse, R6). Accuracy improves with SNR.
σθ ≈ θ3 / ( k · √(2·SNR) )    (k ≈ 1.6 for monopulse)Worked: θ3 = 2°, SNR = 20 dB (100): σθ ≈ 2 / (1.6 × 14.1) ≈ 0.09°, more than 20 times finer than the beamwidth.

Check yourself

Why does accuracy improve with SNR but resolution does not?

Accuracy is about locating the centre of one known bump in the presence of noise, so less noise gives a better estimate. Resolution is about two bumps merging into one, which depends on the bump width (the beamwidth), not on noise.

Step 4

Moving the beam mechanically

The classic radar antenna sits on a motorised pedestal and rotates continuously (360°), or nods up and down, or swings back and forth over a sector. The RF has to get from the fixed transmitter to the moving antenna through a rotary joint.

The beam sweeps past a target, and the target is lit only during the short time the main beam covers it. That time is the dwell time (or time on target), and the number of pulses sent during it is the hits per scan. These pulses are the ones available for integration (R1, step 7).

Where your component shows up

  • Rotary joint: its VSWR, insertion loss and phase variation with rotation angle ("wow") add directly to the system losses and to phase errors between pulses.
  • Encoder: the angle reported for each target is the shaft encoder reading at that instant, so encoder resolution and latency matter for angle accuracy.
Step 5

Steering electronically: the phased array from scratch

You already know

Two antennas fed with the same signal radiate in phase straight ahead. Put a length of extra cable in front of one, and the signals no longer arrive in phase straight ahead. They arrive in phase in a different direction, where the free-space path difference cancels the cable delay.

Take a row of N small antennas (elements), spaced d apart. Feed them all in phase and they behave like one big aperture with its beam at broadside. Now give each element a slightly larger phase than its neighbour, a constant step Δφ. The direction where the free-space path difference d·sinθ exactly cancels that step is where all elements add in phase again: the beam has moved to θ, with no moving parts. A phase shifter behind each element sets the step, and it can be changed in microseconds.

Δφ = 360° · (d/λ) · sin θ0    θ3(θ0) ≈ θ3(0) / cos θ0    no grating lobe if d/λ < 1 / (1 + |sin θ0|)Worked: at 10 GHz with d = λ/2 = 1.5 cm, steering to 30° needs Δφ = 360 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 90° per element.

Three things to look for in the demo:

  • Scan loss and broadening. Steered off broadside, the array looks shorter from the target's direction (its projected length is L·cosθ). The beam widens as 1/cosθ and the gain falls. Arrays rarely steer beyond ±60°.
  • Grating lobes. If elements are spaced more than λ/2 apart, a second full-size main beam can appear in another direction, because the phase step also lines up there (shifted by a full 360°). Try d = 0.8 λ and steer to 30°.
  • Phase-shifter bits. Real digital phase shifters have steps: a 3-bit device only gives multiples of 45°. The rounding errors raise sidelobes (quantisation lobes) and cost a little gain. Select the cosine taper first (its sidelobes start near −23 dB), steer to about 13°, then step from "Ideal" down to 2 bits and watch the "highest other lobe" readout climb.

Top: the phase each element is given (0–360°, wrapped). Bottom: the resulting array pattern in dB (an element pattern of cos θ is included). The orange marker is where you asked the beam to point.

Two kinds of electronically scanned array

  • PESA (passive): one central transmitter and receiver, with a phase shifter at each element.
  • AESA (active): every element has its own small T/R module: a PA, an LNA, a limiter, switches, a phase shifter and an attenuator. Losses are lower, it keeps working if a few modules fail, and it can form several beams at once.

One limitation: a phase shifter gives a fixed phase, but the steering really needs a fixed time delay. At frequencies away from the design frequency the beam points slightly elsewhere (beam squint, Δθ ≈ (Δf/f)·tanθ₀). Wideband arrays use true-time-delay lines for this reason.

Where your component shows up

  • Phase shifters and digital attenuators: number of bits, rms phase and amplitude error across the band, phase change when the attenuator is switched (and vice versa), switching speed.
  • T/R module: PA efficiency and heat (hundreds of modules in one array), LNA noise figure, limiter protection, channel-to-channel gain and phase matching, and calibration over temperature.
  • Element spacing ≤ λ/2 at the highest frequency, which squeezes the whole T/R channel into a cell about 1.5 cm wide at 10 GHz.

Check yourself

A 16-element array with d = λ/2 has a 6.4° beam at broadside. What is its beamwidth steered to 60°?

6.4 / cos 60° = 12.8°. The beam doubles in width and the gain drops about 3 dB (plus the element pattern loss).

Why must d be no more than λ/2 if the array must steer to ±90°?

The grating-lobe condition is d/λ < 1/(1 + |sin θ₀|). At θ₀ = 90° that is 1/2.

Step 7

The search budget: how long to cover the sky

You already know

From R1: detection needs a certain energy on the target, SNR ∝ Pavg × time on target. From steps 1 and 4: a narrow beam has high gain but covers only a small patch of sky at a time. This step puts the two together. A radar has a fixed amount of time, and it must share that time between every direction it is told to watch.

A search radar must cover a search volume (an azimuth sector by an elevation sector) and come back to each part of it often enough not to lose fast targets. The volume is divided into beam positions, each about one beamwidth by one beamwidth. Each position needs a dwell long enough for the SNR from R1. So:

Definitions

Search volume Ω: the solid angle to be watched (azimuth sector × elevation sector, in steradians when calculating).
Beam position: one pointing direction; the beam spends its dwell there and moves on.
Dwell Td: time spent at one position (number of pulses × PRI, or one FMCW frame).
Frame time Tf (also revisit time or scan time): time to visit every position once. For a rotating radar it is simply the rotation period.
Time budget (timeline): the share of every second given to search, tracking, confirmation and other jobs.

beam positions ≈ (Az sector / θaz) × (El sector / θel)    frame time = positions × dwell per position

Why the frame time matters

Between two visits the radar is blind in that direction. In the example above, a 300 m/s aircraft moves 1 km between looks. That matters for two reasons:

  • Warning time. If a target is first detectable at 20 km and closes at 300 m/s, it arrives in 67 s. With a 3.4 s frame the radar gets about 20 looks. With a 20 s frame it gets only 3, and if each look detects with Pd = 0.5 there is a real chance of missing it for a long time.
  • Tracking. To join detections from frame to frame into a track, the radar predicts where the target will be next. The longer the frame, the larger the region it must search for the next plot, and the more easily two targets get confused or a manoeuvring target is lost.

Several looks also add up: the chance of detecting at least once in n looks is Pc = 1 − (1 − Pd)n. Four looks at Pd = 0.5 give 1 − 0.5⁴ = 94 %. So a radar can accept a modest Pd per look if it looks often enough. That is one more trade between dwell and frame time.

The trade, derived step by step: the search radar equation

Start from the energy form of the radar equation (R1, step 8), with the antenna written as G·Ae instead of G²λ²/4π (the same thing, since Ae = Gλ²/4π):

SNR = Pavg · Td · G · Ae · σ / ((4π)² · R⁴ · kT0·F·L)
  1. Dwell from the frame. The frame time is shared equally by N positions: Td = Tf / N.
  2. Positions from the beam. Each position covers θaz·θel (radians), so N = Ω / (θaz·θel).
  3. Gain from the beam. For an ideal antenna, G ≈ 4π / (θaz·θel): the whole sphere (4π sr) divided by the patch the beam covers.
  4. Multiply. Td·G = (Tf·θazθel/Ω) · (4π/θazθel) = 4π·Tf/Ω. The beamwidth cancels out.
SNR = Pavg · Ae · Tf · σ / (4π · Ω · R⁴ · kT0·F·L)The search radar equation. Only average power × receive area (the power-aperture product), the frame time and the volume remain. The beam's gain and its narrowness do not appear.

In words: a narrow beam puts more energy per unit time onto a target, but it must visit more positions, so it spends less time on each one. The two effects cancel exactly. Halve the beamwidth in both planes and you get +6 dB of gain on transmit and +6 dB on receive, but four times as many positions. If you keep the frame time, the dwell per position is cut by four (−6 dB). The net is +6 dB, which is exactly the 4× larger area. For a search radar, what really matters is average power × antenna area (the "power-aperture product"), not the beamwidth itself. The beamwidth still matters for angle accuracy, resolution and clutter, but not for search range.

The fourth-root law from R1 is harsh here as well. A 16-fold improvement in hardware is needed to double the range, which is why radar designers look so hard for small losses (L) and for clever ways to spend time.

Spending the time cleverly

  • Not every direction needs the same range. Aircraft fly below some ceiling, say 15 km. At 5° elevation a target at that height can be 172 km away. At 30° it can be at most 15/sin 30° = 30 km away. Since the energy needed scales as R⁴, the 30° beams need only (30/172)⁴ ≈ 1/1000 of the dwell of the 5° beams. Search radars spend most of their time near the horizon. This is also why fan-beam radars shape their elevation pattern as cosecant² (step 1b).
  • Beam spacing loss. Positions one beamwidth apart leave targets that sit halfway between two beams, where each beam is 3 dB down. Averaged over all target positions this costs roughly 2–3 dB for spacing in both planes. Closer spacing reduces the loss but adds positions, so the frame grows.
  • Confirmation dwells. A phased array can respond to a single, weak detection with an immediate extra dwell in that direction to confirm it. That lets the routine search run with a lower threshold and a shorter dwell (more in R3).
  • Sharing time with tracking. A multifunction phased array must also update its tracks. With 20 tracks, each updated once a second with a 10 ms dwell, tracking takes 0.2 s of every second. Search then gets 80 % of the time, and the 3.4 s frame stretches to 3.4/0.8 = 4.25 s. Each new track slows the search a little: the time budget is a real, finite resource.
  • Mechanical radars have no choice. For a rotating antenna the frame time is the rotation period and the dwell is set by the rotation rate and beamwidth (step 4). It cannot spend extra time in one direction. That is the main operational advantage of electronic scanning.

Each circle is one beam position. The orange one is being visited; the radar steps through them in a raster. (The animation is slowed down; the real frame time is in the readout.)

Check yourself

A 2-D surveillance radar uses a fan beam, 1.5° wide in azimuth and 40° tall in elevation, and rotates 360°. How many beam positions does it have, and what is the catch?

360/1.5 = 240 positions, all elevations at once, so it can rotate fast. The catch: low gain (about 26 dBi) and no measurement of target height.

A radar detects a 1 m² target at 50 km while searching 90° × 30° in 3.4 s. The customer now wants 120° × 30° with the same frame time. What happens to the range?

The volume grows by 120/90 = 1.33×, so SNR falls by 1.25 dB and the range falls by 1.33−1/4 = 0.93 to about 46.6 km. To restore 50 km, increase the power-aperture by 1.33× or accept a 4.5 s frame.

An engineer proposes a narrower beam to "get more range out of search". Is that right?

Not if the antenna area and frame time are unchanged: a narrower beam needs a bigger antenna anyway. The search radar equation contains only Pavg·Ae, Tf and Ω. A narrower beam improves angle accuracy, resolution and clutter rejection, not search range.

Per look, Pd = 0.3. How many looks give at least 90 % cumulative detection?

1 − 0.7n ≥ 0.9 → 0.7n ≤ 0.1 → n ≥ 6.5, so 7 looks. With a 3.4 s frame that takes about 24 s.

Step 8

What a scanning radar looks like from outside

Stand at the target with a receiver and look at the radar's signal level over time (the lower trace in the step 6 demo). You see:

  • A main-lobe burst once per scan, lasting one dwell time. For our 2° beam at 10 rpm: a 33 ms burst every 6 s.
  • Sidelobe pulses between the bursts, 20–40 dB weaker. Because of R1's one-way advantage, a sensitive receiver often still hears these.
  • Inside each burst, the individual pulses at the PRF (too fine to see on this time scale).

That envelope is a fingerprint. A circular scan gives regular bursts. A sector scan gives bursts in pairs (there and back). A conical-scan tracker gives a steady signal with a slow ripple, which means "this radar is tracking me". An electronic radar gives irregular bursts. Parts 4 and 5 show how an intercept receiver measures these to recognise a radar type.

Check yourself

A listener measures main-lobe bursts 25 ms long, every 4 s, with pulses at 2 kHz inside. What can it work out?

Scan period 4 s (15 rpm, a circular search radar). Beamwidth = 25 ms × 90 °/s ≈ 2.25°. Hits per scan = 0.025 × 2000 = 50. All without ever seeing the radar.

Glossary of terms introduced on this page

Aperture, L
The radiating face of an antenna and its size. Beamwidth ∝ λ/L.
−3 dB beamwidth θ₃
Angle between the half-power points of the main lobe.
Main lobe / null / sidelobe
The central beam / directions of zero radiation / the smaller lobes outside the main beam.
Taper
Feeding the aperture edges weaker than the centre, to lower sidelobes at the cost of a wider beam and some gain.
Aperture efficiency η
Real gain divided by the ideal gain 4πA/λ² of the physical area.
Feed / reflector
The small antenna (usually a horn) at the focus that lights the dish / the large curved surface that sets the aperture size.
f/D, rim half-angle ψ₀
Focal length over diameter / the angle the feed must cover to reach the dish edge.
Edge taper
Power at the dish edge relative to the centre. About −10 to −12 dB gives the most gain.
Spillover
Feed power that misses the reflector. It wastes gain, makes wide-angle sidelobes and picks up ground noise.
Blockage
The shadow of the feed, struts or subreflector across the centre of the mouth.
Ruze formula
Surface-error loss, η = exp(−(4πε/λ)²), with ε the rms surface error.
Phase centre
The point a horn's radiation appears to come from. It must sit at the focus.
Cassegrain / Gregorian / offset feed
A convex or a concave subreflector folds the feed to the back of the dish / the feed sits outside the beam, so nothing blocks it.
Clutter
Unwanted echoes from ground, sea, rain, birds and so on.
Angular resolution / accuracy
Separating two targets in angle (≈ one beamwidth) / locating one target's angle (much finer, improves with SNR).
Rotary joint
RF connection between the fixed and rotating parts of a mechanically scanned radar.
Dwell time, hits per scan
How long the main beam stays on a target / how many pulses hit it in that time.
Phased array (ESA)
An antenna made of many elements whose relative phases steer the beam electronically.
PESA / AESA
Passive array with a central transmitter / active array with a T/R module at each element.
T/R module
Per-element transmit/receive electronics: PA, LNA, limiter, switches, phase shifter, attenuator.
Grating lobe
An unwanted second main beam that appears when elements are spaced too far apart for the steering angle.
Scan loss
Gain loss and beam broadening when an array is steered away from broadside.
Beam squint
Beam direction changing with frequency in an array steered by phase shifters.
Fan beam / pencil beam
Narrow in one plane and wide in the other / narrow in both.
Search volume, frame time
The angular region a radar must search / the time to visit every beam position once.
Power-aperture product
Average power × antenna area: the figure of merit that sets a search radar's capability.
TWS
Track-while-scan: building tracks from detections made during normal search.
Conical scan / monopulse
Tracking methods that measure a target's offset from the beam axis (monopulse is covered in R6).
