Antenna fundamentals, from the current to the system

You design the LNA, the PA, the filter or the synthesiser that sits behind an antenna. This page opens the antenna itself, from first principles: how a current radiates, what each datasheet parameter means, how wavelength and aperture size set the beam, how phase across the aperture shapes and steers it, and how every one of these numbers ends up as radar range, sidelobe level, noise or ESM sensitivity. Every term is explained before it is used. Every step has worked numbers and a demo you can change.

Step 1

How an antenna radiates, and where its "far field" begins

You already know

A current through an inductor stores energy in a magnetic field; a voltage across a capacitor stores it in an electric field. Each cycle that energy flows in and back out again: this is reactive power. A transmission line carries a wave along it with E and H in phase and power flowing in one direction: this is real power. An antenna is the device that turns the guided wave on a line into a wave that leaves into free space and never comes back.

Radiation comes from changing current. A steady (DC) current makes a steady magnetic field that stays attached to the wire. An alternating current means charges that accelerate back and forth. A changing field cannot update everywhere at once: the change travels outward at the speed of light, c. At a distance of about one wavelength λ = c/f, the field from the start of a cycle is still travelling outward while the source has already reversed. That part of the field has "come loose": it no longer returns to the source. That loose part is the radiated wave.

The current distribution is what radiates. Every short piece of conductor carrying current contributes a small wave. The field at a distant point is the sum of all these small waves, each with its own amplitude (how much current) and phase (when that current peaks, plus how far its wave has to travel). On a half-wave dipole the current is a half sine: largest at the feed, zero at the tips. On a horn or dish we do not track the current on the metal; we track the field across the opening, the aperture. The rule is the same: the far pattern is the sum of contributions across the aperture, each weighted by its amplitude and phase. Steps 5 and 6 are built entirely on this one idea.

Terms

Aperture: the opening through which the antenna radiates (horn mouth, dish face, face of an array). Its largest size is called D.

Aperture distribution (illumination): how the field amplitude and phase vary across the aperture.

Field regions: zones around the antenna where the field behaves differently. The boundaries are conventions, not sharp edges.

Three field regions. Close to a small dipole the field has three parts that fall off as 1/r³, 1/r² and 1/r (r = distance). The 1/r³ and 1/r² parts are stored energy, like the field of the inductor and capacitor: they explain the reactive part of the antenna impedance (step 2). The 1/r part is the radiated wave. For a dipole the 1/r part dominates beyond about λ/2π. For a large aperture we need two boundaries:

Reactive near field: R < 0.62 · √(D³/λ)
Radiating near field (Fresnel region): 0.62·√(D³/λ) < R < 2D²/λ
Far field (Fraunhofer region): R > 2D²/λ (and R ≫ D, R ≫ λ) In the reactive near field stored energy dominates and anything placed there (a radome, a mast, your hand) changes the antenna impedance. In the Fresnel region the energy is radiating, but the shape of the pattern still changes with distance. In the far field the pattern shape no longer depends on distance; only its level falls as 1/R² in power.

Why phase sets the boundary: the π/8 rule

Stand on boresight (straight ahead) at distance R from an aperture of size D. The path from the centre of the aperture is R. The path from the edge, D/2 off-centre, is √(R² + (D/2)²), which is longer by about Δ ≈ (D/2)²/(2R) = D²/(8R). Contributions from the edge therefore arrive late by a phase of 2πΔ/λ = πD²/(4λR). In the true far field (R → ∞) this phase is zero and all contributions add exactly in phase; that is what gives the full gain and the deep nulls of the pattern.

By convention, "far enough" is when the edge phase error has fallen to π/8 (22.5°). Setting πD²/(4λR) = π/8 gives R = 2D²/λ. At that distance the error costs about 0.06 dB of gain on a uniform aperture and very slightly fills the nulls. Halve the distance and the error doubles to 45°; the loss grows about four times and the first sidelobes start to merge into the main lobe.

Where your component shows up

  • Radome, mast, nearby structure: anything inside the reactive near field couples to the stored energy and detunes the antenna. That is why the feed return loss on the bench differs from the return loss once installed.
  • Test engineers: a gain or sidelobe figure measured at less than 2D²/λ is optimistic for sidelobe depth and pessimistic for gain. Ask at what distance (or with which near-field method) the datasheet pattern was measured.
  • EMC and co-site: a nearby antenna in the near field of a large array sees a field that does not follow the far-field formula. Isolation between co-located systems must be measured, not computed from far-field gains.

Check yourself

A 0.6 m array at 16 GHz is to be measured. What is the minimum far-field distance?

λ = 1.875 cm. 2D²/λ = 2 × 0.36 / 0.01875 = 38 m. A 10 m indoor chamber is not enough: use a compact range or near-field scanner.

Why does the far-field distance grow with frequency for the same dish?

The edge path difference D²/8R is fixed by geometry, but it is measured in wavelengths. At a higher frequency the same path difference is more degrees of phase, so you must go further away before it falls to 22.5°.

Step 2

Impedance and matching: VSWR, return loss, Γ and bandwidth

You already know

You match an LNA input or a PA output to 50 Ω and quote S11. An antenna port is exactly the same: it is a one-port load. The only difference is that the "load resistor" is radiation into space, and the stored near-field energy from step 1 adds reactance that changes with frequency.

Terms

Input impedance ZA = RA + jXA: what the antenna port looks like. RA = radiation resistance Rr (power that leaves as radiation) + loss resistance RL (power turned into heat). XA is the stored near-field energy.

Reflection coefficient Γ: the ratio of reflected to incident voltage wave at the port, Γ = (ZA − Z0)/(ZA + Z0), with Z0 usually 50 Ω. Γ is complex; |Γ| is between 0 (perfect match) and 1 (total reflection). Γ is S11.

VSWR: voltage standing wave ratio on the line, the ratio of maximum to minimum voltage along it. VSWR = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|). 1:1 is perfect.

Return loss (RL): how far the reflected power is below the incident power, RL = −20·log₁₀|Γ| dB. A bigger number is better. (S11 in dB is the same number with a minus sign.)

Mismatch loss (ML): the power that never enters the antenna because it is reflected, ML = −10·log₁₀(1 − |Γ|²) dB.

The real problems of a bad match are elsewhere: the reflected power goes back into the PA (heat, instability, load-pull of its output), it forms ripple with other reflections in the chain (a gain ripple across the band, and group-delay ripple that distorts wideband signals), and a receive antenna with a poor match presents the wrong source impedance to the LNA, which raises its noise figure.

Why the match changes with frequency

Near its resonance, most antennas behave like a series (or parallel) RLC circuit: the near-field stored energy gives inductance and capacitance, the radiation resistance is the R. At the resonance frequency f0 the reactances cancel and ZA = R. Away from it XA grows, and how fast it grows is set by the quality factor Q = (energy stored)/(energy radiated per radian of the cycle). For a matched resonator the bandwidth over which VSWR stays below S is

BW (fraction of f₀) ≈ (S − 1) / (Q·√S) e.g. Q = 10, S = 2: BW ≈ 1/(10 × 1.414) = 7.1 % A small antenna (size ≪ λ) stores a lot of energy for the little it radiates, so it has high Q and is narrowband: this is a physical limit (the Chu limit), not a design shortfall. Wideband antennas (horns, spirals, Vivaldis, log-periodics) avoid a single resonance.

Terms: what "bandwidth" means on an antenna datasheet

Impedance bandwidth: the band where VSWR ≤ 2 (RL ≥ 9.5 dB) or RL ≥ 10 dB, whichever the vendor states.

Pattern (gain) bandwidth: the band where gain, beamwidth and sidelobes stay within limits. A dish is matched over the feed's band, but its beamwidth still changes with λ (step 5).

Axial-ratio bandwidth: for circularly polarised antennas, the band where axial ratio ≤ 3 dB (step 7). Often the narrowest of the three.

Instantaneous vs tunable: an antenna may cover a wide band only when retuned, or only with its beam moving (squint, step 7).

Right: the same Γ on a Smith chart (the plane of complex Γ; the outer circle is |Γ| = 1, the dashed circle is VSWR 2). Add cable loss: the measured return loss looks better by twice the cable loss, because the reflection passes through the loss twice. A bad antenna behind a lossy cable can look well matched.

What the feed, the connector and the radome do

The feed (the probe, slot or balun that couples the line to the radiating structure) does the impedance transformation, exactly as your matching network does. A balun is needed when a balanced antenna (a dipole) is fed from an unbalanced coaxial line; without it current flows on the outside of the coax shield, which then radiates too and distorts the pattern. The connector and its launch add a small step discontinuity that sets a floor on return loss, worse at higher frequency. The radome reflects a little power back into the antenna (a dielectric wall is matched only at thicknesses near multiples of half a wavelength in the material) and adds loss; both change with frequency and with scan angle.

Where your component shows up

  • PA designers: antenna VSWR is the PA's load. A 2:1 antenna means the PA sees any phase of a 2:1 load as frequency changes. In an array, the active (scan-dependent) impedance of each element can be much worse than its isolated VSWR.
  • LNA designers: the antenna is the LNA's source impedance. The optimum noise match Γopt is rarely 50 Ω, so a mismatch between antenna and LNA changes the noise figure, not just the gain.
  • Filters and isolators: a filter between antenna and LNA sees the antenna mismatch at its port; its passband ripple is measured into 50 Ω but used into the antenna's impedance.
  • Test: always ask where return loss was measured. Each dB of cable loss makes RL look 2 dB better.

Check yourself

An antenna is specified with RL ≥ 10 dB. What VSWR and mismatch loss is that?

|Γ| = 10^(−10/20) = 0.316, VSWR = 1.316/0.684 = 1.92, mismatch loss = −10·log(1 − 0.1) = 0.46 dB.

You measure 15 dB return loss through 2 dB of cable. What is the antenna's real return loss?

15 − 2 × 2 = 11 dB. The reflected wave lost 2 dB going in and 2 dB coming back.

Step 3

The radiation pattern: how to read one

You already know

A filter response is |S21| in dB against frequency: a passband, a skirt, nulls (transmission zeros), and a stopband floor. An antenna pattern is the same kind of plot, with angle instead of frequency: a main lobe (the passband), nulls, sidelobes (stopband ripple), and a back lobe.

Terms

Radiation pattern: the radiated power density (or field strength) at a fixed large distance, plotted against direction. Almost always normalised so the peak is 0 dB.

Boresight: the direction of the peak of the main lobe. Main lobe: the lobe containing the peak. HPBW (half-power beamwidth, θ₃): the angle between the two −3 dB points of the main lobe.

Nulls: directions where the contributions from the aperture cancel. Sidelobes: the smaller lobes between nulls. The first sidelobe level (SLL) is the highest sidelobe next to the main lobe, in dB below the peak. Back lobe: radiation towards 180°.

Front-to-back ratio (F/B): peak level divided by the level at 180°, in dB.

E-plane and H-plane: the two principal cuts through the pattern. The E-plane contains the boresight direction and the electric field vector; the H-plane contains boresight and the magnetic field vector. For a vertically polarised antenna the E-plane is the vertical cut.

Co-polarisation (co-pol): the polarisation the antenna is meant to radiate. Cross-polarisation (cross-pol, X-pol): the orthogonal polarisation (step 7), unwanted. Cross-pol is usually worst in the 45° plane, between the E- and H-planes, and often has a null on boresight.

Polar and rectangular plots show the same data. A polar plot shows direction intuitively (good for wide-beam antennas). A rectangular plot (level against angle) is better for narrow beams and for reading sidelobe levels precisely. Linear power scale shows that almost all the power is in the main lobe; linear field (√power) is what a voltage probe sees; a dB scale is what you need to see sidelobes at −30 dB, which are invisible on a linear plot (0.1 % of the peak power).

Why E- and H-plane patterns differ. In a rectangular waveguide horn (TE₁₀ mode) the field across the mouth is uniform in the E-plane direction but falls to zero at the side walls in the H-plane direction (a cosine). A uniform distribution gives the narrowest beam but −13.3 dB sidelobes; a cosine gives a 35 % wider beam but −23 dB sidelobes. Step 5 shows why.

A square horn mouth of the chosen size in wavelengths. E-plane (uniform) H-plane (cosine) cross-pol, 45° plane. Switch to a linear scale: the sidelobes disappear from view, which is why patterns are plotted in dB. Cross-pol has a null on boresight and peaks near the −6 dB points of the co-pol beam.

Worked numbers. A square horn with a 4λ mouth: E-plane HPBW ≈ 50.8°/4 = 12.7° with −13.3 dB first sidelobes; H-plane HPBW ≈ 68.8°/4 = 17.2° with −23 dB first sidelobes. The beam is not round. A dish fed by this horn would be lit unevenly in the two planes, which is why dish feeds use corrugated or dual-mode horns with equal E- and H-plane patterns.

Where your component shows up

  • Radar: a target or a jammer in a sidelobe at −30 dB is received 30 dB weaker on receive, and also illuminated 30 dB weaker on transmit: 60 dB two-way. Sidelobe level sets how much clutter and jamming leak in.
  • ESM / ELINT: an intercept receiver usually sees the radar's sidelobes, not its main beam. The radar's sidelobe level sets how far away it can be detected while not pointing at you.
  • Back lobe: sets isolation between back-to-back antennas on a mast, and how much ground noise a dish picks up (step 8).
  • Cross-pol: sets isolation in dual-polarised links (two channels on one frequency), and polarimetric radar accuracy.

Check yourself

A datasheet shows a sidelobe at −20 dB. What fraction of power density is that, relative to the peak?

10^(−20/10) = 1 %. On a linear power plot it is a barely visible bump.

An antenna has F/B = 25 dB and 30 dBi gain. What is the gain towards the back?

30 − 25 = 5 dBi. Still more than an isotropic antenna.

Step 4

Directivity, gain, realised gain, efficiency, effective aperture and EIRP

You already know

An amplifier has gain because it adds energy from the supply. An antenna has no supply: its "gain" is only concentration. It sends the same power into a smaller solid angle, like a lens focusing a lamp. Any loss inside it (copper, dielectric, mismatch) reduces that concentration, exactly like loss in front of an LNA.

Terms

Isotropic radiator: an imaginary antenna radiating equally in all directions. The reference for "dBi".

Solid angle and steradian (sr): the 3-D version of an angle. A full sphere is 4π sr. A pencil beam of θ₃ × θ₃ radians covers about θ₃² sr.

Directivity D: peak power density divided by the power density an isotropic antenna would give if it radiated the same total power. Pattern shape only; losses do not enter. D ≈ 4π / ΩA, where ΩA is the beam solid angle.

Gain G: the same ratio, but referred to the power accepted at the antenna port. G = ηrad·D, where radiation efficiency ηrad = Rr/(Rr + RL) is the fraction of accepted power that is radiated (the rest heats conductors and dielectrics).

Realised gain GR: referred to the power available from the source, so it also includes mismatch: GR = (1 − |Γ|²)·G. This is what a link or radar budget needs.

dBi, dBd: gain over isotropic; gain over a half-wave dipole (dBd = dBi − 2.15).

The aperture: the most gain any aperture can have

A uniformly lit, in-phase aperture of area A has the highest possible directivity, Dmax = 4πA/λ². For a circular aperture A = πD²/4, so Dmax = (πD/λ)². Every real antenna falls short of this, and the shortfall is the aperture efficiency ηap, the product of separate terms that each have a physical cause:

Terms

Effective aperture Ae: the receiving area. A receive antenna in a wave of power density S (W/m²) delivers P = S·Ae to a matched load. For every antenna, large or small: Ae = Gλ²/4π. For an aperture antenna Ae = ηap·A (physical area). Even a tiny dipole has an effective aperture, 0.13λ².

EIRP (effective isotropic radiated power): transmit power times gain, EIRP = Pt·GR, or in dB, EIRP (dBW) = Pt (dBW) + GR (dBi). It is the power an isotropic antenna would need to give the same power density in the direction of the beam.

Worked example. A 1 m dish at 10 GHz (λ = 3 cm): Dmax = (π × 1/0.03)² = 10 966 = 40.4 dBi. With an 11 dB edge taper, ηi·ηs = 0.815 (−0.89 dB). Surface error 0.5 mm rms: ηp = exp(−(4π × 0.5/30)²) = 0.957 (−0.19 dB). Blockage 10 % of diameter: ηb = (1 − 0.1²)² = 0.98 (−0.09 dB). Ohmic loss 0.3 dB. VSWR 1.3 (|Γ| = 0.13): −0.07 dB. Realised gain = 40.4 − 0.89 − 0.19 − 0.09 − 0.30 − 0.07 ≈ 38.9 dBi (ηap ≈ 0.70). Ae = 7690 × 0.03²/4π ≈ 0.55 m², against 0.785 m² physical. With 100 W (20 dBW) at the port, EIRP ≈ 58.9 dBW.

Their product peaks near 11 dB at 0.815: more taper wastes aperture, less taper spills more power past the rim. Raise the frequency with 1 mm surface error: the surface term grows fast.

Where your component shows up

  • PA: EIRP = Pt + GR. Each 0.1 dB of loss between PA and antenna (filter, circulator, cable, radome) is 0.1 dB of PA power you paid for and lost. In a radar it is lost twice (out and back).
  • Feed network (arrays): the corporate feed of a large microstrip array can lose several dB. That loss sits in ηrad and cannot be recovered by any amplifier after it.
  • Receivers: the power you collect is S·Ae. The ESM sensitivity in dBm converts to a field strength in dBW/m² by subtracting 10·log(Ae); a lower-gain antenna at the same frequency means a smaller Ae and less signal.

Check yourself

A wideband ESM horn has 10 dBi gain at 2 GHz and at 18 GHz. What happens to its effective aperture?

Ae = Gλ²/4π. λ shrinks 9×, so Ae shrinks 81× (−19 dB). Same gain, much less collected power at the top of the band. Constant-gain antennas are not constant-aperture antennas.

Why can directivity be measured without knowing any losses, but gain cannot?

Directivity is pure pattern shape: integrate the measured pattern over the sphere. Gain also needs the accepted power, so it must be measured against a reference antenna of known gain (the gain-comparison method).

Step 5

How wavelength and aperture size set the pattern

You already know

A rectangular pulse of length T has a sinc-shaped spectrum with its first zero at 1/T and sidelobes at −13 dB. Windowing the pulse (Hann, Hamming) lowers the spectral sidelobes and widens the main lobe. An antenna aperture is exactly this: the far-field pattern is the Fourier transform of the aperture distribution, with position across the aperture (in wavelengths) taking the place of time and sin θ taking the place of frequency.

The uniform line aperture: sinc

Take an aperture of length L lit uniformly and in phase. In direction θ off boresight, a contribution from position x along the aperture travels a path shorter by x·sin θ than one from the centre (far field: the rays are parallel). Its phase leads by k·x·sin θ, with k = 2π/λ. Adding all contributions from −L/2 to +L/2:

E(θ) ∝ ∫ e^(j·k·x·sinθ) dx = L · sin(u)/u, u = (πL/λ)·sin θ Nulls where u = ±π, ±2π…: sin θ = λ/L, 2λ/L… HPBW = 0.886 λ/L rad = 50.8°·λ/L. First sidelobe at u ≈ 1.43π, level −13.26 dB.

The size in wavelengths, L/λ, is all that matters. Double the frequency and the same aperture gives half the beamwidth. At L = 1 λ the "beam" fills 50°; at 33 λ (1 m at 10 GHz) it is 1.5°.

The uniform circular aperture: the Airy pattern and J₁

A dish is round. Think of the circular aperture as many thin vertical strips side by side. Each strip is a line aperture, but the strips near the edge are short and those in the middle are long (length 2√(a² − x²) for radius a). So a circular aperture is a line aperture with a built-in taper: it has less "length" at the edges. Doing the sum over the disk gives not a sine but a new function, the Bessel function of the first kind of order one, J₁.

What J₁ is, from scratch

Sine and cosine are the functions that come out when you add up waves along a straight line. Bessel functions are the functions that come out when you add up waves around a circle. J₀(x) is the average of cos(x·sin τ) as τ goes once around the circle; J₁(x) is the average of cos(τ − x·sin τ). In symbols, Jn(x) = (1/π)·∫₀^π cos(nτ − x·sin τ) dτ.

Equivalently, J₁ is the series J₁(x) = x/2 − (x/2)³/(1!·2!) + (x/2)⁵/(2!·3!) − … Like a sine it oscillates and passes through zero again and again, but its swings shrink as 1/√x and its zeros are not evenly spaced at first: 3.832, 7.016, 10.173, 13.324… (then about π apart).

Near x = 0, J₁(x) ≈ x/2, so 2J₁(x)/x starts at exactly 1, like sin(x)/x. 2J₁(x)/x is the "circular sinc". Every scientific library has J₁; this page computes it with the integral above.

J₀(x) J₁(x) 2J₁(x)/x, the circular-aperture field pattern. The readout computes J₁ two ways (series and integral) to show they agree. Put x on the first zero (3.832): that is the first null of a dish. Put x near 5.136: that is its first sidelobe, 2J₁(x)/x = −0.132, which is −17.6 dB.

E(θ) ∝ 2·J₁(u)/u, u = (πD/λ)·sin θ (D = dish diameter) First null at u = 3.832, so sin θ = 3.832/π · λ/D = 1.22 λ/D. HPBW = 1.029 λ/D rad = 58.9°·λ/D. First sidelobe −17.6 dB. Compare the line aperture: first null at 1.00 λ/D, −13.3 dB. The disk's built-in edge taper widens the beam and lowers the sidelobes.

Tapered circular apertures

To lower sidelobes further, the feed lights the dish edge weaker than the centre. A common model is the family a(r) = (1 − r²)n, with r from 0 (centre) to 1 (rim), sometimes on a "pedestal" (a constant floor at the edge, since a real feed never goes to zero there). Each step of n swaps one order of Bessel function: n = 1 gives 8J₂(u)/u², n = 2 gives 48J₃(u)/u³.

Where your component shows up

  • Synthesiser / frequency plan: beamwidth scales with λ. A radar that hops from 9 to 10 GHz changes beamwidth by 10 %; a wideband ESM antenna's beam changes by the full ratio of the band.
  • Feed horn and array amplitude weighting: the taper is set by the feed pattern (dish) or by the amplitude of each element (array: attenuators or PA bias in T/R modules). An amplitude error in one T/R module is an error in the taper and raises sidelobes.
  • Radar: HPBW sets angular resolution and how long the beam dwells on a target as it scans. Sidelobes set clutter and jamming rejection.

Check yourself

What dish diameter gives a 1° beam at 35 GHz (uniform-ish, use 65° λ/D)?

λ = 8.57 mm. D = 65 × 0.00857/1 = 0.56 m.

Why does a circular aperture have lower sidelobes than a square one of the same width?

Seen along any cut, the disk has less area near its edges than a square does: a built-in amplitude taper. A taper lowers sidelobes and widens the beam, exactly like a window on a pulse.

Step 6

How phase across the aperture shapes and steers the beam

You already know

A linear phase across frequency is a pure delay: it shifts a pulse in time without changing its shape. A quadratic phase is dispersion: it smears the pulse. Random phase ripple across a band raises the floor of a compressed pulse. Exactly the same three things happen across an aperture, with angle in place of time.

Step 5 assumed every point of the aperture is in phase. Now let the phase vary with position x across the aperture: φ(x). We split φ(x) into three kinds, because each has a distinct effect.

Linear phase: steering

If φ(x) = −k·x·sin θ₀, the contributions add in phase not at boresight but in the direction θ₀, where the path difference exactly cancels the applied phase. The beam steers to θ₀ unchanged in shape (apart from widening by 1/cos θ₀, because the aperture looks shorter from an angle). This is how a phased array steers: each element at position xn gets phase −k·xn·sin θ₀ from its phase shifter. With elements spaced d apart, the step between neighbours is Δφ = 360°·(d/λ)·sin θ₀. For d = λ/2 and θ₀ = 30°: Δφ = 90°.

A phase shifter can only make phase modulo 360°, but at a single frequency 360° is the same as 0°, so this works. It stops working when frequency changes: see beam squint in step 7.

Quadratic phase: defocus

If φ(x) = δ·(2x/L)², the edges lag the centre by δ. This is exactly the error from step 1 (finite distance), and also from a dish feed placed off the focal point along the axis, or a lens of the wrong focal length. The main lobe loses gain and broadens, and the nulls fill in because the contributions no longer cancel perfectly. At δ = 22.5° (π/8) the loss is about 0.06 dB; at 90° about 1 dB; at 180° the main lobe starts to split.

Random phase: Ruze

If φ(x) is random with rms σ (in radians), part of the power stays coherent and forms the beam; the rest is scattered into a wide pedestal of raised sidelobes. The fraction that stays coherent is

Gain loss factor = exp(−σ²) (Ruze equation) for a reflector: σ = 4π·ε/λ (ε = rms surface error; the wave reflects, so path error = 2ε) σ = 20° (0.35 rad): exp(−0.122) = 0.885, −0.53 dB. The lost power does not disappear: it raises the average sidelobe level to roughly σ²/(η·N) relative to the peak, where N is the number of independent error patches. Low-sidelobe arrays therefore need tight phase errors, not just a good taper.

A line aperture sampled every λ/2 (as an array would be). Dashed: the ideal pattern with the same steering and no errors. Solid: with your quadratic and random phase. Add δ = 180°: gain drops and the first nulls fill. Add σ = 30°: sidelobes rise everywhere and the peak drops close to the Ruze prediction. "New random draw" shows another error set with the same rms: the details change, the average loss does not.

Where your component shows up

  • Phase shifters (firmware and RF): a k-bit phase shifter has quantisation error with rms 360°/(2k·√12). 4 bits: 6.5° rms, a gain loss of only 0.06 dB, but the error is correlated across the array and can produce discrete "quantisation lobes" unless the firmware randomises it.
  • T/R modules: phase tracking between modules over temperature is a random phase error. A 10° rms tracking error costs 0.13 dB of gain but sets a floor on achievable sidelobes.
  • Cables and LO distribution: unequal cable lengths or LO phase drift across channels are phase errors across the aperture. In a digital array, the synthesiser phase noise and the clock distribution to each ADC appear the same way.
  • Mechanics: surface error and feed position (defocus) on a dish.

0.5 dB → exp(−σ²) = 0.891 → σ = 0.34 rad. ε = σλ/4π = 0.34 × 8.57/12.57 = 0.23 mm rms.

Step 7

Polarisation, and bandwidth with beam squint

You already know

An I/Q signal is two quadrature components: plot I against Q and a single tone traces a circle; unequal amplitudes trace an ellipse. Polarisation is the same picture made by the two transverse components of the electric field, Ex and Ey, at one point as the wave passes.

Terms

Polarisation: the shape traced by the tip of the E-field vector in the plane across the direction of travel.

Linear: Ex and Ey in phase: the tip moves on a line (vertical, horizontal or slant). Circular: equal amplitudes, 90° apart: a circle. Elliptical: the general case.

Sense (handedness): the direction of rotation. IEEE definition: viewed from behind, looking along the direction of travel, clockwise is right-hand (RHCP), anticlockwise is left-hand (LHCP).

Axial ratio (AR): major axis / minor axis of the ellipse, usually in dB (20·log). 0 dB = perfect circle, ∞ = linear. A "circular" antenna is usually specified as AR ≤ 3 dB.

Tilt angle τ: the angle of the ellipse's major axis.

Polarisation loss factor (PLF): the fraction of the wave's power the antenna accepts because of polarisation alone; 1 = 0 dB is a perfect match.

Bandwidth and beam squint

Step 6 steered a beam with phase shifters: each element gets phase −k·xn·sin θ₀, set at the design frequency f₀. At a different frequency f the same phase steers the beam to where k·x·sin θ = k₀·x·sin θ₀, that is

sin θ(f) = (f₀ / f) · sin θ₀ squint Δθ ≈ −(Δf/f₀) · tan θ₀ (radians) At broadside (θ₀ = 0) there is no squint. At 45° and ±5 % frequency offset the beam moves by ±2.9°. Replacing phase shifters with true time delay (TTD), a delay τn = xn·sin θ₀ / c, makes the phase scale with frequency and removes the squint.

f₀ − B/2 f₀ f₀ + B/2. The vertical dashed line is the intended direction θ₀. With phase shifters, the low and high ends of the band point either side of θ₀; when the squint is comparable to the beamwidth, the band edges miss the target. Switch to true time delay: the three beams line up.

Other frequency effects on the beam. Even without steering, beamwidth scales with λ (step 5), so a fixed dish has a 2:1 beamwidth change over an octave. Series-fed arrays and slotted waveguides feed the elements one after another along a line; the line length between elements is a delay that scales with frequency, so their beam moves with frequency even at "broadside". This is used deliberately in frequency-scanned radars, and is a limit everywhere else.

Where your component shows up

  • ESM receivers: a single linear antenna can lose 20 dB or more on a cross-polarised emitter; a circular antenna loses 3 dB on any linear emitter but never more. Axial ratio over the band (often 4–6 dB at the band edges of a spiral) adds a further variable loss to the sensitivity budget.
  • Wideband waveforms: a radar chirp or a wideband jammer on a phase-steered array has squint: the band edges point in slightly different directions. Instantaneous bandwidth of an array is limited to roughly θ₃/tan θ₀ (fraction) unless TTD is used.
  • Synthesiser: when the array frequency changes, the phase-shifter settings must be recomputed for the new f, or the beam moves. Firmware owns this table.
  • Polariser and hybrid: a circularly polarised antenna is often two linear ports combined by a 90° hybrid. Hybrid amplitude and phase imbalance sets the axial ratio directly.

Check yourself

A 90° hybrid has 0.5 dB amplitude imbalance and 5° phase error. Roughly what axial ratio results?

Write the two outputs as Ex = 1 and Ey = a·e^(jφ) with a = 10^(−0.5/20) = 0.944 and φ = 95°. The ellipticity angle ε (tan ε = minor/major axis) follows from sin 2ε = 2a·sin φ/(1 + a²) = 0.995, so ε = 42.0° and AR = 1/tan ε = 1.11, about 0.9 dB. Both errors must be held small: each 0.5 dB or 5° costs roughly half a dB of axial ratio.

A 64-element array steered to 30° uses phase shifters. What is the squint at +5 % frequency?

Δθ ≈ −0.05 × tan 30° = −0.029 rad = −1.65°. The beamwidth at 30° is about 102°/64/cos 30° ≈ 1.8°, so the beam moves by nearly a full beamwidth.

Step 8

Antenna noise temperature and G/T

You already know

A resistor at temperature T delivers available noise power kTB into a matched load (k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K, B = bandwidth). Noise figure F converts to an equivalent noise temperature Te = 290·(F − 1). A lossy line of loss L at 290 K adds (L − 1)·290 K referred to its input. All of this applies to the antenna too.

Terms

Brightness temperature: everything warmer than absolute zero radiates noise. The ground at about 290 K glows; the clear sky at X band glows at only a few kelvin (oxygen and water vapour, plus 2.7 K from the cosmic background); the Sun glows at thousands of kelvin.

Antenna noise temperature TA: the temperature a resistor would need to deliver the same noise power that the antenna delivers. It is the brightness of the scene, averaged with the antenna pattern as the weighting, plus the noise from the antenna's own ohmic loss.

System noise temperature Tsys: TA plus the receiver's contribution, all referred to the antenna port.

G/T ("G over T"): antenna gain divided by system noise temperature, in dB/K. The single figure of merit for a receiving system: received SNR is proportional to G/T.

T_A = η_rad · [ (1 − s)·T_sky + s·T_ground ] + (1 − η_rad)·T_phys
T_sys = T_A + (L_line − 1)·290 + L_line · T_LNA, T_LNA = 290·(10^(NF/10) − 1)
G/T (dB/K) = G (dBi) − 10·log₁₀(T_sys) s is the fraction of the pattern (spillover, sidelobes, back lobe) that sees the ground. Later receiver stages are neglected here (assume the LNA gain is high).

Worked example (X band, the dish of step 4 at 30° elevation): Tsky ≈ 10 K, 3 % of the pattern on 290 K ground → 8.7 K, 0.3 dB ohmic loss (ηrad = 0.933) → 19.4 K. TA = 0.933 × (9.7 + 8.7) + 19.4 ≈ 37 K. Then 0.5 dB of cable before the LNA adds 35 K, and a 1 dB NF LNA (75 K) seen through that cable adds 84 K. Tsys ≈ 156 K = 21.9 dBK, and G/T = 38.9 − 21.9 = 17.0 dB/K. Notice: 0.3 dB of antenna loss added more noise than the whole sky, and the half dB of cable more than doubled the effect of the LNA.

Top: where Tsys comes from, in kelvin. Bottom: clear-sky brightness against elevation for the chosen band (atmosphere at 275 K, cosmic 2.7 K; illustrative zenith attenuation). For an antenna pointing at the horizon or a ground-based ESM antenna, raise the ground fraction to 50 %: TA approaches 150 K and a 1 dB NF LNA no longer makes a difference you could measure.

Where your component shows up

  • LNA: its NF only matters relative to TA. Looking at cold sky (satcom), 0.3 dB of NF is worth fighting for. Looking at the 290 K ground (most ESM, ground radar sidelobes), TA is already near 290 K and the LNA NF matters much less.
  • Filters, limiters, switches, cables before the LNA: each dB of loss there adds both noise and signal loss, and is multiplied into the LNA's contribution. Put the LNA as close to the antenna as possible.
  • Antenna loss and radome: ohmic loss is a noise source at physical temperature. A radome with 0.5 dB loss adds about 32 K.
  • ESM sensitivity: sensitivity in dBm = −174 dBm/Hz + 10·log(Tsys/290) + 10·log B + required SNR. G/T is how the antenna and front end together set it.

Check yourself

Why can T_A be lower than 290 K even though the antenna itself is at 290 K?

A lossless antenna adds no noise of its own; it only delivers what it sees. Looking at a cold sky, it delivers cold-sky noise. Only the lossy part (1 − ηrad) contributes noise at the physical temperature.

Two systems: A has 40 dBi and T_sys = 400 K, B has 38 dBi and T_sys = 150 K. Which receives better?

A: 40 − 26.0 = 14.0 dB/K. B: 38 − 21.8 = 16.2 dB/K. B is 2.2 dB better despite the smaller antenna.

9.1 Quarter-wave monopole

Construction. A vertical conductor of height h over a ground plane (a vehicle roof, a PCB ground, radials). The ground plane acts as a mirror: the monopole and its image together behave as a dipole of length 2h, but radiate only into the upper half-space. So the input impedance is half the dipole's and the directivity is double: about 36 Ω and 5.15 dBi for h = λ/4 over an infinite ground.

Where your component shows up

  • Matching networks and baluns: a short monopole's efficiency is set by your coil's Q, not the antenna. A missing or poor balun turns the feed cable into part of the antenna.
  • PCB and materials: the patch is a substrate component. εr tolerance, tan δ and copper roughness are on the material datasheet you already read for filters; they set the patch's frequency yield and efficiency in the same way.
  • Wideband receivers and ESM: an LPDA gives constant gain and impedance over a decade, but its moving phase centre adds frequency-dependent delay; time-of-arrival and interferometric systems must calibrate it out.
  • Narrowband transmitters and jammers: a Yagi gives 8–14 dB more EIRP than a dipole for the same PA, but only inside a few percent of bandwidth; outside it the PA sees a poor match and the gain collapses.

Check yourself

A vehicle whip datasheet says 5 dBi. Why might your ESM link budget see only about 0 dBi from a target on the horizon?

5.15 dBi is for an infinite ground plane. On a finite roof the horizon field is about half (−6 dB) and the peak tilts upwards. Use the elevation pattern measured on the platform, at the elevation of the emitters you care about.

You replace a dipole with a 2.2λ-boom Yagi on a one-way link. How much does range change?

Gain 14.4 vs 2.15 dBi: +12.2 dB. Range ∝ √G, so 10^(12.2/20) = 4.1× the range, but only within the Yagi's few-percent band and only in its 34° beam.

Why does a lossy FR-4 patch show more bandwidth than the same patch on PTFE?

Loss lowers the total Q, which widens the VSWR band. The extra "bandwidth" is power turned into heat: lower gain and higher noise temperature. Always check efficiency, not just return loss.

Glossary

Aperture
The opening (or face) through which an antenna radiates. Size D.
Aperture distribution
Amplitude and phase of the field across the aperture; its Fourier transform is the far-field pattern.
Aperture efficiency ηap
Gain divided by the gain of an ideal uniform in-phase aperture of the same area; product of illumination, spillover, phase, blockage and ohmic terms.
Axial ratio (AR)
Major/minor axis of the polarisation ellipse; 0 dB is circular.
Back lobe
Radiation towards 180° from boresight.
Balun
Balanced-to-unbalanced transformer between a coaxial line and a balanced antenna such as a dipole.
Bessel function Jn
Function from adding waves around a circle; 2J₁(u)/u is the pattern of a uniform circular aperture.
Boresight
Direction of the main-lobe peak.
Brightness temperature
The temperature that describes how much noise a scene (sky, ground) radiates.
Co-pol / cross-pol
The intended polarisation / the orthogonal, unwanted one.
Directivity
Peak power density relative to isotropic, for the same radiated power. Pattern shape only.
E-plane / H-plane
Principal pattern cuts containing boresight and the E-field (or H-field) vector.
Edge taper
How much weaker (dB) the illumination is at the aperture edge than at the centre.
Effective aperture Ae
Receiving area: power delivered = power density × Ae; Ae = Gλ²/4π.
EIRP
Effective isotropic radiated power, Pt·G.
Far field (Fraunhofer)
R > 2D²/λ: the pattern shape no longer changes with distance.
F/B ratio
Front-to-back ratio: peak level over level at 180°.
Fresnel region
Radiating near field: energy radiates but the pattern still changes with distance.
G/T
Gain over system noise temperature, dB/K; receiving figure of merit.
Gain
Directivity × radiation efficiency, referred to accepted power.
HPBW (θ₃)
Half-power (−3 dB) beamwidth of the main lobe.
Isotropic
Imaginary antenna radiating equally in all directions; the 0 dBi reference.
Mismatch loss
−10·log(1 − |Γ|²): power reflected at the port.
Null
Direction where aperture contributions cancel.
Phase centre
The point from which the far-field wave appears to originate; where a dish feed must be placed.
PLF
Polarisation loss factor: fraction of power accepted because of polarisation match.
Q (antenna)
Stored energy over radiated energy per radian; high Q means narrow impedance bandwidth.
Radiation efficiency
Rr/(Rr + RL): fraction of accepted power that radiates.
Radiation resistance Rr
The part of the input resistance that represents power radiated.
Reactive near field
R < 0.62√(D³/λ): stored energy dominates; objects here detune the antenna.
Realised gain
Gain including mismatch loss, referred to available power.
Return loss
−20·log|Γ| dB; larger is better.
Ruze equation
Gain loss exp(−σ²) for random aperture phase error σ (rad).
Sidelobe level (SLL)
Level of the highest (usually first) sidelobe relative to the peak.
Spillover
Feed power that misses the reflector.
Squint
Movement of a phase-steered beam with frequency.
Steradian
Unit of solid angle; a sphere is 4π sr.
TA, Tsys
Antenna noise temperature; system noise temperature referred to the antenna port.
True time delay (TTD)
Steering with delays instead of phase shifts; no squint.
VSWR
(1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|): ratio of maximum to minimum voltage of the standing wave.
XPD
Cross-polar discrimination: co-pol over cross-pol level, dB.
Active region (LPDA)
The few elements near λ/2 long that radiate at the present frequency; it moves along the boom with frequency.
Common-mode current
Current on the outside of a coax shield when a balanced antenna is fed without a balun; it makes the cable radiate.
Folded dipole, gamma match
Ways to raise or transform the low driven-element resistance of a Yagi to the feed-line impedance.
Ground plane
The conducting surface that mirrors a monopole or backs a patch; its size sets the elevation pattern and back lobe.
Inset feed
A notch that moves a patch's microstrip feed inside the edge, where the input resistance is lower (∝ cos²(πy₀/L)).
LPDA
Log-periodic dipole array: dipoles scaled by τ, spaced by σ, giving near-constant impedance and gain over a wide band.
Microstrip patch
A resonant metal rectangle on a substrate over ground; size, bandwidth and efficiency are set by εr, h and tan δ.
Parasitic element
An unfed element driven by coupling (Yagi reflector and directors).
τ, σ (LPDA)
Scale factor between successive elements; spacing relative to element length.
Yagi–Uda
A driven dipole with a reflector and directors on a boom; gain set by boom length, narrow band.
Γ (reflection coefficient)
(ZA − Z0)/(ZA + Z0); equal to S11.
General textbook material: Balanis, Antenna Theory: Analysis and Design; Kraus & Marhefka, Antennas for All Applications; Stutzman & Thiele, Antenna Theory and Design. Example numbers are illustrative and not tied to any RFME product.
